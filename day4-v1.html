SECTION 01 — THE CORE PROBLEM

One Action, Four Boxes

A customer clicks Place Order. The business has made a promise: take money, hold stock, confirm the sale. That promise is not one write. It is four steps that often live in four services.

In one application with one database, this is a transaction: all four writes succeed, or none do. When each step is a different service with its own data, there is no single “undo.” Payment lives at a bank. Inventory lives in a warehouse system. Email lives at a vendor. None of them share a database lock.

The example that makes the problem real

PARTIAL SUCCESS

Order ✓ · Payment ✓ · Inventory ✗

WHAT HAPPENEDThe order row exists. The card was charged $89. The warehouse has zero stock — reservation fails.
WHAT THE DB CANNOT DOThere is no rollback that reaches the payment gateway. The money has already left the customer’s account.
THE BUSINESS PROBLEMYou took money for a product you cannot ship. That is not a “technical error.” That is a broken promise: overcharge, angry customer, refund work, support tickets, and if it happens often — lost trust.
⇒ THE HARD PART IS NOT CREATING THE ORDER. IT IS DECIDING WHAT “CORRECT” MEANS WHEN ONLY SOME STEPS WORKED.
SAY THIS IN THE INTERVIEW

“The customer thinks they placed one order. The system ran four independent actions. My job is to define the business outcome for every mix of success and failure — then design the technical flow that produces that outcome.”

SECTION 02 — TWO WORLDS

One Transaction vs Many Services

Same business. Two shapes. Completely different failure stories.

One app + one database

A single lock on the truth

Order, payment record, stock decrement live in one SQL Server. You wrap them in one transaction — a group of writes that all commit or all roll back.

  • If stock update fails, payment row never commits.
  • The customer never sees a charged-but-empty order.
  • Easy. Correct. This is why Day 3 said: keep related data together when you can.

Many services + separate data

No shared lock

Order service owns orders. Payment talks to Stripe. Inventory owns stock. Notification talks to SendGrid. Four owners. Four databases. Four failure clocks.

  • A commit in one place does not undo a commit in another.
  • A timeout does not mean “it failed” — it may have succeeded on the other side.
  • This is why “just wrap it in a transaction” stops working.

Warm-up: money transfer

Same bank, one ledger: debit A, credit B, one transaction. If credit fails, debit never happened. Money is conserved.

Two banks: Bank A has already sent the money. Bank B is down. You cannot roll back Bank A from Bank B’s database. You send a reversal message — a new business action, not an undo of the old one. That reversal is the seed of everything today.

BACK TO THE ORDER

Payment gateway = Bank A. Inventory = Bank B. Once the gateway says “charged,” your SQL ROLLBACK is theatre. The correction is a refund — a second business action with its own failures.

SECTION 03 — FAILURE IS NORMAL

Failure Is Normal

At 5,000 orders a day, “rare” failures happen every afternoon. Design as if each of these will occur this week.

01

Payment succeeds, order update fails

PROBLEMGateway charged the card. Saving “Paid” on the order crashes. Customer is billed; order still says Pending.
IMPACTCharge without a confirmed order. Support chaos. Possible double-charge if the user retries.
SOLUTIONStore a payment intent before calling the gateway. On return, match by that ID. A recovery job completes or refunds unpaid-looking charges. Never call the gateway without a durable local record.
02

Order succeeds, payment fails

PROBLEMOrder row exists. Card declined or gateway error.
IMPACTHarmless if the order stays Unpaid and stock is not reserved. Harmful if you reserved stock or promised a ship date.
SOLUTIONOrder state = AwaitingPayment. No stock hold until money is taken — or hold stock with a short timer and release it. Tell the customer to retry payment. Do not ship.
03

Inventory reservation fails

PROBLEMMoney taken. Warehouse says out of stock (or the service is down).
IMPACTYou sold what you cannot deliver. Refunds, substitutions, or broken promises.
SOLUTIONCompensate: refund (or cancel the capture), mark order Cancelled / Failed, notify the customer with the truth. Optionally offer a wait-list. Do not pretend the order is fine.
04

Notification service is down

PROBLEMOrder, payment, stock all succeeded. Email never left.
IMPACTCustomer is anxious, not unpaid. The business promise is kept; the receipt is late.
SOLUTIONQueue the email. Retry. Never block checkout on email. Never roll back a paid order because SendGrid sneezed. (Day 1 + Day 2 already said this.)
05

Network call times out

PROBLEMYou asked the gateway to charge. No answer in 8 seconds. Did it charge or not?
IMPACTIf you retry blindly, you may charge twice. If you assume failure, you may leave money taken and the order unpaid.
SOLUTIONTimeout ≠ failure. Treat it as unknown. Look up the payment by a unique ID before charging again. Until known, order state = PaymentPending — not Paid, not Failed.
06

A service is slow, not dead

PROBLEMInventory takes 12 seconds. Checkout threads wait. The site feels down even though nothing crashed.
IMPACTThread pool exhaustion. Healthy services starve. One slow dependency takes the shop with it.
SOLUTIONTimeouts on every outbound call. Do not wait forever. Prefer async for slow steps. Circuit-break a sick dependency so you fail fast and shed load.
07

A message is sent twice

PROBLEMThe queue delivered “charge this order” twice (network retry, at-least-once delivery).
IMPACTTwo charges. Two emails. Two stock decrements. The customer did one click.
SOLUTIONIdempotency (Section 8): the same business ID produces one result. Second message is a no-op.
08

The same HTTP request is processed twice

PROBLEMCustomer double-clicks Pay. Browser retries after a blip. Load balancer retries a 502.
IMPACTSame as 07 — duplicate business actions from the front door, not the queue.
SOLUTIONIdempotency key on the request (order id + “pay”). Unique constraint in the payment table. Disable the button. Still assume the click arrives twice.
THE PATTERN

Notification failure is a delay. Payment timeout is an unknown. Inventory failure after payment is a correction (refund). Duplicate messages are a second copy of a success. Each needs a different design. One retry policy does not cover all four.

SECTION 04 — THE TEMPTATION

Distributed Transaction

A distributed transaction is an attempt to make several services behave like one database transaction: all commit together, or all abort together.

It sounds like the clean answer. Architects are careful with it — not because they love complexity, but because the “clean” version is slow, brittle, and often unavailable when you need it most.

WHY IT IS DIFFICULTEvery participant must agree. If inventory is down, payment cannot finish “for real.” You need a coordinator, locks across networks, and a protocol both sides implement. Payment gateways do not join your two-phase commit.
WHY IT BECOMES SLOWEach step waits for the others to vote “yes.” Locks are held while a remote call crosses the internet. Under load, those locks become a queue. Checkout p95 dies.
WHY SERVICES COUPLETo vote in one transaction, services must be up together, speak the same protocol, and share a failure domain. You split the system into services — then glued them back with a distributed lock. You paid twice.
WHY FAILURE GETS HARDERIf the coordinator dies after “prepare” but before “commit,” participants are stuck holding locks. Recovery is a specialist sport. Most teams do not want that sport in checkout.
PLAIN ENGLISH

Trying to make Stripe, SQL Server, and a warehouse API act like one BEGIN TRAN is like asking three companies in three countries to sign the same cheque at the same second — with the internet in between. Prefer small local transactions plus a plan to correct the ones that already happened.

SECTION 05 — AFTER THE PROBLEM

Saga — Split the Action, Plan the Correction

A saga is a long business action split into smaller steps. Each step has a local commit. If a later step fails, previous steps are corrected with a new action — not rolled back as if they never happened.

You only need this word after you accept Section 1–4. The pattern is not the point. The point is: forward steps + correction steps, owned by the business.

How it works

Orchestration

A conductor (often the Order service) calls Payment, then Inventory, then Notification. If Inventory fails, the conductor calls Payment to refund. The sequence lives in one place you can read.

When to use it

Clear, short, owned flow

Checkout with 3–6 steps, one team owning the journey, need to see the whole path in one service. Most order flows start here — and many stay here.

ADVANTAGESDISADVANTAGES
Easy to understand. Easy to debug. Easy to explain in an interview. Compensation is explicit.The conductor becomes a dependency. Adding a step means changing the conductor. If the conductor dies mid-flow, you need recovery (a state machine, not a forgotten HTTP call).

How it works

Choreography

Order publishes “placed.” Payment listens, charges, publishes “paid.” Inventory listens, reserves, publishes “reserved.” If reserve fails, it publishes “reserve-failed”; Payment refunds. Nobody calls anybody directly.

When to use it

Many listeners, loose coupling

When several teams must react to the same fact (paid, shipped) and you do not want Order to know all of them. Heavier to debug. Worth it when the web of listeners is real.

ADVANTAGESDISADVANTAGES
Services stay independent. Easy to add a new listener (fraud check, analytics) without editing Order.The flow is invisible — it lives in many codebases. “What happens after pay?” needs a diagram. Loops and missed events are harder to see. Prefer this when coupling is the bigger pain than understanding.
INTERVIEW ANGLE

Do not start with “I would use a saga.” Start with the failure. Then: “I would split the work into local steps and define the correction for each failure. For a 4-step checkout owned by one team, I would orchestrate it — one state machine I can test. I would not choreograph until many independent consumers need the same events.”

SECTION 06 — THE BUSINESS UNDO

Compensation

Payment succeeded. Inventory failed. Why can’t we ROLLBACK the payment?

Because the payment is not in your database. It is a completed business event at the gateway. Rolling back your Order row does not call the bank. Compensation is the new action that makes the world right again: refund the charge, cancel the order, tell the customer.

ONE SENTENCE

Rollback pretends the first action never happened. Compensation admits it happened and does the opposite on purpose.

Five compensation examples

FORWARD STEPIF A LATER STEP FAILSCOMPENSATION
Charge the cardStock not availableRefund (or void an uncaptured charge)
Reserve inventoryPayment later fails / order cancelledRelease the reservation
Create shipmentCustomer cancels in timeCancel the shipment at the carrier
Grant loyalty pointsOrder refundedClaw back points
Send “order confirmed” emailOrder later cancelledSend a follow-up “order cancelled, money returned” — you cannot unsend, you correct
COMPENSATION CAN FAIL TOO

Refunds time out. Design a retry with the same refund ID (idempotent). Until it succeeds, the order stays in CancelPending — visible to support, not to the warehouse. Compensation is not magic. It is another reliable flow.

SECTION 07 — WHEN TO WAIT

Synchronous vs Asynchronous

Synchronous: Service A calls Service B and waits for the answer. Asynchronous: Service A writes a message and continues; Service B works later.

A calls B and waits

Synchronous

Checkout → Payment API → wait → “Paid” or “Declined.” The customer is still on the page. You can show the real result now.

  • Better when: the user must know before leaving (pay now, login, stock check at the last second).
  • Cost: your request is as slow and as fragile as B. Timeouts and unknown states still happen.

A sends a message

Asynchronous

Checkout saves “PaymentPending,” publishes a message, returns “we’re confirming.” A worker charges the card and updates the order.

  • Better when: the work is slow, spiky, or allowed to finish seconds later (email, reports, some payments).
  • Cost: the customer does not get a final answer in the same click. You need status pages and honest language.
ON THE ORDER FLOW — MIX, DON’T PICK A RELIGION

Payment: often sync if the UI must show Paid/Failed now; or async if you accept “confirming payment” for a few seconds (common with 3-D Secure). Inventory: sync if you must not oversell in that click; async if you reserved earlier. Notification: always async. One flow, three different answers.

SECTION 08 — TWICE IS ONCE

Duplicate Messages and Idempotency

Payment service receives the same “charge order 8812” twice. If you are not careful, the customer is billed twice. That is not a queue bug. At-least-once delivery means duplicates are normal: the sender retries when it is not sure you got it.

Idempotency in one sentence: doing the same request again must not create a second business result. The second charge returns “already paid — here is the original receipt,” not a new $89.

HOW ARCHITECTS PREVENT ITGive every business action a stable ID (order id + “capture”). Persist “I already did this” before or as you call the gateway. Unique constraint on that ID. If the row exists, return the stored result — do not charge again.
WHERE THE ID COMES FROMThe client sends an Idempotency-Key, or you derive it from the order number. The gateway should get the same key too — Stripe and similar APIs support this. Two layers: yours and theirs.

Three real examples

01 · PAYMENT

Double click / double message

WITHOUTTwo charges, two settlement rows. Finance cannot explain it. Customer disputes.
WITHKey = order-8812-capture. First request charges. Second request sees the row and returns Paid. One $89.
02 · INVENTORY

Reserve stock twice for one order

WITHOUTTwo units reserved for one sale. Other customers see “out of stock” wrongly.
WITHReservation unique on OrderId. Second message is a no-op. Stock math stays honest.
03 · EMAIL

“Order confirmed” sent twice

WITHOUTTwo emails. Annoying, not catastrophic — unless it is a password reset or a one-time code.
WITHNotification unique on (OrderId, template). Second send skipped. For money and stock, uniqueness is mandatory; for email it is still good manners.
INTERVIEW ANGLE

“I assume every message and every HTTP call arrives at least twice. Money and stock operations are idempotent by business ID. I do not rely on the queue being exactly-once — because it will not be, on the worst day.”

SECTION 09 — TRY AGAIN, CAREFULLY

Retry and Timeout

A timeout says: “I will not wait forever.” A retry says: “I will ask again.” Both are required. Together they can also stampede a dying payment service.

IDEAWHY IT HELPSWHY IT IS DANGEROUS
RetryTransient blips (1s network dip) succeed on the second try. Customers never see the blip.If the first call did charge, the retry is a duplicate. If the service is overloaded, retries add more load — the patient gets worse.
TimeoutThreads are released. The user is not stuck on a spinner for 60 seconds. You get to decide “unknown” instead of hanging.Too short: you abort healthy slow calls. Too long: you hold resources. There is no universal number — payment might be 8s, inventory 2s, email 3s.
Unlimited retriesNever. Not a benefit.A poison message or a dead gateway is hammered forever. CPU, money, and rate limits all burn.

A sensible payment retry — in plain English

POLICY

Charge order 8812

TIMEOUTWait at most 8 seconds. Then treat the result as unknown — not failed.
BEFORE RETRYLook up the payment by idempotency key. If Paid, stop. If Failed with a hard decline (stolen card), stop. If unknown, retry.
HOW OFTENThree tries: immediately, then after ~2s, then after ~8s (backoff). Then give up and park the order in PaymentPending for a job or for support.
DO NOT RETRYHard business failures: insufficient funds, invalid card. Retrying those is rude and expensive.
⇒ RETRY ONLY WHAT MIGHT BE A BLIP. ALWAYS WITH THE SAME KEY. NEVER UNLIMITED. TIMEOUT ON EVERY CALL.
THE STAMPEDE

Gateway is slow. 2,000 checkouts time out together and retry together. You just tripled the load on a sick system. Space retries (backoff + a little randomness). If error rate is high, stop calling for a short time (fail fast) instead of adding fuel.

SECTION 10 — PUT IT TOGETHER

The Complete Order Flow

Customer → Order → Payment → Inventory → Notification. Click each path. The expected business result is the architecture.

INTERACTIVE — PICK A WORLD

Happy path — written once

  1. Customer submits checkout. Order service writes order AwaitingPayment (local transaction). Idempotency key = order id.
  2. Payment is attempted with that key (sync or via queue). Gateway returns Paid. Order becomes Paid.
  3. Inventory reserves stock for this order id. Order becomes Confirmed.
  4. Notification message is queued. Customer sees Confirmed. Email may arrive seconds later.
STATES BEAT VIBES

Name the states out loud in an interview: AwaitingPayment · Paid · Confirmed · Cancelled · PaymentPending (unknown) · CancelPending (refund in flight). If you cannot name the state, you cannot design the recovery.

SECTION 11 — BEFORE YOU CHOOSE

Questions to Ask Before Selecting an Approach

Ten questions. Ask them before you say “saga” or “queue.” Each one changes the design.

If the customer must leave the page knowing Paid vs Failed, payment stays on the request path (sync, with a timeout). If “we’re confirming” for 15 seconds is acceptable, you can queue it and update a status page. This one question decides sync vs async for the most expensive step.
Email: yes. Invoice PDF: yes. Stock reservation at checkout: often no. Putting a must-be-now step on a queue hides a business requirement behind a technical preference.
“Please try again,” “we charged you and will email,” and “out of stock — refund started” are three different products. If nobody can answer, you will invent a white error page in production.
If no, you must add an idempotency key before you add retries or a queue. Retries without idempotency are how double charges are born.
That is compensation. If the answer is yes (paid but no stock), design the refund path with the same care as the charge path — including its own retries and states.
Money and stock are irreversible-ish. Notification is not. Spend consistency budget on the first two. Let the third be late. Ranking steps prevents treating email like a bank.
A number. “Fast” is not a number. 2 seconds vs 30 seconds vs “we’ll email you” are three architectures. Get the number from the business, not from a blog.
PaymentPending at 2 a.m. needs an owner: a worker, a dashboard, a support playbook. Orchestration makes that owner obvious. Choreography needs extra work to make it obvious. Ask now or staff will grep logs in a panic.
Stripe will not join your distributed transaction. Design around it: local record first, idempotent API, timeout, unknown state. Internal inventory might be stricter. External vs internal changes the tools you can use.
Wrong (double charge, oversell) is usually worse than late (email in 2 minutes). When in doubt, prefer a pending state over a guessed success. That single preference kills a class of outages.
SECTION 12 — TWO SHAPES

Two Diagrams, One Difference

Direct service calls

Order is the conductor. It calls Payment, then Inventory, then Notification. Simple to read. Your latency is the sum of the calls you wait on. Put Notification off this line.

Message-based flow

Difference: the queue absorbs spikes and lets Order respond without waiting on email. It does not remove payment unknown-states or inventory failure — it moves them in time. You still need idempotency, compensation, and visible order states. The queue is a shock absorber, not a conscience.

SECTION 13 — HONEST LIST

Ten Mistakes

Direct. You will have made some of these. The interview is whether you catch them in the design, not in the incident.

01

Treating many services like one database transaction

It feels like the correct, “safe” answer. Gateways and other teams’ APIs do not join your transaction.

Local transactions plus compensation. Name the undo as a first-class step.

02

No failure plan

The demo path works. Production is the other path.

For each arrow, write the customer-visible outcome. If you cannot, the design is unfinished.

03

No timeout

Waiting forever feels polite. It is how thread pools die.

Timeout on every outbound call. Timeout ≠ failed. Timeout = unknown until you check.

04

Unlimited retries

“Eventually it will work.” Eventually you DDoS your own payment provider.

Cap retries. Back off. Stop on hard business errors. Park the rest for humans or a slower job.

05

No duplicate protection

Queues and browsers retry. Hoping they won’t is not a strategy.

Idempotency keys on money and stock. Unique constraints. Assume twice.

06

Making every call synchronous

It is easier to code. Email then becomes a checkout outage.

Wait only for what the click must know. Queue the rest.

07

Ignoring compensation

“Inventory won’t fail.” It will. Then you have money and no product.

Refund/release as designed paths with states and retries — not as a support ticket.

08

Coupling services too tightly

Order knows Payment’s tables, Inventory’s internals, email templates. Every change ripples.

Talk through small contracts: command in, event out. Do not share databases “just this once.”

09

Assuming messages are processed only once

Marketing for queues said “reliable.” Reliable often means at-least-once.

Design consumers to be idempotent. Exactly-once is a wish; once-effect is the requirement.

10

Ignoring business impact

Engineers optimize for “the call returned 200.” The business cares whether money and stock match the email.

Start every design with the customer sentence: charged, shipped, or refunded? If the architecture cannot say, it is not done.

SECTION 15 — SELF-TEST

Ten Interview Questions

Reasoning, not definitions. Answer out loud, then reveal.

Q1Payment succeeds, then inventory fails. What do you do, and what does the customer see?
Compensate: refund or void the charge, mark the order Cancelled (or CancelPending until refund confirms), do not ship. Customer sees “we could not complete the order; you will not be charged / refund started.” Do not leave Paid + no stock.
Q2Why can’t we wrap Order, Payment, and Inventory in one database transaction?
They do not share a database. The gateway is another company. A SQL ROLLBACK does not un-charge a card. Distributed transactions across these parties are slow, often unsupported, and couple availability. Use local commits plus compensation.
Q3The payment call times out. Did it fail?
Unknown. Look up by idempotency key. Until known, order is PaymentPending — not Paid, not Failed. Retry only if the lookup says not charged. Guessing “failed” causes silent charges; guessing “paid” ships unpaid orders.
Q4Should notification failure fail the checkout?
No. The promise (paid + reserved) already held. Queue the email and retry. Failing checkout because email is down turns a delay into lost revenue.
Q5Explain saga without using the word “pattern.”
A long business action is a sequence of small commits. If a later commit fails, we run a planned correction for the earlier ones — refund, release stock — because we cannot pretend they never happened.
Q6Orchestration or choreography for this four-step checkout? Why?
Default: orchestration. One team, short flow, need a visible state machine and clear compensation. Choreography if many independent consumers must react and coupling is the bigger pain. Say the trigger to switch — do not pick choreography for fashion.
Q7A queue delivers “charge this order” twice. How do you not double-charge?
Idempotency key (order id + capture) stored uniquely. Second delivery finds the row and returns the first result. Also send the same key to the gateway. Do not trust “exactly-once” from the broker.
Q8When is synchronous payment the wrong choice?
When the user does not need the final answer in that click (background renewal, invoice later), when 3-D Secure / redirects already break the single request, or when gateway p95 would blow your page budget. Then accept PaymentPending and a status page.
Q9Why can retries make a payment outage worse?
Everyone times out together and retries together — load multiplies on a sick dependency. Unbounded retries never stop. Cap, back off, jitter, stop on hard declines, and shed load when error rate is high.
Q10What is more dangerous: a late email or an unknown payment?
Unknown payment. Late email is anxiety. Unknown payment is double-charge or unpaid shipment. Prefer pending + lookup over guessing. Rank failures by business damage, not by HTTP status.

DAY 4 SUCCESS CRITERIA

  • Explain why multiple services make one business action harder than one database.
  • Identify failure points between Order, Payment, Inventory, Notification.
  • Design a reliable order flow with explicit states.
  • Choose sync vs async per step — not as a religion.
  • Explain compensation as a business undo, not a rollback.
  • Handle duplicate messages with idempotency.
  • Design retry and timeout without stampeding a sick service.
  • Explain saga in plain English after the problem, not before.
  • Talk about customer and money impact during technical failures.
  • Defend the approach and name what you rejected.
FINAL — CARRY THESE FORWARD

Architect Mindset, Day 4

Five rules. About promises that span boxes — not about RabbitMQ brand names.

01

The customer clicked once. The system must still tell one story.

Four services are an implementation detail. Paid, reserved, cancelled, refunded — those are the story. If the architecture cannot narrate the story after a failure, it is not done.

02

Timeout is not failure. It is unknown.

Look up. Then retry or compensate. Guessing is how you double-charge or ship unpaid orders.

03

You cannot roll back another company’s success.

You compensate: refund, release, cancel, email the truth. Design that path as carefully as the happy path.

04

Assume every call and every message happens twice.

Idempotency is not an optimization. It is how money and stock stay honest under retries.

05

Wait only for what the click must know.

Payment maybe. Inventory maybe. Email never. Mixing those three into one synchronous chain is how shops die at lunch.
