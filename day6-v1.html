SECTION 01 — THE PROBLEM

Why One Service Should Not Always Wait for Another

A customer clicks Place Order. The order is saved in 40 ms. Then the request keeps the customer waiting for a payment call, an inventory call, and an email that has nothing to do with the button they pressed.

When everything is synchronous, four things are true at once: latency is the sum of every dependency; availability is the product of them (four services at 99.9% give you 99.6%); throughput is capped by the slowest one; and the notification team's deploy can take your checkout down.

Response time

Return before the work finishes

The API does the one thing the click needs — persist the order — publishes a message and returns. The customer's wait stops being the sum of four services and becomes the cost of one local write.

Reliability

The broker remembers

A durable queue holds work while a consumer is down, restarting or deploying. Notification being unavailable becomes a delay instead of a failed checkout — the difference Day 4 drew between an inconvenience and an outage.

Scalability

Add workers, not rewrites

A burst of 10,000 orders stops being an emergency: it is a backlog with a known drain rate. Add consumers to drain faster. The web tier never feels it (Sections 12–13).

Service independence

Nobody knows who is listening

Order publishes what happened, not who cares. Adding a fraud check or an analytics feed means adding a subscriber — not editing the checkout.

WHAT MESSAGING INTRODUCES — THE BILL

Eventual consistency: the order says Confirmed while the email has not been sent. You must decide what the customer is allowed to see in that gap.
Duplicates: at-least-once delivery means the same message can arrive twice (Section 9).
Ordering: two messages for the same order may be processed out of sequence (Section 10).
Operations: retries, dead-letter queues, backlog dashboards, and a new piece of infrastructure that can itself fail.
Debugging: one business action now spans a producer, a broker and a consumer — you need correlation IDs or you are reading three log files and guessing.
Contracts: a message payload is a public API. Renaming a field breaks a consumer you have never met.

SECTION 02 — THE FOUR WORDS

What Is a Message?

Four words — producer, message, queue, consumer. Learn them with a courier first, because the courier version has no jargon to unlearn.

REAL WORLD

Shipping a parcel

PRODUCERYou. You decide something must be delivered, write the address on the box and hand it over. You do not follow the van.
MESSAGEThe parcel: a labelled, self-contained thing. The label says what it is; the contents are enough for the receiver to act without calling you back.
QUEUEThe depot. A safe shelf that holds parcels in order and does not lose them. Parcels wait there when the driver is on another route.
CONSUMERThe driver. Takes the next parcel, delivers it, and gets a signature — proof it arrived. Only then is the parcel out of the system.
⇒ IF THE DRIVER IS OFF SICK, PARCELS PILE UP AT THE DEPOT. THEY DO NOT DISAPPEAR, AND THE SENDER WAS NEVER BLOCKED.
SOFTWARE

The same four words in your stack

PRODUCERThe Order API. After committing the order to SQL Server it publishes a message — it does not call Payment, Inventory or Email.
MESSAGEA small, serialised fact: { "type":"OrderPlaced", "orderId":8812, "tenantId":88, "total":89.00 }. Carry identity (who, which tenant), intent (what happened), and enough data to act without a callback.
QUEUEA durable named queue on RabbitMQ or Azure Service Bus. It holds messages until a consumer takes responsibility for them.
CONSUMERA background worker. Reads one message, does the work, then acknowledges. Acknowledging is the signature: it means “safe to delete”.
⇒ PRODUCER → QUEUE → CONSUMER. THE QUEUE IS THE ONLY THING THAT MAKES “LATER” SAFE.
WHAT HAPPENS IF THE CONSUMER IS TEMPORARILY UNAVAILABLE?

Nothing is lost. The queue simply grows. Messages wait — durable, in order of arrival for that queue — until the consumer comes back, then processing resumes where it stopped. This single property is the entire reason queues exist: it converts “the notification service is down” from an incident into a backlog with a number on it. The producer never noticed. The customer never noticed.

TWO KINDS OF MESSAGE — NAME THEM OUT LOUD

A command (“send this email”) has one intended receiver and a definite job. An event (“OrderPlaced”) is a fact that has already happened, and anyone may react. Mixing them up is how you get a checkout that must know the email service's queue name. Publish facts; send commands to the service that owns the work.

SECTION 03 — THE COMPARISON

Queue vs Direct API Call

Both are correct. They answer different questions: “do you need the answer now?” and “who is allowed to be down?”

  DIRECT CALL — A → B QUEUE — A → QUEUE → B
Speed Fastest possible path when B is healthy: one network hop, and you get the real answer — success, failure, or the value you asked for. The producer returns almost immediately, but the work completes later. Total end-to-end time is often the same or longer; only the waiting moves off the request path.
Reliability If B is down, slow or deploying, the call fails now and A must decide what to do — retry, fail the user's request, or lose the work. B can be completely unavailable. Messages wait durably and are processed when it returns. A transient outage becomes a backlog, not a loss.
Dependency A needs B online, reachable and fast. Their p95 becomes your p95, and their deploy window becomes your incident window. A only needs the broker. It does not know how many consumers exist, or who they belong to. Adding a listener is a new subscription, not a change to A.
Failure handling Immediate and visible: status code, timeout, your retry policy, and an error the user may see. Failures are synchronous — you learn at once. Deferred and instrumented: retry with backoff, then a dead-letter queue and an alert. Failures are asynchronous — nobody is standing there, so you must build the visibility.
When to use The user needs the answer in this click: payment authorisation, login, stock check at checkout, any read, anything under ~2 seconds that must be confirmed before continuing. The work is slow, spiky, fan-out, or allowed to finish seconds or minutes later: email, notifications, reports, file processing, invoices, indexing, analytics.
QUEUES ARE NOT AUTOMATICALLY BETTER

A queue makes the system more reliable and less coupled, and harder to reason about. You accept eventual consistency, duplicates, ordering questions, a broker to run, and debugging that spans processes. If the user must know the answer before leaving the page, a queue is the wrong tool — you would be hiding a real business requirement behind a technical preference. If the work can wait, a synchronous chain is the wrong tool, because you have made an email service load-bearing for checkout. Decide per step, not per project. (Day 4 said: payment maybe, inventory maybe, email never.)

THE ONE QUESTION

“If this step fails, does the customer need to know right now — or can we tell them later, honestly?” Right now → direct call. Later is acceptable → queue, with a retry and a dead-letter plan attached.

SECTION 04 — SIX WORDS

RabbitMQ, From an Architect's Chair

Six concepts cover every architecture conversation you will have about RabbitMQ. No commands, no configuration.

QUEUEA named, durable shelf that holds messages until a consumer takes them. Declare it durable and publish persistent messages and it survives a broker restart. One queue can have many consumers competing for work.
EXCHANGEThe sorting office. The producer never publishes to a queue directly — it publishes to an exchange, which copies the message to whichever queues have a matching binding. It holds nothing: if no queue matches, the message is dropped unless you said otherwise.
ROUTINGThe addressing rules. Direct matches an exact key (“order.email”). Topic matches patterns (“order.*” catches email, inventory, notification). Fanout broadcasts to everything bound. This is how one OrderPlaced reaches three services without the producer knowing their names.
CONSUMERThe worker that takes a message and does the job. Several consumers on one queue share the work automatically — that is competing consumers, and it is your scaling lever (Section 12).
ACKNOWLEDGEMENTThe delivery signature. The consumer processes the message, then acks; only then does the broker delete it. If the consumer crashes first, the unacked message returns to the queue for someone else. Ack too early and you can lose work; ack never and the message comes back forever.
DEAD-LETTER QUEUEThe returns desk. After a message fails a set number of times — or is rejected — it is moved to a separate queue instead of being retried forever. It is the evidence locker for your failures, and the alarm bell (Section 8).
THE ARCHITECT'S THREE SENTENCES ABOUT RABBITMQ

“Producers publish to an exchange, never to a queue, so adding a consumer does not change the producer. Messages are at-least-once: ack after processing, so I design consumers to be idempotent. Anything that fails repeatedly goes to a dead-letter queue with an alert, because a message that fails silently is a customer I will hear from later.”

DURABILITY — THE COMBO PEOPLE GET WRONG

Durable queue alone is not enough, and persistent message alone is not enough. You need durable queue + persistent message + acknowledge after processing. Miss any one and a broker restart can still lose work. It is a three-legged stool, and it is a favourite interview trap.

SECTION 05 — THE AZURE THREE

Azure Messaging, Briefly

Three services that get confused with each other. They answer three different questions: “who must do this work?”, “who cares that this happened?”, and “can somebody please just take this job later?”

  AZURE SERVICE BUS AZURE EVENT GRID AZURE QUEUE STORAGE
What it is A full enterprise message broker: queues and topics/subscriptions, transactions, sessions, dead-lettering. An event router. It pushes “something happened” to subscribers — it does not hold work for you. A simple, very cheap, very large queue with no routing concepts at all.
Answers the question “Which service must reliably perform this business step?” “Who needs to be told that this happened?” “Can something please process this job when it gets a chance?”
Strengths Ordered sessions (all messages for one order to one consumer), transactions, duplicate detection, dead-letter queues, message deferral, rich delivery guarantees. Push-based and near real-time, fine-grained filters, huge fan-out, pay per event, almost nothing to operate. Built-in retries then dead-lettering. Enormous volume for very little money, trivially simple, no broker to size. Ideal for spillover and simple background job lists.
Weaknesses Most expensive; more concepts to learn; namespaces and throughput units to size. Overkill for “send an email”. Not a work queue: no long-lived consumer pulling work, small payloads, short retention. Not where you put a 5-minute report job. No routing, no topics, no sessions, weaker guarantees. First-in-first-out only, and ordering is best-effort.
Fits when Business workflows that need order, transactions or once-only effect — order processing, payment events, financial messages. The closest Azure cousin to RabbitMQ. Reactive plumbing: “blob uploaded → thumbnail”, “resource created → audit it”, fan-out to many microservices. Simple producer/consumer pairs, high-volume spillover, prototypes, work needing no ordering and no routing.
Think of it as RabbitMQ's managed enterprise cousin the postal notification service a very big cheap tray
HOW AN ARCHITECT CHOOSES, IN ONE PASS

Need order, transactions or dead-letter semantics for a business flow → Service Bus (or RabbitMQ if you want portability and already run it). Need to react to a platform event or fan out to many services → Event Grid. Need a cheap, huge, dumb job list → Queue Storage. And the honest note: if you already operate RabbitMQ and your team knows it, introducing a second broker “because Azure” is a cost, not an improvement.

INTERVIEW ANGLE

Do not recite feature lists. Say the difference in one line: “Service Bus carries the work; Event Grid carries the news; Queue Storage holds the overflow.” Then pick per flow, and say which ones you would not mix.

THE BUSINESS BENEFIT, IN WORDS A MANAGER USES

Faster checkout — the click returns in milliseconds, so fewer baskets are abandoned. No lost work at peak — a launch spike becomes a queue depth number instead of dropped orders. Failures are recoverable — the email retries and eventually succeeds, instead of vanishing with a 500 the customer saw once. That is the whole pitch, and it is why you do it — not because a queue looks modern on a diagram.

THE TRADE YOU ACCEPTED

The customer is no longer told the truth immediately. If email can fail, the order page cannot promise “confirmation sent”. Say “order received — confirmation is on its way”, give the work a status the user can see, and make sure someone is alerted when it stalls. Background work without a visible status and an alert is just work that disappeared politely.

SECTION 07 — TRY AGAIN, ON PURPOSE

Retry

Most message failures are temporary. The database was restarting. The API had a two-second blip. The network dropped one packet. A pod was rescheduled. Retry is how a system heals without a human.

DATABASE TEMPORARILY UNAVAILABLEFailover, backup, index rebuild, pool exhausted. Usually over in seconds — a retry three seconds later succeeds and nobody ever knows.
EXTERNAL API TIMEOUTThe payment or tax service was slow once. On attempt two it answers in 200 ms. Retrying is the difference between a recovered order and a support ticket.
NETWORK ISSUEA dropped connection, a DNS hiccup, a broken keep-alive. Transient by definition — and the reason the message is still safely in the queue.
SERVICE RESTARTA deploy or pod reschedule mid-processing. The consumer died before acknowledging, so the message goes back and the next attempt lands on a healthy instance.

RETRY VISUALISER — WHAT HAPPENS TO ONE MESSAGE

Why retry helps

It converts a blip into a non-event

Transient failures are the majority in a distributed system. A bounded retry with a delay recovers most of them with no human, no ticket and no customer-visible error. The message is safe in the queue the whole time — that is the point of having a queue.

Why unlimited retry is dangerous

It hides a real failure forever
  • A genuinely broken message loops forever, consuming workers and logs with no end and no signal.
  • It hammers a dependency that is already struggling — your retry becomes their outage.
  • It costs money on metered or billable calls (every retry can be a charge).
  • It destroys the evidence: nothing is ever “dead”, so nobody is alerted, and the customer finds out first.
HOW DELAYS HELP

Exponential backoff with jitter: attempt 1 now, attempt 2 after ~5 s, attempt 3 after ~25 s. The delay gives a restarting database time to come back and a struggling API room to breathe; jitter stops a thousand messages from all retrying on the same second and recreating the spike you were avoiding. Three to five attempts is a sane ceiling for most business flows — after that it is not transient, it is a defect, and it belongs in a dead-letter queue with a human.

WHEN RETRY SHOULD NOT HAPPEN AT ALL

Validation and business failures: a declined card, a missing required field, an order that no longer exists. Retrying is rude and expensive — the answer will be the same every time.
Non-idempotent work without protection: never retry “charge the card” unless the same attempt cannot charge twice (Section 9).
Permanent protocol errors: expired credentials, deleted resource, malformed payload. Fix the cause; do not retry the symptom.
When a human must decide: a fraud hold or a compliance review. Park it in a review state, not in a retry loop.

SECTION 08 — THE RETURNS DESK

Dead-Letter Queue

When a message fails repeatedly, the worst outcomes are both silent: retry forever, or delete it. A dead-letter queue is the third option — stop, keep the evidence, and tell a human.

WHY MOVED

Stop the bleeding, keep the body

REASON 1It is a poison message: the payload, the mapping or the code is wrong, and no amount of retrying will fix it. Every further attempt is waste.
REASON 2It is blocking the lane: on a FIFO queue a permanently failing head message can hold up everything behind it.
REASON 3It is damaging the dependency: hammering a sick payment provider with the same doomed request makes their outage yours.
REASON 4It is evidence: the payload, the exception, the attempt count and the correlation ID are exactly what the owning team needs to fix it.
WHO INVESTIGATES

Somebody specific, soon

OWNERSHIPThe team that owns the consumer, not the team that owns the broker. A DLQ is a product backlog item with a timestamp.
THE SIGNALAlert on DLQ depth > 0 (or > a small threshold, to absorb known-noisy flows) — and on the rate of messages arriving. Depth without a page is a museum.
THE DASHBOARDCount, oldest message age, error-type breakdown, and a one-click “view payload”. If your engineers must use a broker console in an incident, you have not finished the tooling.
HOW RECOVERED

Four legitimate outcomes

1 · FIX AND REPLAYCorrect the bug or repair the payload, then re-publish to the original queue. This is the normal path and it must be a one-click, auditable operation — not a copy-paste into a console.
2 · COMPENSATEThe work cannot be retried (the order was cancelled, the window closed). Run the compensation: refund, release stock, notify the customer. Then archive.
3 · DISCARD WITH A RECORDGenuinely useless — but write an audit row saying what it was, why it was dropped and who decided. “Discarded” must never mean “vanished”.
4 · ROUTE TO A HUMANFraud holds, compliance reviews, bad addresses: into a review queue where a person decides, with the customer told what is happening.
WHY SILENTLY DELETING FAILED MESSAGES IS DANGEROUS

Lost business: a deleted PaymentCompleted is an order that never ships and a customer who paid for nothing — the refund call comes from them, not from you. Lost evidence: you cannot debug a failure you threw away, so it will happen again. Lost signal: a rising DLQ is your earliest warning that a deploy broke something; deleting messages disables the alarm. Lost trust: “we'll retry it later” becomes “we have no idea what happened to your order”. A dead-letter queue with a number on it is a health indicator. An empty error log is not.

INTERVIEW ANGLE

“Three retries with exponential backoff and jitter, then to a DLQ with the payload and exception attached, an alert on depth, and a one-click replay tool. And an explicit list of failures that never retry at all — validation and business rules — because retrying those is how you turn one bug into a bill.”

SECTION 09 — TWICE IS ONCE

Duplicate Messages

The same PaymentCompleted will arrive more than once. Not sometimes — routinely: the publisher retried, the consumer crashed after doing the work but before acknowledging, or the broker redelivered. Design for it or it will design for you.

SCENARIO

PaymentCompleted delivered twice

WHY IT HAPPENSConsumer does the work, then dies before the ack → broker redelivers. Or the publisher could not confirm publish and sent again. Both are normal behaviour, not bugs in the broker.
WITHOUT PROTECTIONTwo shipments created. Inventory decremented twice — stock is now wrong and other customers see “out of stock”. Two confirmation emails. Loyalty points granted twice. Finance cannot reconcile the ledger.
WITH PROTECTIONThe second message finds orderId 8812 already processed and returns success without doing anything. One shipment, one decrement, one email, one ledger entry.
⇒ THE MESSAGE ARRIVED TWICE. THE BUSINESS RESULT HAPPENED ONCE. THAT IS THE WHOLE GOAL.
IDEMPOTENCY, IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Doing the same request again should not create an unwanted second result. Not “the message is ignored” — the consumer should still reply with what happened the first time. Practically: give every business action a stable identity (message ID, or better, a business ID like orderId + operation), record “I have done this” in the same transaction as the work itself, and skip if that record already exists. A unique constraint is the enforcement; the check is just a fast path.

Three practical examples

1 · SHIPPING

PaymentCompleted → create shipment

KEYUnique index on Shipments.OrderId.
BEHAVIOURSecond message tries to insert, the constraint rejects it, the consumer catches the duplicate and acks. One parcel, ever.
2 · STOCK

OrderPlaced → reserve inventory

KEYUnique on Reservations(OrderId, LineId) — and the decrement guarded by the same key.
BEHAVIOURStock is decremented exactly once per line, no matter how many times the message is delivered. Without this, duplicate delivery quietly invents sales you never made.
3 · EMAIL

OrderShipped → send confirmation

KEYUnique on Notifications(OrderId, Template), plus a fast pre-check in Redis for high-volume flows.
BEHAVIOURTwo emails is only embarrassing — until the template is a password reset or a one-time code, where it becomes a security problem. Same mechanism, higher stakes.
INTERVIEW ANGLE

“Delivery will be at-least-once, so I make every consumer idempotent by business key, not by message ID alone — a redelivered message may get a new envelope but the same order. I record the processed key inside the same database transaction as the work, so a crash cannot leave one without the other. Exactly-once effect, built from at-least-once delivery plus a unique constraint.”

SECTION 10 — SEQUENCE

Message Ordering

Some facts only make sense in order. The moment you scale to several consumers, you have usually given up the guarantee — so decide deliberately.

THE CHAIN

OrderCreated → PaymentCompleted → OrderShipped

IF SHIPPED ARRIVES FIRSTThe warehouse ships an order that was never paid. Real cost: goods gone, no money, and a recovery process you invented on a Friday.
IF SHIPPED ARRIVES BEFORE PAYMENT IS PROCESSEDConsumer 1 is still handling payment (slow gateway call) while consumer 2 finishes shipping. State says Shipped and AwaitingPayment at once — the dashboard contradicts itself and the email says both.
IF A RETRIED MESSAGE LANDS LATEPayment succeeded on attempt 3, but by then the inventory release from a stale timeout has already given the stock away. Overselling, from a timing problem.
⇒ ORDERING IS NOT A NICE-TO-HAVE FOR MONEY AND STOCK. IT IS A CORRECTNESS REQUIREMENT.
APPROACH HOW IT WORKS BENEFIT COST
Per-entity ordered lane All messages for one aggregate go to the same queue or session — orderId 8812 is always handled by the same consumer, in sequence. Real ordering for the things that need it, and you keep parallelism across different orders. One slow order can hold its lane; a hot entity becomes a hotspot. Sessions in Service Bus, consistent-hash exchange or a partition key in RabbitMQ.
Version / sequence number Stamp every message with a per-aggregate version. A handler ignores anything older than the state it already holds. Cheap, and robust to redelivery as well as reordering. Works even if the broker promises nothing. Requires the consumer to keep state and the producer to assign versions correctly.
State-machine guard A handler only acts if the current state allows it: OrderShipped is rejected unless state is Paid. Makes order safe by construction — wrong-order messages become no-ops instead of damage. You must define legal transitions and decide what a rejected message does (retry, DLQ, or wait).
Remove the need for order Design handlers to be commutative, or have one component recompute the truth from the source rather than react to deltas. The most scalable answer: no ordering constraint means full parallelism. Not always possible — some flows are genuinely sequential. It is a design goal, not a default.
WHAT AN ARCHITECT ACTUALLY SAYS

“I do not need global ordering — that would serialise the whole system and destroy throughput. I need ordering per order, so I group by orderId into a session or partition. On top of that I make every handler idempotent and guard it with a state machine, so a redelivered or reordered message cannot corrupt money or stock. Ordering is a correctness tool, not a performance feature.”

SECTION 11 — THE GUARANTEE

Message Delivery

Three words that decide whether you lose work, duplicate work, or spend a year chasing a promise. They are not definitions — they are risk profiles.

At-most-once

Fire, forget, never repeat

What it means: the message is sent and never retried. If it is lost in transit, or the consumer dies after receiving it, it is gone. Acknowledge before processing and this is what you have.

  • Risk: silent data loss. A dropped message is invisible — nothing alerts, nothing recovers.
  • Suitable for: telemetry, click and view counters, ephemeral presence, metrics you would sample anyway.
  • Never for: money, orders, inventory, anything a customer will ask about.

At-least-once

The practical default

What it means: the broker keeps trying until a consumer acknowledges. The message may be delivered more than once — a crash after the work but before the ack guarantees a redelivery.

  • Risk: duplicates. This is not a flaw, it is the deal — and it is why idempotency is mandatory, not optional.
  • Suitable for: almost every business flow: orders, payments, notifications, inventory, emails, reports. Pair it with a unique business key and a dead-letter queue.
  • The trade: you accept duplicate handling in exchange for never silently losing work.

Exactly-once

The promise nobody fully keeps

What it means: every message produces exactly one business effect, no matter what fails.

  • Risk: it is very hard to actually deliver across a distributed system. A broker can dedupe within its own store, but your business write lives in SQL Server — and no broker can wrap your database transaction in its own.
  • Suitable for: the effect, not the delivery. You build exactly-once effect by combining at-least-once delivery with an idempotent consumer.
WHY “EXACTLY ONCE” IS DIFFICULT IN REAL SYSTEMS

1 · Two systems cannot share one transaction. The broker's dedupe window and your SQL transaction are separate worlds. Committing one and crashing before the other leaves an ambiguity no protocol can resolve from outside.
2 · The crash can land anywhere. Consumer reads → does the work → dies before ack: redelivery. Ack arrives at the broker → your write was rolled back: lost. There is no single instant where both sides agree.
3 · The cost is brutal. True exactly-once needs distributed consensus or a shared transactional store, which costs throughput, latency and operational simplicity — usually for a problem idempotency solves in one unique index.
4 · It is the wrong question anyway. The business never asked “was the message delivered once?”. It asked “did the customer get charged once?”. Answer that with an idempotency key, and the delivery guarantee stops mattering.

THE SENTENCE FOR THE INTERVIEW

“I assume at-least-once everywhere, because the alternative is silent loss. I make every consumer idempotent on a business key inside the same transaction as the work, and guard transitions with a state machine. That gives me exactly-once effect on ordinary infrastructure — which is the only version of “exactly once” the business can actually use.”

SECTION 12 — ADD WORKERS

Scaling Consumers

One consumer is a promise you cannot keep at peak. The queue is already holding the work — adding consumers is the cheapest capacity you will ever buy.

SIMULATOR — 10,000 MESSAGES, HOW LONG TO DRAIN?

1 consumer
THROUGHPUT—messages / minute
DRAIN TIME—to empty the queue
REAL LIMIT—consumers or database?
DB LOAD—writes / minute

What it buys you

Near-linear capacity
  • Throughput scales with instances: the broker distributes messages, so N consumers is roughly N× the drain rate — no coordination code to write.
  • Failover for free: a consumer that dies simply stops acknowledging; its messages go to the survivors.
  • Independence: you scale the notification pool without touching the web tier or the database tier's shape.

What it costs you

Four new problems
  • Ordering breaks: with several consumers, two messages for the same order can be processed in parallel or in reverse (Section 10). Fix with per-entity grouping.
  • Duplicates get more likely: more consumers means more redeliveries on crash. Idempotency stops being optional.
  • The downstream becomes the bottleneck: 10 consumers is 10× the database writes, API calls and connection pools. Your ceiling moves to SQL.
  • Hot-partition skew: if you group by tenant or order, one huge tenant can monopolise a lane while others idle.
THE WORKED EXAMPLE

10,000 messages. One consumer processes 1,000/minute. One consumer: 10 minutes. Five consumers: 2 minutes. Twenty consumers: still ~10 minutes if the database can only absorb 1,000 writes a minute — you have bought workers that spend their lives waiting for a lock. Always scale the two together. The right question is never “how many consumers?” but “what does the next bottleneck cost, and is it cheaper than the backlog?”

WITH A QUEUE

The same 10,000 orders

HOW MESSAGES WAITAll 10,000 are published and acknowledged by the broker in seconds. The checkout returns in ~40 ms every time. The backlog is a number on a dashboard, not a queue of angry customers.
HOW CONSUMERS SCALEAt 1,000/minute the backlog clears in ~10 minutes — acceptable for email. Enable four more consumers and it is ~2 minutes, provided the database and the email vendor can take 5,000/minute. Scale the consumer pool, not the checkout.
WHEN A CONSUMER CRASHESIts unacknowledged messages return to the queue automatically and the survivors pick them up. Throughput dips for a few seconds, then recovers. Nothing is lost, nobody is paged for data loss.
WHAT HAPPENS TO FAILED MESSAGESBad payloads or repeated failures retry three times with backoff, then move to the dead-letter queue with payload and exception attached. An alert fires on depth. The rest of the flow continues untouched — one poison message cannot block 9,999 good ones.
HOW IT CATCHES UPBecause arrival was a 60-second burst and processing is a steady 1,000/minute, the backlog drains at a known rate. You can state it to the business: “every email goes out within 10 minutes of the order, guaranteed, and here is the graph.”
⇒ SAME TRAFFIC. ONE DESIGN HAS AN OUTAGE; THE OTHER HAS A BACKLOG CHART THAT GOES UP AND THEN COMES DOWN.
THE LESSON, IN ONE LINE

A queue does not make you faster. It makes the difference between “slow” and “down” visible, bounded and recoverable. The notification service was never going to do 10,000 a minute. The only choice was whether that fact would be a backlog or an outage.

SECTION 14 — CASE STUDY

Case Study: Order → Payment → Inventory → Notification

Built from your own stack — ASP.NET Core APIs, RabbitMQ (or Azure Service Bus), background workers, SQL Server, Redis where it earns its place. Deliberately not over-engineered: one API, two workers, one exchange, four queues.

1–3

User request → message → queue

1 · USER REQUESTPOST /orders. The API validates, prices and — inside one SQL transaction — inserts the order and an outbox row. It returns 201 in ~40 ms with the order id. No call to payment, inventory or email. That single transaction is why you never charge a card for an order that does not exist.
2 · MESSAGE CREATIONA dispatcher (a hosted service polling the outbox) reads unsent rows and publishes order.placed with a routing key, the tenant id, a correlation id and a message id. The outbox row is marked sent. If the broker is down, the row stays unsent and is published on the next pass — the transaction already succeeded, so nothing is lost.
3 · QUEUEA topic exchange routes by key: order.payment, order.inventory, order.notification. Queues are durable, messages persistent, each queue has its own dead-letter exchange. Adding an analytics consumer later means one new binding — zero change to the Order API.
4–5

Consumer and successful processing

4 · CONSUMERA BackgroundService (worker service, containerised) reads one message at a time with manual ack and prefetch limited to a handful — so one slow handler cannot hoard work. The handler runs inside a transaction that does two things: the business change, and an insert into ProcessedMessages(messageKey) with a unique index. Insert succeeds → first time. Duplicate key → already done, ack and move on.
5 · SUCCESSTransaction commits, the consumer acks, the broker deletes the message. Payment records the capture, inventory decrements under a row lock, notification queues the email. Order state moves Created → Paid → Confirmed. End to end the customer saw 40 ms and an honest “we're processing your confirmation”.
6–7

Retry and dead-letter handling

6 · RETRYTransient failures — timeout, deadlock, connection — are retried 3 times with exponential backoff and jitter (~5 s, ~25 s, ~2 min), then the message is nacked without requeue so the broker dead-letters it. Validation and business failures (declined card, order cancelled, missing field) skip retry entirely and dead-letter immediately with a classification on the message.
7 · DEAD LETTEREach queue dead-letters into its own DLQ carrying the original payload, the exception, the attempt count and the correlation id. Alert when depth > 0. The owning team gets a dashboard with one-click replay (after a fix or a repaired payload), plus explicit “compensate” and “discard with audit” options. Nothing is ever silently deleted.
8–9

Duplicate protection and monitoring

8 · DUPLICATE PROTECTIONPrimary: the ProcessedMessages unique index, committed with the work. Secondary: Redis holds recent keys with a short TTL as a fast pre-check, so a redelivered high-volume message does not even open a SQL transaction. Business keys — orderId + operation — not broker message ids, because a redelivered message may get a new envelope. Ordering is handled by grouping on orderId plus a state-machine guard, so a late or duplicate message cannot ship an unpaid order.
9 · MONITORINGQueue depth and age of oldest message per queue (depth without age hides a stuck head), DLQ depth with an alert, consume rate vs publish rate, retry and failure counts by error type, end-to-end latency from publish to processed via the correlation id, and consumer liveness. Every log line carries the correlation id so one order can be traced from API to broker to worker to database in one query.
⇒ NOT OVER-ENGINEERED: NO ORCHESTRATOR, NO SAGA FRAMEWORK, NO EVENT STORE. A TRANSACTIONAL OUTBOX, IDEMPOTENT WORKERS, A DEAD-LETTER QUEUE AND DASHBOARDS.
WHAT THIS DESIGN DELIBERATELY DOES NOT DO

No microservice per step — the API and two workers are enough at this scale. No event sourcing, no CQRS read store, no choreographed saga across five services. No distributed transaction, no two-phase commit. Every one of those is available later with a written trigger. The complexity here is already the honest minimum: outbox, idempotency, retry, dead-letter, monitoring. Anything less loses work; anything more needs a reason.

SECTION 15 — DECISIONS

10 Questions to Ask Before Introducing Messaging

Ask all ten before a queue appears on the diagram. Each one has an option you will reject and a trade-off you will sign for.

D01

Does this work need an immediate response?

OPTIONS — yes, in this click · yes, within seconds · later is fine · nobody ever looks at it.

RECOMMENDATION

Immediate and user-visible → synchronous call. Everything the user cannot act on → queue and return.

WHY

This one answer determines the shape of the whole flow. Queuing something the user needs now hides a business requirement behind a technical preference; keeping something synchronous that nobody waits for couples two services for no benefit.

TRADE-OFF

Queuing means the user is told “processing”, so you owe them a visible status and honest language about timing.

ASK THE BUSINESS

“If this takes 30 seconds instead of 2, does anyone care?” Their answer, not your preference.

D02

What happens if the consumer is unavailable?

OPTIONS — fail the request · retry at the caller · hold in a queue and process later · drop it.

RECOMMENDATION

Hold in a durable queue with bounded retry, then dead-letter. Dropping is only acceptable for data whose loss costs nothing.

WHY

The consumer will be unavailable — during a deploy, a restart, an incident. The question is whether that becomes a backlog you can measure or an outage you cannot.

TRADE-OFF

A queue means you must watch depth and age, and drain it after the incident. Unmonitored, the backlog becomes tomorrow's surprise.

PROVE IT

Kill the consumer in staging and show that nothing is lost. That test is the design.

D03

Can processing happen later — and how much later is acceptable?

OPTIONS — seconds · minutes · hours · by next business day.

RECOMMENDATION

Write the number down per flow and size the consumer pool to meet it with headroom. A number is a requirement; “fast” is a mood.

WHY

The acceptable delay tells you how many consumers you need and how loudly to alarm on backlog. “Email within 10 minutes” and “email eventually” are different systems with different costs.

TRADE-OFF

Tighter deadlines cost more consumers, more database write capacity and more monitoring. Loose deadlines cost nothing but need a stated expectation.

WATCH FOR

A delay that quietly grows because nobody set a number. Age of oldest message is the metric that catches it.

D04

How many messages can arrive, and how spiky is it?

OPTIONS — steady · daily peak · campaign bursts · unpredictable (10× in a minute).

RECOMMENDATION

Size for peak. If bursts are 10×, let the queue be the buffer and size consumers for the sustained rate you can afford.

WHY

Queues absorb bursts; consumers cost money continuously. Absorbing a 60-second spike and draining calmly is almost always cheaper than running peak capacity 24/7.

TRADE-OFF

Absorbing means a backlog exists by design — so depth and drain-time dashboards are part of the deliverable, not an extra.

THREE NUMBERS

Peak publish rate, sustained consume rate, worst-case drain time. That paragraph is the sizing.

D05

Can messages be duplicated?

OPTIONS — assume no · assume yes and design for it · buy exactly-once from the broker.

RECOMMENDATION

Assume yes, always. Make every consumer idempotent on a business key, enforced in the same transaction as the work.

WHY

At-least-once delivery plus a consumer crash after the work but before the ack guarantees redelivery. Believing otherwise is how double shipments and double charges happen.

TRADE-OFF

Idempotency adds a processed-keys table and a little code to every handler. Far cheaper than reconciling a duplicate payment.

NEVER RELY ON

Broker-level dedupe as your only defence — it does not cover your database transaction.

D06

How long can a message wait in the queue?

OPTIONS — minutes · hours · days · indefinitely.

RECOMMENDATION

Set an explicit maximum age per queue, alarm on it, and decide what happens to a message that breaches it — replay, compensate, or escalate.

WHY

An unbounded wait is indistinguishable from a lost message. Age is what turns “the queue is deep” into “customers have been waiting four hours”.

TRADE-OFF

Expiry means some work may be abandoned deliberately — so it needs an owner, an audit record and often a customer message.

METRIC

Age of oldest message, per queue. Depth alone lies.

D07

What happens after repeated failure?

OPTIONS — retry forever · retry N then dead-letter · retry N then discard · park for human review.

RECOMMENDATION

Retry 3–5 times with backoff and jitter, then dead-letter with payload, exception and attempt count — and alert on depth. Never discard silently.

WHY

Forever burns workers and hides the failure. Discard loses money and evidence. Dead-letter stops the damage, keeps the evidence and pages a human.

TRADE-OFF

A DLQ implies somebody must work it. If nobody owns it, you have built a graveyard with a dashboard.

ALSO DECIDE

Which failures never retry at all — validation and business rules — because retrying them is how one bug becomes a bill.

D08

Do messages need ordering?

OPTIONS — no · per entity · strict global order · order does not matter if handlers are safe.

RECOMMENDATION

Per-entity ordering only (group by orderId or tenantId), plus idempotent handlers and a state-machine guard so a reordered message cannot cause damage.

WHY

Global order serialises the system and destroys throughput. Per-entity gives correctness where it matters and parallelism everywhere else. The guard makes it safe even when the grouping fails.

TRADE-OFF

Grouping creates lanes, and a hot entity can monopolise one — you accept head-of-line risk on that lane in exchange for correctness.

BEST OUTCOME

Design the handlers so order genuinely does not matter. That is the answer that scales best.

D09

How do we monitor failures — not just success?

OPTIONS — logs only · queue depth · depth + age + DLQ · end-to-end correlation.

RECOMMENDATION

Queue depth, age of oldest message, DLQ depth with an alert, consume vs publish rate, retry counts by error type, consumer liveness, and publish-to-processed latency via a correlation id.

WHY

Asynchronous systems fail quietly: the request already returned 200, so nothing in the API looks wrong. Without these metrics the first signal is a customer asking where their order is.

TRADE-OFF

You must put a correlation id on every message and carry it through every log line. Small cost — and it is the difference between debugging and guessing.

THE TEST

“Can you tell me, right now, how many orders placed in the last hour have not had their notification sent?” If not, monitoring is not done.

D10

When is a direct API call simply better?

OPTIONS — always queue · always call · decide per step.

RECOMMENDATION

Use a direct call when the caller needs the answer now, when the operation is a fast read, or when you would otherwise invent request/reply over a queue to get one answer back.

WHY

Adding a queue to a two-second lookup buys latency, eventual consistency and a broker to operate — for a problem a synchronous call solves today.

TRADE-OFF

Synchronous means shared fate: their outage is yours. Accept that deliberately for the steps that genuinely need a live answer.

SMELL

If you are building request/reply over a message bus for a single call, you wanted an HTTP call.

THE MAIN LESSON

A queue is not a feature you add; it is a set of promises you take on. Delivery guarantees, ordering, retries, dead-letters, duplicates, monitoring and an owner for failures. Ten answers, written down. That document is the architecture.

SECTION 16 — HONEST LIST

10 Messaging Mistakes

Each one is a promise made and never kept.

01

Using queues everywhere

Messaging looks like modern architecture, so every call gets wrapped in one. A two-second lookup now needs request/reply over a broker, eventual consistency appears where nobody asked for it, and debugging one action means three log files.

Queue the work the user does not wait for. Keep reads and anything needing an answer now synchronous. Justify each queue with a sentence about failure or latency.

02

No retry strategy

The happy path works in the demo, so nobody writes the failure path. Then a dependency has a two-minute blip and every message in that window is lost or dead-lettered by the thousand.

Decide per message type: what is transient, how many attempts, what delay, what never retries. Write it down before the first consumer ships.

03

Infinite retry

“Eventually it will work.” Instead a poison message loops forever, consuming workers and logs, hammering a struggling dependency and generating no alert — because nothing ever “failed”.

Bound the attempts. After the last one, dead-letter with the evidence and page someone.

04

Deleting failed messages

They clutter the queue and the team wants a clean graph. So they are dropped — along with the money, the shipment and the only evidence of the bug.

Dead-letter, never delete. If a message is genuinely useless, archive it with a reason and an owner.

05

No dead-letter handling

The queue exists because the broker created it automatically, but nobody owns it, nothing alerts on it, and it quietly grows to 40,000 messages over three months.

Own every DLQ: alert on depth, dashboard it, and build one-click replay before you need it in an incident.

06

Assuming messages arrive only once

Delivery semantics were never read, so consumers do real work then acknowledge — and a crash after the work guarantees a redelivery that ships twice.

Assume at-least-once. Idempotent consumers on business keys, enforced in the same transaction as the work.

07

Ignoring ordering

Adding a second consumer breaks the implicit sequence nobody documented. Suddenly “shipped” can be processed before “paid”, and the system ships unpaid orders.

Group messages per entity, stamp versions, and guard handlers with legal state transitions so wrong-order messages become no-ops.

08

No monitoring

The API returns 200, so the dashboards are green — while 9,000 notifications sit in a queue nobody looks at. Asynchronous failure is invisible from the request path.

Depth, oldest-message age, DLQ depth, consume vs publish rate, retry counts, consumer liveness, end-to-end latency with correlation ids.

09

Making background jobs depend on one server

A BackgroundService hosted inside the web app, with in-memory state and no coordination. Deploy the site and background work stops; scale to two instances and the same job runs twice.

Separate worker processes, state in the queue and database, idempotent handlers. Background work must survive a deploy and scale horizontally.

10

Using messaging without a clear business reason

“Microservices should communicate asynchronously.” No failure was identified, no latency target set — so the team pays for retries, dead-letters and distributed debugging without buying anything.

Name the business outcome: absorbed a 10× burst, survived a vendor outage, cut checkout from 2.5s to 40ms. If you cannot, do not build it.

SECTION 18 — CHECK

Self-Test: Ten Messaging Scenarios

Decisions and failures, not definitions. Answer out loud, then reveal.

Q1The team wants to put every service-to-service call on a queue. What do you say?
I ask one question per call: does the caller need the answer to continue? Reads, login, payment authorisation and any stock check that gates the order stay synchronous — a queue there adds latency, eventual consistency and a broker for no benefit. I queue the work nobody waits for: email, notifications, reports, indexing, analytics. Then I justify each queue in one sentence about failure or latency. If a sentence does not exist, the queue does not either.
Q2A consumer processes a message, then crashes before acknowledging. What happens — and what must already be true?
The message returns to the queue and another consumer processes it — so it runs twice. What must already be true: the consumer is idempotent, keyed on a business identifier, and the “I have done this” record is committed in the same transaction as the work. Then the second attempt finds the key and acks without doing anything. If that protection is missing, this normal broker behaviour becomes a duplicate shipment or a double charge.
Q3Your dead-letter queue has 12,000 messages and nobody noticed for three weeks. What went wrong, and what do you change?
Two failures: nobody owned the DLQ and nothing alerted on it. I assign ownership to the team that owns the consumer, alert on depth greater than zero (or a small threshold), add a dashboard with count, oldest age and error breakdown, and build one-click replay with audit. Then triage the 12,000 by error type — most will be one deploy bug with a bulk fix and a bulk replay.
Q4A payment message keeps failing with “insufficient funds”. Should you retry it?
No. That is a business answer, not a transient fault — retrying will return the same result forever while costing a billable call each time. Classify it, dead-letter it or move the order to a “payment failed” state immediately, notify the customer honestly, and reserve retries for genuine transients: timeouts, deadlocks, connection drops, restarts.
Q5Order shipped is sometimes processed before payment completed. How do you fix it?
Three layers, in order of preference. Group messages per order so one lane handles one order in sequence. Stamp a per-order version and ignore stale messages. And guard the handler with a state machine: OrderShipped is only accepted from Paid; anything else is rejected or deferred. I would not chase global ordering — that serialises the whole system for a problem per-entity grouping already solves.
Q6Is “exactly once” delivery realistic? What do you actually build?
End-to-end exactly-once delivery is not realistic across a broker and a separate database — no protocol can make one transaction span both, and a crash between them is always ambiguous. What I build is exactly-once effect: at-least-once delivery plus an idempotent consumer keyed on a business id, committed with the work. The business question was never “was the message delivered once?” — it was “was the customer charged once?”.
Q7One consumer drains 10,000 messages in 100 minutes. You add nine more and nothing improves. Why?
The bottleneck moved downstream. Ten consumers generate ten times the database writes, API calls and connection usage, and something down there is saturated — usually SQL locks or an external rate limit. Adding workers now just builds a queue in front of the same wall. I measure the real limit, raise it if it is cheap (indexes, batching, a higher rate limit), and only then scale consumers to match.
Q8Notification processing falls 45 minutes behind during a flash sale. Is that an incident?
Only if 45 minutes breaks a promise. I set the acceptable delay with the business in advance — say 10 minutes for order confirmation. If we agreed 10 and we are at 45, it is an incident: I scale consumers, check for a slow downstream, and communicate. If we agreed “within the hour”, it is a backlog behaving exactly as designed, and I say so with the graph rather than paging anyone.
Q9Where does Redis belong in a messaging design — and where does it not?
Redis is a fast pre-check and a rate limiter, not the system of record. I use it to short-circuit duplicate detection on high-volume consumers, to cache permissions and reference data the handler needs, and to throttle calls to a vendor. I do not use it as the durable record of “this message was processed” — that belongs in the same database transaction as the business change, because Redis can be flushed and the work cannot be.
Q10A teammate says “the queue makes us reliable, so we don't need monitoring”. Respond.
The queue makes loss less likely and failure invisible. The request already returned 200, so the API looks perfectly healthy while 9,000 notifications sit unprocessed. Without depth, age, DLQ alerts and end-to-end latency, the first signal is a customer asking where their order is — three days late. Asynchronous systems do not fail loudly; they fail quietly and slowly, which is exactly why they need the most monitoring.

FINAL — CARRY THESE FORWARD

Architect Mindset, Day 6

Five rules. Not about RabbitMQ — about work that outlives the request.

01

A queue is not a feature. It is a set of promises.

The moment you add one you owe delivery guarantees, ordering, retries, dead-letters, duplicate protection, monitoring and an owner for failures. If you cannot name those, you have not added a queue — you have added a place for work to disappear.

02

Assume the message arrives twice, and that the consumer dies mid-work.

Both are normal broker behaviour, not defects. Idempotent consumers on a business key, committed with the work, turn both into non-events. This single habit prevents double charges, double shipments and double emails for the rest of your career.

03

Retry what is temporary. Never retry what is a decision.

Timeouts, deadlocks and restarts deserve backoff and jitter. Declined cards and invalid input deserve an immediate, honest stop. Infinite retry does not make a system reliable — it makes failure invisible and expensive.

04

A dead-letter queue is a health indicator, not a rubbish bin.

Alert on its depth, own it, build one-click replay. Silently deleting failed messages deletes the money, the evidence and the early warning — and the customer becomes your monitoring system.

05

Asynchronous failure is invisible. Instrument it or it does not exist.

Depth, age of oldest message, DLQ count, consume rate, consumer liveness, correlation id end to end. The API returns 200 either way — so the queue is where the truth lives.
