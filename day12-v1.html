SECTION 01 — THE REAL PROBLEM

"It Works" Is Not a Health Status

An application can be running and still be failing the business. The process is alive, no crash, no red banner — and yet customers are suffering. This is the situation monitoring exists for.

Think of a busy e-commerce platform. Every one of these can be true while the service "works":

  • The API is very slow — requests succeed, but checkout takes 12 seconds. Nobody is "down".
  • The database is overloaded — CPU at 97%, queries queueing. The app keeps answering, just late.
  • Redis is failing — every cache miss now hits SQL. Still working. For now.
  • The queue is growing — order confirmation emails are 40 minutes late. Orders "succeed".
  • The external payment API is slow — your code is perfect; customers still abandon carts.
  • Errors are increasing — 0.4% → 2% → 6%. Each step is invisible one request at a time.
  • One customer consumes too many resources — tenant ACME's nightly report slows down the other 9,999 companies.

None of these crashes the app. All of them cost money. The only difference between the two stories below is visibility.

NO MONITORING — CUSTOMER DISCOVERS

  1. 14:02A bad query ships. Database CPU climbs to 95%.
  2. 14:07Checkout errors quietly rise from 0.4% to 6%.
  3. 14:23Customers complain on social media. Support tickets spike.
  4. 14:40Support escalates. Engineers start logging in.
  5. 14:58Someone guesses correctly: it's the database.
  6. 15:40Query killed, index added. System recovers.
≈ 98 MINUTES OF BUSINESS IMPACT · CUSTOMERS FOUND IT FIRST

GOOD MONITORING — TEAM DISCOVERS

  1. 14:02Same bad query ships. Database CPU climbs.
  2. 14:04Alert fires: DB CPU > 90% for 2 min and checkout p95 crossed the SLO.
  3. 14:06On-call opens the dashboard: query-time spike, one new slow query.
  4. 14:09Trace confirms: the slow span is one SQL call, deployed 14:00.
  5. 14:12Query killed, rollback of the offending change.
  6. 14:15Metrics back to normal. Most users never noticed.
≈ 13 MINUTES · DETECTED BEFORE MAJOR BUSINESS IMPACT
WHY THE ARCHITECT OWNS THIS

The difference between these two stories is not skill — the same engineers fixed it both times. The difference is that in story two, the system was designed to explain itself. What gets measured, what gets alerted, what gets kept, what gets sampled — those are architecture decisions, made before the incident, not during it.

INTERVIEW INSTINCT

In any design interview, expect: "How would you know it's broken?" A design with no answer for that is incomplete — say it yourself before they ask: "and for visibility, I'd instrument…"

SECTION 02 — TWO WORDS, DIFFERENT JOBS

Monitoring vs Observability

People use the words interchangeably. They are not the same job, and an architect who mixes them up builds systems that page you without explaining anything.

Monitoring"Is the system healthy?"
You decided the questions in advance: is error rate high? is CPU high? is the queue growing? Monitoring watches known conditions and tells you when one is true.
Observability"Why is the system unhealthy?"
You ask a new question you never planned — "why is checkout slow only for tenant ACME's mobile users?" — and the system's outputs (logs, metrics, traces) let you answer it without shipping new code.
The monitoring exampleAn alert fires: checkout error rate is 8%. That is monitoring doing its job — a predefined condition became true. It does not tell you who, where, or why.
The observability exampleYou then slice the 8%: it is one tenant, one endpoint, and only when Redis misses. You discovered a pattern nobody pre-programmed — because the telemetry was rich enough to explore.

One more pairing to make it stick:

QUESTION WHO ANSWERS IT
"Are we up? Is the error rate high? Is the queue growing?" Monitoring — predefined checks on predefined signals.
"Why is checkout slow only for EU mobile users since 14:00?" Observability — explore high-cardinality data you never pre-aggregated.
THE ONE-LINER TO REMEMBER

Monitoring tells you the building is on fire. Observability tells you which room it started in. You need both: alerts without drill-down produce panic; drill-down without alerts produces discovery by customers.

There is a price tag attached. Observability needs rich, high-cardinality data — logs with tenant IDs, traces of individual requests — and that costs more to store and index than simple counters. Deciding how rich is rich enough is an architect decision, not a tool default. You will meet this trade-off again in the case study (Section 17).

SECTION 03 — THE TOOLKIT

Three Signals, One System

Almost everything you will ever monitor reduces to three kinds of data: logs, metrics and traces. Same system, three different questions.

Logs — what happenedTimestamped records of events, one line per thing that happened. Useful when you need detail: the exact error, the exact user, the exact input. E-commerce example: "Order ORD-58211 for tenant ACME — payment declined by gateway (code 05), took 1.2 s."
Metrics — how it behaves over timeNumbers measured at regular intervals: counts, rates, durations. Cheap to store, perfect for dashboards and alerts. Useful when you need to spot a change fast. E-commerce example: checkout error rate moved from 0.4% to 8.1% in five minutes.
Traces — where the time wentThe journey of one request across many services, split into timed steps. Useful when a request is slow and no single service looks guilty. E-commerce example: checkout took 5.2 s — and 4.6 s of it sat inside the payment provider call.
How they work togetherA real incident is solved by all three, in order: a metric wakes you up, a trace points at the guilty component, a log explains what it did. One signal alone leaves you guessing.

Watch the same incident from all three angles:

METRIC — "SOMETHING IS WRONG"Checkout error rate jumps 0.4% → 8.1% at 14:03. The alert pages the on-call. Nobody knows why yet.
TRACE — "WHERE"The failed requests all share one slow span: PaymentService.Charge, timing out at 30 s. Order service and database look innocent.
LOG — "WHY"Inside that span: "gateway returned 504 after 3 retries · req=8f3c-22ab · tenant=ACME". Root cause found — the payment provider, not your code.
DESIGN RULE

When you design a new service, ask three questions in order: What must I log? What must I measure? What must I be able to trace? If telemetry is added after go-live, it is always too late for the first incident.

SECTION 04 — SIGNAL ONE

Logs That Answer Questions

A log line has one job: when something goes wrong at 2 a.m., it must answer four questions — What happened? Where? When? For whom? — for someone who was not there.

What a useful log line contains

FIELD WHY IT EXISTS EXAMPLE
Timestamp UTC, with milliseconds — so you can order events across services. 2026-02-12T14:03:22.481Z
Level How urgently should a human care: ERROR is actionable, WARN is suspicious, INFO is a business event, DEBUG stays off in production. WARN
Service + instance "Where" — which service, which machine/container wrote this. payment-service / i-0a3f
Operation What the code was doing. Searchable without reading code. op=ChargeCard
Request / correlation ID The single most important field — stitches every log line of one request together across every service. req=8f3c-22ab
User / tenant context "For whom" — where appropriate: tenant ID, user ID. In multi-tenant SaaS this is how you find the noisy or affected customer. tenant=ACME order=ORD-58211
Result What happened — success, failure, and the machine-readable reason. result=declined gateway_code=05
Duration How long it took — slow is a kind of broken. duration_ms=1204
Error details For failures: exception type and message (full stack at ERROR level only). GatewayTimeoutException

Put together, one line tells the whole story — no code access needed:

2026-02-12T14:03:22.481Z WARN payment-service/i-0a3f op=ChargeCard req=8f3c-22ab tenant=ACME order=ORD-58211 amount=142.00 result=declined gateway_code=05 duration_ms=1204

What must never be logged

  • Passwords — ever, in any form, even "for debugging".
  • Access tokens and session cookies — a stolen log becomes a stolen identity.
  • API secrets and connection strings — logs are the least-protected store in most companies.
  • Sensitive personal information — full card numbers (PCI), national IDs, health data. Mask them: card=****-4242.

Why so strict? Because logs are read by many tools and many people, copied into tickets, shipped to third-party platforms and kept for months. Anything you log is data you now protect. One leaked log file has ended careers and triggered breach notifications.

THE ARCHITECT'S BAR

A log nobody can search under stress is not a log — it is noise with a storage bill. Structure beats volume: machine-readable fields (JSON), levels with a purpose, and a correlation ID on every line. "Log everything" is not a strategy; it is a cost and a haystack.

SECTION 05 — SIGNAL TWO

Metrics That Reveal Trouble Early

Metrics are the cheap, always-on vital signs. They cannot tell a story, but they can raise their hand in time. Here is what a production system should measure, by layer.

LAYER METRIC WHAT IT TELLS YOU DANGER SIGN
Application Request count How much traffic the system is serving right now. Sudden drop = upstream failure or routing problem.
Error rate Share of requests failing — the fastest symptom of almost anything. 0.5% → 2% in minutes is an incident, even if "most" requests work.
Response time (p50 / p95 / p99) What users actually experience. Slow is a kind of broken. p99 ten times the p50 — a tail of suffering users.
Throughput Work completed per second — orders/min, messages/sec. Throughput flat while load rises = saturation somewhere.
Infrastructure CPU Compute pressure on each instance. Sustained > 85% — no headroom for spikes.
Memory Allocation pressure, leaks, GC stress. Steady climb that never falls back = leak.
Disk Space and I/O capacity. > 85% full — a full disk crashes writes hard.
Network Bandwidth and errors between components. Retransmissions / packet loss = mysterious latency.
Database Query time Slow queries make everything above them slow. p95 climbing after a deploy — new bad query.
Connections Pool usage — is the app running out of ways to talk to the DB? Near pool max = timeouts under load.
CPU The database's own effort — the classic bottleneck. > 90% sustained = everything slows soon.
Failures / deadlocks Contention and broken statements. Any spike right after a release.
Cache (Redis) Hit rate Is the cache doing its job? < 80% — keys wrong, TTLs wrong, or cache under pressure.
Miss rate Every miss is a future database query. Rising miss rate = database load is about to rise.
Memory How full the cache is. Near max → evictions → hit rate collapses.
Failures Connection errors to the cache. Any — cache-down means a database storm is coming.
Queue (RabbitMQ) Message count Backlog — work waiting to happen. Growing steadily = consumers can't keep up.
Processing time How long each message takes a consumer. Rising = a dependency of the consumer is degrading.
Failed messages / DLQ Messages that could not be processed. Any DLQ growth — poison message or a bug.
Queue delay (age) How long the oldest message has waited — the latency of async work. Oldest message > 5 min — emails, confirmations are now late.

Why averages lie

Here are 100 real requests to your Order API. Ninety-two of them were fast. Eight were not. The average says the system is "235 ms — fine". The average is lying by omission.

100 REQUESTS TO POST /ORDERS — SORTED BY DURATION
HOVER A BAR TO INSPECT ONE REQUEST

Read the percentiles out loud — this is interview language:

  • p50 (median) = 145 ms — half of requests are faster than this. The "typical" user.
  • p95 = 440 ms — 95% of requests are faster; 5% are slower. The edge of normal.
  • p99 = 2,400 ms — 1 in 100 requests waits 2.4 seconds. At 5,000 requests/second, that is 50 users per second having the bad experience.
THE RULE

Alert and SLO on p95/p99, never on the average. Averages hide the tail, and the tail is where customers live. If your only latency number is an average, you do not know your latency.

SECTION 06 — SIGNAL THREE

Tracing: Where Did the 5 Seconds Go?

In a distributed system, one user request travels through many services. No single log line can see the whole journey — the delay is spread across components, and each one looks innocent on its own.

Take a checkout: User → API → Order Service → Payment Service → Database. It took 5 seconds. Every service logged "I was fast". Here is what a trace shows that no log could:

FIG. 12.1 — A TRACE WATERFALL: ONE REQUEST, FOUR SERVICES, ONE GUILTY SPAN

Three concepts, kept conceptual — this is what you must be able to explain, not configure:

  • Trace — the whole journey of one request, drawn as a tree of timed steps. The checkout above is one trace.
  • Span — one unit of work inside the trace: an HTTP call, a SQL query, a queue publish. Each span knows its parent, its start, and its duration. The red bar is one span.
  • Correlation / request ID — one ID, generated at the front door, carried in a header (e.g. X-Request-Id or the W3C traceparent) through every HTTP call, every service, every queue message — and stamped on every log line. It is the thread that stitches the whole story together.

In your world: ASP.NET Core middleware generates the ID at the edge, your HttpClient handlers flow it downstream, and you copy it into RabbitMQ message headers so even the background workers stay part of the same story.

THE QUESTION TRACING EXISTS TO ANSWER

"Where did the 5 seconds go?" You do not guess. You open the waterfall and read the longest bar. And a trade-off to know: tracing every request at high volume costs money — so production systems sample (e.g. 1 in 10 requests), while always capturing the two kinds of traces you actually need: errors and slow requests.

SECTION 07 — THE SYSTEM CHECKS ITSELF

Health Checks: Alive vs Ready

A running process is not automatically a serving process. Health checks are how the platform — Kubernetes, a load balancer, App Service — learns the difference before the customer does.

Liveness — "Is this application running?"Is the process alive and able to respond at all — not deadlocked, not frozen? If liveness fails, the right response is to restart the instance. So a liveness check must test only the process itself — never the database.
Readiness — "Is it ready to receive traffic?"Can this instance do its job right now: database reachable, cache reachable, queue connected, warm-up done? If readiness fails, the right response is to stop sending it traffic — remove it from the load balancer — and let it recover. No restart.
Running but unhealthy — how?Thread pool deadlocked. Connection pool exhausted. Dependency down. Disk full. Still warming its cache after start. The process is "up" — and every request it accepts will fail.
Why two checks, not oneOne check cannot decide between "restart me" and "don't send me traffic". Confusing them turns a database blip into a cluster-wide restart storm.

The ASP.NET Core shape of it

ASP.NET Core ships health-check middleware. The architectural decision is not the syntax — it is which dependency belongs to which endpoint:

ENDPOINT WHAT IT CHECKS IF IT FAILS… KUBERNETES MAPS TO
/health/live Nothing external — just "the process answers". Instance is dead/frozen → restart it. livenessProbe
/health/ready SQL ping · Redis ping · queue connection · warm-up complete. Dependency degraded → remove from load balancer, keep running. readinessProbe
THE CLASSIC MISTAKE

Put the database check in liveness and watch what happens: the database hiccups for 30 seconds → every instance fails liveness → Kubernetes restarts all of them → cold instances hammer the already-struggling database → the blip becomes an outage. Liveness checks the self. Readiness checks the dependencies. Say that sentence in an interview and you have answered the question before they finish it.

SECTION 08 — WAKING HUMANS UP

Alerts: The Metric Exists — So What?

A metric existing is not a reason to wake anyone up. An alert is a very specific contract: "a human must do something, now." Most monitoring systems don't fail from too little data. They fail from too many alerts nobody trusts.

THE BAR FOR ANY ALERT

Before an alert may page someone, it must have four things: a symptom users would feel, a threshold with duration (not a one-second spike), an owner, and a runbook — the written first steps. Missing any of the four, it belongs on a dashboard, not a phone.

Alerts worth their pager

ALERT WHY IT MATTERS FIRST ACTION
Error rate > 2% for 5 min Users are failing, right now, across the front door. Open the service dashboard; check the most recent deploy.
API p95 latency above SLO for 10 min The experience is degrading before it becomes errors. Open slow traces → find the slowest span.
Database CPU > 90% for 10 min The truth layer is saturating — everything above it is next. Find the top query; kill runaways; scale reads.
Queue backlog / oldest message > 5 min Async work (emails, confirmations, stock) is now late. Check consumer health and errors; scale consumers out.
Redis unavailable The cache is gone — a database load storm is seconds away. Confirm; enable DB-protected fallback; watch SQL.
Disk > 85% A full disk turns "slow" into "hard down" without warning. Clean or archive; expand volume.
Unhealthy instances > N Capacity is quietly shrinking toward a tipping point. Replace instances; investigate the pattern, not just the instance.

Bad alerts and alert fatigue

  • CPU at 70% pages someone, yet no user feels it — you alerted on a cause, not a symptom. Causes belong on dashboards; symptoms page.
  • Every single exception alerts — transient, retried, expected failures flood the channel until real ones drown.
  • Thresholds without duration — one garbage-collection pause or traffic blip pages at 3 a.m.
  • Six alerts for one cause — DB slows, and latency, errors, queue and saturation all page separately. Group by incident, not by graph.
  • No owner — an alert that goes to "everyone" goes to no one.

Alert fatigue is the end state: 300 alerts a week teaches the team to mute, and the one alert that mattered gets muted too. The test is brutal and simple — if an alert was ignored three times, fix it or delete it.

SEVERITY — THREE BUCKETS, NO MORE

Page — user impact now, a human acts in minutes (error rate, SLO burn, payment failures). Ticket — real, but survivable until morning (disk trend, DLQ growth). Dashboard only — interesting, never actionable at night (CPU, memory, cache hit rate). If everything is a page, nothing is.

SECTION 09 — THE AZURE STACK

Azure Monitoring at Architecture Level

Three names cover almost all of it. You do not need portal skills — you need to know which piece owns which decision.

  • Azure Monitor — the umbrella platform. Collects metrics and logs from everything (apps, VMs, databases, Kubernetes), runs alert rules, renders dashboards. When in doubt, "it's an Azure Monitor feature".
  • Application Insights — the application-performance layer (it lives inside Azure Monitor). Drop the SDK into an ASP.NET Core API and it auto-captures requests, dependencies (SQL calls, Redis calls, HTTP calls), exceptions and distributed traces — plus availability tests. This is where your logs, metrics and traces from code land.
  • Log Analytics — the queryable log store (KQL). Application Insights data and platform resource logs flow into a workspace here, where you join, slice and search across services.
FIG. 12.2 — ASP.NET CORE API → APPLICATION INSIGHTS → AZURE MONITOR → LOG ANALYTICS

The six decisions the architect makes

DECISION WHAT YOU DECIDE TYPICAL ANSWER
Logs What gets logged, structure, levels, sampling of verbose events. Structured JSON; correlation + tenant ID on every line; DEBUG off in production.
Metrics Which metrics, at what granularity, which are custom. Auto request/dependency metrics per service + custom business metrics; 1-minute granularity.
Traces Distributed tracing on/off, sampling strategy. On everywhere; ~10% sampling — but always capture errors and slow requests.
Alerts Symptoms vs causes, severity, who gets paged. ~10 symptom alerts with runbooks; action groups route pages to the owning team.
Dashboards Who looks at what, and when. Three views: SRE health · service deep-dive · business outcomes.
Retention How long data stays searchable, where archives go, what it costs. 30–90 days hot in Log Analytics; compliance archive to cheap storage for 1+ year.
INTERVIEW PHRASING

"I'd instrument the ASP.NET Core APIs with Application Insights, flow everything into a Log Analytics workspace, alert on SLO-burning symptoms only, and tier the retention so we pay hot prices only for recent data."

SECTION 10 — THE AWS STACK

AWS Monitoring, Mapped to What You Know

AWS has two main names: CloudWatch — the umbrella for metrics, logs, alarms and dashboards — and X-Ray — distributed tracing. The decisions are identical to Azure; only the labels change.

NEED AZURE AWS
Application monitoring Application Insights CloudWatch (+ X-Ray, CloudWatch Agent)
Metrics Azure Monitor metrics CloudWatch Metrics (custom metrics included)

One practical note for real systems (and interviews): if your architecture spans both clouds — or you simply refuse vendor lock-in — OpenTelemetry is the open standard that emits logs, metrics and traces in a portable shape, and both Azure Monitor and CloudWatch/X-Ray ingest it. Same three signals, same decisions, one wire format.

THE TAKEAWAY

An architect is not hired to know where the button is. You are hired to say: what we measure, how long we keep it, who it wakes, and what it costs — on any cloud.

SECTION 11 — LAYER BY LAYER

Monitoring a Distributed System

Here is the reference architecture for the rest of the day — the same e-commerce backbone you know: User → API Gateway → Order Service → Payment Service → Queue → Inventory Service → Database. Each layer gets its own watch.

COMPONENT WHAT TO MONITOR WHY IT MATTERS EXAMPLE ALERT
API Gateway Request rate · 5xx share · latency · auth failures The front door. Every user problem is visible here first — and here only, when services behind it lie. 5xx > 1% for 5 min
Order Service Order-creation rate · error rate · p95 latency The money path. If this bends, revenue bends with it. Orders/min 40% below last week's same hour
Payment Service Provider latency · timeout rate · declined vs failed An external dependency you cannot control — but the impact is still yours. Measure it from your side. Provider p95 > 2 s for 5 min
Queue Depth · oldest-message age · consumer lag · DLQ count The buffer that absorbs failure — right up until it overflows silently. Oldest message older than 5 min
Inventory Service Processing time per message · failure rate · stock-sync errors Async correctness: the order "succeeded", but did the stock actually update? DLQ count grows two releases in a row
Database Query time · connections · CPU · deadlocks The truth. A slow database makes every service above it slow — and innocent. Query p95 > 500 ms for 10 min
WHERE FAILURES ACTUALLY SHOW UP

Watch the boundaries and hand-offs — service-to-service calls, service-to-database, service-to-queue, you-to-provider. Failure almost always surfaces at an edge before it surfaces inside a box. Instrument the edges first.

SECTION 12 — THE WALKTHROUGH

Troubleshooting: "Order Creation Is Slow"

The phone rings: customers report order creation is slow. This is the scenario an interview will give you — and the scenario that separates systematic thinkers from guessers. Do not open code. Open the timeline.

  1. Confirm and scope itIs p95 actually up, or is it one loud customer? Which endpoint — all of them, or just POST /orders? Which tenants? And the golden question: when did it start, and what changed then? A deploy, a traffic spike, a provider incident.METRICS FIRST · ALWAYS
  2. Check the front doorGateway: request rate, error rate, latency. Is traffic 3× normal (a campaign, a retry storm, an attack)? If so, the slowness is load — everything downstream is a victim.RULE OUT TRAFFIC BEFORE BLAMING CODE
  3. Open the traces of slow requestsFilter traces > 2 s for POST /orders. Read the waterfall: which span eats the time — SQL, Redis, payment call, or the service's own code?THE LONGEST BAR IS YOUR SUSPECT
  4. Follow the suspect into its signalsIf the DB span is long: query times, CPU, connections, locks. If the payment span: provider latency and timeout rate. If Redis: hit rate and failures. Each dependency has its own dashboard — go look.ONE HYPOTHESIS, ONE DASHBOARD
  5. Check the resources underneathCPU, memory, disk, network on the service instances. Thread-pool starvation and GC pressure look exactly like "mysterious slowness" from the outside.INFRA LAST — BUT NOT NEVER
  6. Verify with one change at a timeKill the query / roll back the deploy / raise the timeout — one move, then watch the metric. If it didn't move, your hypothesis was wrong. That is information, not failure.CHANGE ONE VARIABLE, WATCH THE CURVE
  7. Fix, communicate, reviewMitigate first (rollback beats elegance), tell support and stakeholders what happened, then hold the post-incident review: what signal should have fired earlier? That review writes next quarter's alerts.EVERY INCIDENT IMPROVES THE MONITORING

Three plausible root causes — and how the signals pick between them

ROOT CAUSE METRIC CLUE TRACE CLUE LOG CLUE
1 · Database is slow Query p95 up, DB CPU up or connections near max; app CPU normal. The long span is a SQL call inside Order Service. Slow-query warnings, command timeouts; started right after a deploy or report run.
2 · Payment provider is slow Provider latency and timeout rate up; DB and Redis metrics normal. The long span is the external payment call — your code waiting, not working. Gateway timeouts after retries; provider's own status page may agree.
3 · Queue processing is delayed Queue depth and message age rising; POST /orders itself may be fast — confirmations and stock updates are what lag. Order trace is quick; the downstream consumer spans (inventory) crawl or stall. Consumer logs: slow handling, repeated retries, or too few consumers after a scale-down.
THE SENIOR MOVE

The amateur dives into code. The architect asks "what changed?" first — because 80% of production incidents correlate with a deploy, a traffic change, or a dependency change. Then: metric to scope, trace to locate, log to explain. Same order, every time.

SECTION 13 — BEYOND THE MACHINES

Business-Focused Monitoring

Every metric so far has been technical. But the business does not earn CPU. The sharpest monitoring systems watch outcomes — because outcomes can collapse while every technical graph stays green.

  • Payment failure rate — the direct revenue leak. A rise means money is being refused at the door.
  • Orders completed per minute — the heartbeat of an e-commerce platform. Compare against last week's same hour, not zero.
  • Failed checkout percentage — the funnel's wound: carts started vs orders finished.
  • Notification delivery rate — silent failures here erode trust long before anyone complains.
  • Active tenants — in multi-tenant SaaS, a sudden drop in active companies is a product emergency, not a tech one.
  • Failed background jobs — invoices, reports, syncs. The work nobody watches until a customer asks where their invoice is.

Why do these catch what technical metrics miss? Picture a feature-flag mistake that breaks the checkout button on mobile. CPU is fine. Error rate is zero — no requests are failing, they are simply never sent. Latency is beautiful. The only signal in the entire system is orders per minute falling 40%.

THE BEST ALERT IN THE BUILDING

For many platforms, the single most valuable alert is: "orders per minute dropped 40% vs the same hour last week." It fires when nothing else can. It is also the alert the CEO understands — which is exactly the point: business metrics translate your system into the language of impact.

SECTION 14 — THE PROMISE LADDER

SLI, SLO and SLA — In Plain English

Three letters-words that sound like legal fog but are actually one simple idea in three stages: measure it → target it → promise it.

TERM IN ONE SENTENCE API EXAMPLE — TARGET: 99.9% SUCCESSFUL REQUESTS, FAST ENOUGH
SLI What we measure. A precisely defined number, computed from telemetry. "The percentage of Order API requests that return success and finish under 400 ms, measured over 30 days."
SLO What target we set ourselves. Internal, chosen by the team, no contract attached. "That SLI must stay at 99.9% or better over any rolling 30 days."
SLA What promise we make to customers. External, contractual — with consequences (credits, penalties) if broken. "We contractually guarantee 99.5% monthly availability; below that, customers receive service credits."

Notice the deliberate gap: the internal target (SLO 99.9%) is stricter than the external promise (SLA 99.5%). That gap is your early-warning zone — you burn internal budget and react before you break the promise. A team whose SLO equals its SLA discovers failure at the same moment as its lawyers.

TWO RULES AND ONE PHRASING

Rule 1: never promise what you cannot measure — an SLA without an SLI is a hope. Rule 2: 100% is not an SLA, it is a wish; every architect says so out loud. In an interview: "I'd set the SLO tighter than the SLA, so the team feels the burn before the customer does."

SECTION 15 — THE PRICE OF NINES

Availability and the Error Budget

High availability sounds free until you convert it into time. An error budget is the amount of failure your promise allows — and it is the most practical way to argue about reliability, cost and release speed without adjectives.

AVAILABILITY PROMISE ALLOWED DOWNTIME / MONTH ALLOWED DOWNTIME / YEAR WHAT IT REALLY MEANS
99% ~7.3 hours ~3.7 days Internal tools. Nobody gets paged at night.
99.9% ~43 minutes ~8.8 hours The sensible default for customer-facing SaaS.
99.95% ~22 minutes ~4.4 hours Needs real redundancy and real discipline.
99.99% ~4.3 minutes ~53 minutes Multi-region, automated failover, constant testing — and a bill to match.

Now the budget idea. If you promise 99.9%, you "may" fail for 43 minutes this month. That allowance is the error budget, and it changes five architectural conversations:

  • Architecture — every extra nine demands more redundancy: replicas, multi-zone, then multi-region. You pay for the nines in hardware and complexity.
  • Cost — 99.99% is not 0.09% more expensive than 99.9%; it is a different class of system. The architect's job is to refuse nines nobody needs.
  • Monitoring — you must measure the SLI exactly, continuously, and agree on the clock. "Was it down 41 minutes or 46?" becomes a contractual question.
  • Deployment — budget healthy? Ship boldly, take risks. Budget nearly burned? Freeze risky releases, canary everything, slow down.
  • Recovery — when the budget is small, recovery must be rehearsed: failover drills, backup restores, documented runbooks. Unrehearsed recovery eats budgets whole.
THE INTERVIEW SOUND-BITE

"Reliability is a budget, not a virtue. I'd set the SLO with the business, measure the burn weekly, and let the remaining budget decide how fast we deploy — more budget left, more risk we may take; budget gone, reliability work jumps the queue."

Case Study: Observability for the SaaS Platform

THE SYSTEM

DIMENSION FIGURE DIMENSION FIGURE

Ten decisions. For each: why this → what problem it solves → the trade-off accepted.

  1. Logs — structured JSON, via Serilog into Application InsightsWHY THIS → every line carries timestamp, level, service, operation, correlation ID and tenant ID, so any incident is searchable in seconds.PROBLEM → "what happened, where, when, for whom" answered without SSH or code access.TRADE-OFF → FULL-RICH LOGS COST STORAGE: VERBOSE EVENTS ARE SAMPLED, DEBUG STAYS OFF
  2. Metrics — auto App Insights metrics + custom business metricsWHY THIS → request rate / errors / duration come free per service; on top: orders/min, payment failure rate, checkout failure %.PROBLEM → symptoms surface in under a minute, and business impact is visible even when all tech graphs are green.TRADE-OFF → CUSTOM METRICS MULTIPLY COST: ONLY BUSINESS-CRITICAL ONES, 1-MINUTE GRANULARITY
  3. Traces — distributed tracing across every serviceWHY THIS → a checkout crosses gateway → order → payment → DB; no single log sees the journey.PROBLEM → "where did the 5 seconds go" becomes a read, not a guess.TRADE-OFF → 10% SAMPLING OF NORMAL TRAFFIC; ERRORS AND REQUESTS > 2 s ALWAYS CAPTURED
  4. Health checks — /health/live + /health/ready on every serviceWHY THIS → liveness proves the process answers; readiness proves SQL, Redis and the queue are reachable.PROBLEM → the load balancer stops feeding broken instances before customers meet them.TRADE-OFF → OVER-SENSITIVE READINESS FLAPS INSTANCES IN AND OUT: SHORT TIMEOUTS, GENEROUS FAILURE THRESHOLDS
  5. Alerts — ~10 symptom alerts, each with severity, owner, runbookWHY THIS → error rate, SLO burn, p95, queue age, DB CPU, Redis down, payment failures, orders/min drop, disk, unhealthy instances.PROBLEM → humans are woken only when a human changes the outcome.TRADE-OFF → COVERAGE VS FATIGUE: CAUSES LIVE ON DASHBOARDS, ONLY SYMPTOMS PAGE
  6. Dashboards — three audiences, three viewsWHY THIS → SRE system-health view, per-service owner view, business outcomes view (orders, tenants, payments).PROBLEM → in an incident, everyone shares one picture instead of six private hunches.TRADE-OFF → BUILD AND MAINTENANCE TIME; A DASHBOARD NOBODY OPENS IS WASTE — REVIEW USAGE QUARTERLY
  7. Business metrics — the outcome layerWHY THIS → orders/min, failed checkout %, payment failure rate, notification delivery, active tenants, failed jobs — with week-over-week baselines.PROBLEM → catches the "everything green, revenue falling" failures no tech metric can see.TRADE-OFF → BASELINES MUST HANDLE TIME-OF-DAY AND SEASONALITY, OR EVERY MONDAY MORNING LOOKS LIKE AN INCIDENT
  8. Error tracking — exceptions grouped, linked to tracesWHY THIS → App Insights groups exceptions by type and stack; every group links to a sample trace and the correlation ID.PROBLEM → "a new error type appeared after the 14:00 deploy" becomes a one-click finding.TRADE-OFF → TRANSIENT RETRIED ERRORS CREATE NOISE: CLASSIFY RETRYABLE VS FATAL, ALERT ONLY ON FATAL SPIKES
  9. Tenant-level visibility — the multi-tenant lensWHY THIS → tenant ID as a dimension on logs and key metrics; top-N tenants dashboard; per-tenant rate and error alerts.PROBLEM → finds both victims ("only ACME is failing") and culprits ("ACME is 40% of traffic") in one query.TRADE-OFF → 10,000 TENANTS = CARDINALITY EXPLOSION: TRACK TOP-N AND AGGREGATES, NOT EVERY TENANT AS A METRIC SERIES
  10. Retention & cost — tiered by age and valueWHY THIS → 30 days hot in Log Analytics (fast queries, incident work), then archive to cheap storage for 1+ year (compliance), sampled telemetry, and a telemetry-spend dashboard with its own alert.PROBLEM → at 5,000 RPS, "keep everything" is a five-figure monthly bill and a haystack.TRADE-OFF → ARCHIVED DATA IS SLOW TO QUERY: ACCEPTABLE — OLD DATA IS FOR AUDITS, NOT INCIDENTS
THE PATTERN

Every decision above is the same three lines: a capability chosen, a failure it answers, a price accepted. That is the entire job. In an interview, recite the triad and you sound like someone who has carried a pager.

Where Telemetry Comes From

The whole day in one diagram: the system runs top-to-bottom; telemetry flows sideways out of every box into one pipeline, and comes back as alerts and dashboards.

FIG. 12.3 — THE OBSERVABILITY ARCHITECTURE: SOLID LINES CARRY REQUESTS, DASHED LINES CARRY THE STORY
  • Logs are collected in application code — the logging middleware in each ASP.NET Core service, plus platform/diagnostic logs from the gateway, SQL and the queue host.
  • Metrics are collected twice: the SDK auto-counts requests and dependencies per service, and the platform agents report infrastructure and PaaS health (CPU, connections, queue depth).

SECTION 19 — THE GRAVEYARD

Ten Mistakes That Kill Monitoring Systems

Every one of these has caused a real outage to last longer than it should. Learn them here so you never have to learn them at 3 a.m.

1 · Logging everythingCost explodes, and the one line you need drowns in a haystack of noise. Volume is not visibility.
2 · Logging sensitive dataPasswords, tokens, card numbers in logs = a breach waiting for a leaked workspace. Logs are the least-protected store.
3 · No correlation IDFive services, fifty log lines, zero way to know they belong to one request. Troubleshooting becomes archaeology.
4 · Monitoring only server CPUCPU is green while a bad query, a dying cache or a failing provider ruins the experience. Infra health ≠ user health.
5 · No business metricsEvery graph green, revenue down 40%. Without orders/min and checkout %, the money failures are invisible.
6 · Too many alerts300 alerts a week teaches muting. The alert that mattered gets muted with the rest. Fatigue is a design failure.
7 · No dashboardsAn incident with no shared picture becomes six people with six private hunches. Dashboards are the war-room wall.
8 · No tracing across services"It's not my service" — said by every service. Without traces, distributed systems devolve into blame games.
9 · No health checksThe load balancer happily routes traffic to a deadlocked instance until customers report it. The platform cannot help what it cannot see.
10 · Alerts with no action"Something paged. Now what?" Monitoring without runbooks is an expensive anxiety machine — everyone knows, nobody acts.

DAY 12 — CLOSE

The Architect Mindset, Day 12

Five rules from today. If you remember nothing else, remember these — they are the whole of monitoring and observability.

01

Monitor outcomes before machines.

Orders per minute, payment failures, checkout completion — the money signals fire when every CPU graph is green. Business metrics are not a nice-to-have; they are the last line of defense.

02

Every alert earns its pager.

Symptom, threshold with duration, owner, runbook — or it goes on a dashboard. An ignored alert is a debt; paid three times, it must be fixed or deleted. Fatigue is a design failure, not a people failure.

03

Averages lie.

The tail is where customers live. p95 and p99 are the honest numbers — for alerts, for SLOs, and for any sentence that starts with "it's fast".

04

Nothing is investigable without a thread.

One correlation ID, born at the front door, carried through every service, every queue message, every log line. Without it, a distributed system is five strangers describing the same event.

05

Observability is designed, not bolted on.

What we measure, how long we keep it, who it wakes, what it costs — decided before go-live, defended like any other architecture choice. Telemetry added after the first incident is always too late for the first incident.

A system you cannot see is a system you cannot run. From today on, every architecture you draw carries a telemetry line — or it is not finished.
