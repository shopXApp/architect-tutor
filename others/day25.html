PART 01 — UNDERSTAND THE BUSINESS FIRST

Ten Thousand Businesses, One Application, Zero Leaks

An architect who picks technology before understanding the business builds the wrong thing elegantly. This platform's business in plain language: many companies each run their whole business in the same software — their customers, products, stock, suppliers, orders, purchases, money and reports — and no company must ever see, touch or slow down another company's business.

THE BUSINESS PROBLEM IN ONE PARAGRAPH

A trading company sells products it bought from suppliers. It must always know how much stock it has, what customers owe it, what it owes suppliers, and what it sold this month. Do this for 10,000 different companies inside one shared system, with each company's staff using it daily, month-end close arriving like a tidal wave, and reports that must not freeze the day's sales. The architecture problems that fall out of that paragraph are: tenant isolation, write correctness on shared stock, read-heavy dashboards, heavy reporting, noisy tenants, and boring operations.

Why business understanding comes before technology

  • It sets the isolation bar. "Companies are small resellers" and "companies include regulated pharma" need completely different data isolation and audit depth. Ask first.
  • It sets the consistency bar. Stock that gets oversold costs real money and trust; dashboards two minutes late cost nothing. The business tells you which is which.
  • It sets the growth shape. 10,000 small companies scale differently from 50 large ones — and the noisy-tenant problem exists only because it's multi-company.
  • It sets the cost line. Enterprises pay for reliability, but "technically better" that multiplies ops cost 5× gets rejected by the same board that approved the platform.
  • It sets what "done" means. Orders created per day, close speed, report freshness — these become your non-functional requirements, and those drive the architecture.

What each company has its own of

PEOPLEUsers with roles (admin, sales, warehouse, purchase, accounts), each seeing only their own company's screens and data.
CUSTOMERSOwn customer list, addresses, credit limits, order history — Company A's customer list is Company A's private data.
PRODUCTSOwn catalog: categories, variants (sizes/colors), prices, product settings. Two companies can sell the same physical product — the records are separate.
STOCKOwn inventory by warehouse/location: on-hand, reserved, adjusted, transferred. Overselling their own stock is their disaster; touching someone else's is your lawsuit.
SUPPLIERSOwn supplier list, purchase history, outstanding balances and credits.
ORDERS & PURCHASESOwn sales orders (customer-facing) and purchase orders (supplier-facing), each with its own lifecycle and history.
MONEYOwn payments, refunds, receivables and payables; per-company payment settings and provider accounts.
RECORDS & RULESOwn reports, dashboards, audit trail, and settings: currencies, taxes, number formats, business rules, branding.
THE INTERVIEW OPENING

Start the interview by restating: "So the product promise is: every company gets the full business in one place, completely separated from the other nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine, at a cost we can operate." That sentence frames the day's two giants — isolation and operational cost — and everything after serves it.

PART 02 — FUNCTIONAL REQUIREMENTS

What the Platform Must Let Companies Do

The full feature map, grouped by area. Notice two things: nearly every feature has a per-company setting attached, and several features (orders, purchases, inventory, payments) must stay correct under concurrency. Both observations shape the architecture in later parts.

Company management

  • Create company — onboard a tenant: name, legal info, locale, currency, tax setup, plan.
  • Company settings — business rules that differ per company: numbering formats, tax rates, credit policies, approval workflows.
  • Company-specific configuration — custom fields, warehouses, price lists, document templates.
  • Company isolation — the hard requirement: data, permissions and limits never cross company boundaries.

User management

  • Login — company-scoped accounts; a user belongs to exactly one company.
  • Roles — e.g. Company Admin, Sales, Inventory, Purchase, Reports, Accounts.
  • Permissions — granular: create order vs cancel order vs refund; view cost prices vs sell prices.
  • User access — activate/deactivate users, optional SSO for larger companies.

Product management

  • Products — catalog with names, codes, descriptions, images.
  • Categories & variants — size/color variants, category trees.
  • Pricing — cost price, sell price, price lists, tax rates.
  • Product configuration — barcode, stock warnings (min/max), reorder points, active/inactive.

Customer management

  • Customer details — names, contacts, company info; addresses (multiple, with default).
  • Credit information — credit limit, outstanding balance, payment terms.
  • Order history — full per-customer history for service and reporting.

Supplier management

  • Supplier details — contacts, lead times, terms.
  • Purchase history — what was ordered from whom, when, at what price.
  • Supplier balance — amounts payable, partial payments, credits.

Sales orders

  • Create / update / cancel / confirm order — the core lifecycle with stock and money consequences at each step.
  • Order history — full audit of every state change per company.

Purchase

  • Create purchase order — buy from supplier.
  • Receive stock — full or partial receiving; receiving increases inventory.
  • Update inventory on receipt — purchases are the main source of stock.

Inventory

  • Stock in / stock out / adjustment / transfer — every movement type with a reason.
  • Current stock — live on-hand per product/warehouse, with reservations separated.
  • Inventory history — every movement recorded; the ledger of stock.

Payment

  • Payment — record customer or supplier payments; partial payments.
  • Refund — reverse a payment with full traceability.
  • Payment status — per-order and per-account status (unpaid, partial, paid, refunded).

Reporting

  • Sales / inventory / purchase reports — per company, by period, by customer/product.
  • Dashboards — daily operational views: open orders, low stock, receivables, sales today.
  • Company-level reports — month-end close, profit & loss inputs, stock valuation.

Notifications

  • Email / SMS / push — order confirmations to customers, low-stock alerts to warehouse, payment reminders, and in-app notifications.

Audit

  • User activity — who did what, when (logins, updates, deletions).
  • Business activity — every order/purchase/stock/money change with before/after where it matters. This is how companies trust a shared system.

PART 04 — ASK CLARIFYING QUESTIONS

Twenty-Five Questions Before a Single Box

In the interview you won't ask all of these — but knowing which question changes which decision is what makes you sound senior. Each one below says why it matters.

Business questions

  • What business do these companies run? Retail, trading, manufacturing — order lifecycles, stock complexity and reporting differ completely. Why it matters: sets which modules are core vs optional.
  • Is there a hard month-end close window? If every company closes on the same day, that's a designed-in global spike. Why it matters: drives reporting architecture and capacity planning.
  • What are we replacing, and is there a migration? Legacy data volumes and cut-over risk are usually the real project. Why it matters: migration strategy shapes data design and timeline.
  • Who pays, and what's the unit economics per company? Small tenants can't justify heavy resources. Why it matters: sets the cost ceiling per tenant.

User questions

  • How many users per company on average, and per large company? 10 average but 500 for the top 10? Why it matters: drives the noisy-tenant design and per-company limits.
  • Which roles actually exist in practice? Real role inventory, not imagined ones. Why it matters: permission model granularity.
  • Do users work in shifts or timezones within a company? Warehouse and sales peaks may differ. Why it matters: load patterns, cache TTL choices.

Scale questions

  • What is the expected orders-per-day per company, and the largest company? 99% of companies at 20/day, one at 2,000? Why it matters: hot-tenant handling and scaling keys.
  • What is peak factor (peak vs average)? 3×? 10×? Why it matters: capacity sizing vs graceful degradation.
  • How big can one company's catalog get? 100 SKUs or 500,000? Why it matters: product caching, search and per-tenant storage design.

Data questions

  • How long must history be kept, and can we tier it? 7 years hot, or cold after 12 months? Why it matters: storage tiers, partitioning by time.
  • What happens when a company cancels? Data offboarding, export, deletion rules. Why it matters: tenancy lifecycle and compliance.
  • Are there data residency or compliance rules per company? Some industries demand it. Why it matters: may force per-tenant databases or regions.

Security questions

  • Any regulated data (payments, health, government)? PCI, industry audits. Why it matters: encryption, audit depth, integration choices.
  • Do large companies require SSO or their own identity? Why it matters: auth architecture (OIDC support from day one).
  • How much audit is legally required? Why it matters: audit table design and retention.

Availability questions

  • What is acceptable downtime — minutes or an hour? During month-end? Why it matters: RTO, failover ambition, deploy windows.
  • Can the system run read-only in degraded mode? Dashboards yes, sales paused. Why it matters: degradation switch design.
  • What RPO (data loss) is acceptable? Seconds or hours? Why it matters: replication and backup strategy cost.

Reporting questions

  • Must dashboards reflect today's sales instantly, or within 15 minutes? Why it matters: read model delay budget — the single biggest reporting decision.
  • Who runs the heavy reports, and when? One CFO per company at month-end. Why it matters: report scheduling, queues, and export limits.

Integration questions

  • Which external systems are mandatory at launch? Payments, email, SMS, accounting, shipping. Why it matters: integration boundary count and failure designs.
  • Do any integrations need to be synchronous in the user flow? Payment probably yes; shipping no. Why it matters: where the core can wait and where it cannot.

Deployment questions

  • One region, or multiple from the start? Where are the companies? Why it matters: regionalization, DR design, latency targets.
  • How big is the DevOps team, and what's the deploy culture? "Limited DevOps team" usually kills big microservice programs. Why it matters: architecture choice between modular monolith and services — this single fact is often decisive.

MODULE VS SERVICE — THE RULE

Everything starts as a module with a clean interface inside one deployable. An area earns independent-service status when one of three facts becomes true: it scales on a different curve (reporting reads vs order writes), it must fail independently (payment provider churn, notification vendor outages), or a different team must deploy it on a different cadence. Until then, the module boundary keeps the same benefit — with local transactions and one database.

PART 06 — CHOOSE THE OVERALL ARCHITECTURE

Monolith, Modular Monolith, or Microservices?

The wrong answer is choosing microservices because the system is "big". Size is not the criterion. Evaluate honestly.

CRITERION A — TRADITIONAL MONOLITH B — MODULAR MONOLITH C — MICROSERVICES
Team size One codebase, no module discipline → merge fights, slow teams. Modules with owned code, enforced boundaries — one team or a few work cleanly. Each service wants ops ownership — 8+ services need a big org.
Business complexity OK until modules bleed into each other. Boundaries keep order/purchase/inventory/payment concerns separate. Best for truly independent domains — overkill if domains share one database and transactions.
Traffic (5k RPS) Fine. Fine. Over-engineered — 5k RPS does not demand 12 services.
Deployment needs Every change ships everything; slow, risky releases. Still one deploy, but module tests and staged features reduce risk. Independent deploys — valuable only when cadences truly diverge.
Scaling Scale the whole app for one hot module. Adequate; hot modules can be extracted later on evidence. Scale the hot thing alone — but at 5k RPS you rarely need it.
Cost Cheapest to run. Near-cheapest; one deployable, one database family. N× infra, N× observability, network hops, on-call for N systems.
DevOps maturity (limited team!) Simplest ops. Still simple: one pipeline, one set of metrics. Kills a limited DevOps team: service discovery, deploys, tracing, per-service alerts.
Data consistency Local transactions are free. Local transactions within the deployable; boundaries are code-level. Distributed data: sagas, reconciliation, eventual consistency everywhere.
Failure handling One crash takes all (mitigate with replicas + graceful degradation). One deployable, but fault isolation by module + graceful degradation. Isolated failures — at the price of cascading network failures if not designed.
Future growth (5×) Will strain: hot modules, big deploys, big database. Clean seams make the next step (extract hot modules) cheap. Ready — but you paid for it from day one.
RECOMMENDATION — AND WHY

Modular monolith with a clean module boundary per business area, one SQL database family with tenant-aware schema discipline, plus separated reporting read model and message broker from day one. Why: 5,000 RPS is well inside a strong monolith's reach; the limited DevOps team cannot afford N services; business data is transactional and multi-module (order touches product, inventory, payment — one database makes that correct); and the real problems — tenancy, reporting, noisy tenants — are solved by data and traffic architecture, not by service count.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE THE DECISION LATER

Extract to a service when a measured fact appears: reporting reads starving writes (→ reporting service + own store), notification vendor outages affecting core (→ notification service), a hot tenant needing its own scaling (→ per-tenant isolation tier), a compliance requirement on payment data (→ payment service with its own boundary), or a second team needing independent deploy cadence. The seams are already drawn — extraction is a deployment change, not a rewrite.

PART 08 — MULTI-TENANT DESIGN

One System, Ten Thousand Owners — How Do They Stay Apart?

Multi-tenant means one software instance (and usually shared infrastructure) serving many customers — tenants — who must never see each other's data. The choice is how physically separate the tenants' data is.

OPTION SECURITY / ISOLATION COST & MAINTENANCE SCALE & BACKUP
A · Shared DB, shared tables (TenantId on every row) Isolation by discipline: every query must filter TenantId. A bug = cross-tenant leak. Mitigate with enforced tenant context + query-layer rules + tests + constraints. Cheapest: one DB, one schema, easy upgrades, trivial per-tenant tooling. One storage pool; per-tenant backup/export must be query-based; hot tenants share index pages.
B · Shared DB, separate schema per company Stronger: no shared tables, schema-per-tenant. Still one server to fail; bugs rarer but schema migration ×10,000 is heavy. 10,000 schemas × every migration = operational pain; connections and caches balloon. Per-tenant backup easier; still shared hardware.
C · Separate database per company Strongest isolation and per-tenant backup/restore/delete; compliance-friendly. 10,000 databases = 10,000× backups, upgrades, monitoring, failover. Cost explodes. Excellent per-tenant scale control; global reporting across tenants becomes a federated nightmare.
D · Hybrid: A by default, C (or B) for the few who need it Default cheap and uniform; escape hatch for large, regulated or contractually isolated tenants. Two paths to operate — but only a handful of tenants ride the premium path. Big tenants get dedicated databases (or dedicated clusters at the extreme) with their own scaling and backup cadence.
RECOMMENDATION FOR 10,000 COMPANIES

Hybrid. Default = shared database, shared tables, TenantId on every row and in every composite key (cheapest at 10,000 tenants, uniform upgrades, one reporting pipeline). Premium path = dedicated database for a handful of very large or regulated companies, behind the same module interfaces. The data-access layer abstracts "which home does this tenant's data live in?" so the app code stays identical for both paths.

How tenant information flows end to end

  1. LOGINUser logs inAuthentication checks the credentials and binds the session to one company: the token encodes user id + company id + roles.
  2. TOKENToken carries the identityEvery request sends the token; the gateway validates it and resolves (user, company, roles) into the request context. A user is their company — there is no "switch company" in the data layer, only in the identity layer.
  3. APIAPI layer enforces contextNo handler may accept a company id from the client body; the tenant comes from the token only. Rate limits apply per user and per company.
  4. BUSINESS LOGICBusiness logic is tenant-boundModule interfaces take the tenant from context, never from input parameters a caller can lie about. Cross-tenant operations (platform admin) are a separate, audited path.
  5. DATABASEDatabase queries carry TenantId automaticallyThe data-access layer appends TenantId = context to every query (or uses tenant-scoped connection filters); composite keys are (TenantId, business key); unique constraints include TenantId so Company A's "Order #1001" and Company B's "Order #1001" cannot collide.

How one company is stopped from touching another's data

  • Token-bound tenant — the tenant can't be spoofed by request parameters.
  • Mandatory tenant filter in the data layer — not "remember to filter in the code" but the framework behavior; unfiltered queries are impossible (or flagged by review/tests).
  • Composite keys and constraints — ids are only unique within a tenant; cross-tenant joins need explicit, reviewed permission.
  • Permission checks per action — role grants within the tenant (cancel order, refund, export); nothing grants across tenants.
  • Automated isolation tests — a permanent test suite: user of Company A attempts every read/write against Company B's ids; any success is a failing build. This is what makes "we trust the discipline" a system property.
  • Audit + anomaly monitoring — cross-tenant access attempts are impossible by design and alarmed as security incidents if the pattern appears.
PART 09 — AUTHENTICATION AND AUTHORIZATION

Who Are You, and What May You Touch Here?

Authentication answers "who are you?" (identity). Authorization answers "what are you allowed to do?" (permissions). In a multi-company system, both answers are scoped to the company — the same username at two companies is two different people.

The design, piece by piece

  • Login: phone or email + password (or OTP), bound to the company account; failed-login throttling per user and per IP. Optional SSO (OIDC/SAML) for larger companies — support it at the identity layer so companies can plug in their own identity.
  • Token: short-lived access token (minutes) + refresh flow; the token carries user id, company id, roles. Everything downstream reads identity from the token — never from the request body.
  • User identity: a user belongs to exactly one company; deactivating a user is per-company; session revocation is per-user or per-company (admin can log everyone out).
  • Company identity: the company record is the tenant's root — its settings, limits, plan, and data home. Platform admin operates on companies via a separate, heavily audited console — never via normal user tokens.
  • Roles: built-ins per company (Company Admin, Sales, Inventory, Purchase, Accounts, Reports) + custom roles for bigger companies.
  • Permissions: fine-grained (action, area) pairs: order.create, order.cancel, refund.approve, stock.adjust, report.export… Roles map to permission sets; permissions are cached with the token's lifetime + invalidation on change.
ROLE EXAMPLE TYPICAL PERMISSIONS
Company Admin Everything in the company: users, settings, products, stock, money, reports — except platform-level controls.
Sales User View customers/products, create & confirm orders, view own order history, limited reporting. No cost prices, no refunds.
Inventory User Stock in/out, adjustments, transfers, stock reports. No customer personal data, no money.
Purchase User Suppliers, purchase orders, receiving, supplier balances. No sales data beyond demand hints.
Report User Read dashboards and run reports within their scope; exports logged. No write permissions at all.
ENFORCE CONSISTENTLY — THREE RULES

1. Check permissions server-side at the operation boundary, every time — never trust the UI. 2. Use one permission service/layer so "can user X do Y" has exactly one answer. 3. Audit every denied and allowed privileged action. Inconsistent enforcement in one forgotten endpoint is how tenant B's refund button ends up on tenant A's screen — and how interviews fail.

PART 10 — ORDER FLOW

One Order, Every Step, Correctly

The heart of the business: customer wants things, we promise stock, we take money, we confirm. Label each step — immediate, background, or event — and the architecture writes itself.

  1. IMMEDIATECustomer / sales user creates orderLines, quantities, customer, prices pulled from cached master data. Idempotency key from the client guards double-submits.
  2. VALIDATEValidatePermissions (order.create), company settings (price lists, credit limit), stock availability, business rules. Fails fast with clear reasons.
  3. SAVESave order (state: Draft/Confirmed)One database transaction: order + lines + audit entry. The number is assigned from the company's own numbering sequence.
  4. RESERVEReserve inventoryStock moves from available to reserved for these lines — atomically, same transaction (Part 11). If reservation fails (someone else got it), the order fails before any money moves.
  5. PAYMENTPayment (if the company sells prepay)Payment intent with idempotency key to the provider; or credit-sale: customer balance checked against credit limit, payable later. Either way the charge is recorded, not assumed.
  6. CONFIRMOrder confirmed (state: Confirmed)Reservation becomes committed; stock deducted; order state advanced in one guarded transition. The customer's promise is now on our books.
  7. EVENTOrderConfirmed event publishedOne durable event — everything below hangs off it without the order transaction waiting.
  8. BACKGROUNDNotificationConfirmation email/SMS to customer, task to warehouse, low-stock check — queued, retried, non-blocking.
  9. BACKGROUNDReporting + audit syncRead model aggregates update (dashboard numbers within seconds), audit trail finalized, integrations (accounting, shipping) queued with retry/idempotency.
CATEGORY STEPS WHY
Immediate (user waits) Create, validate, save, reserve, confirm; payment capture for prepay. A salesperson's throughput depends on it; keep it under ~1 second with hot indexes and cached masters.
Background (may lag seconds) Notifications, read-model updates, audit sync, integrations, export generation. None of these blocks a promise to the customer; each must survive failure with retry.
Events (the join points) OrderCreated, OrderConfirmed, PaymentCompleted, InventoryReserved/Released. Let ten consumers (dashboard, email, warehouse, accounting, shipping, audit, analytics, AI forecasting…) react without the order code knowing them.
CANCELLATIONS AND UPDATES FIT THE SAME SHAPE

Cancel: guarded state transition (only from Confirmed-before-fulfillment) + stock release (atomic) + refund flow if paid (idempotent) + events. Update: only before confirmation; after, it's a new version or a credit note. The rule: every order action is a guarded state transition that moves stock and money in the same transaction where they touch each other.

PART 11 — INVENTORY DESIGN

The Number Must Never Go Negative — For Any Company

Inventory is the platform's highest-stakes shared resource: overselling stock means a broken promise to a customer, a warehouse in chaos, and a company that loses trust in the whole system.

The stock picture per product/warehouse

ON-HAND QUANTITYWhat physically exists. Only stock movements (receipt, sale, adjustment, transfer) change it.
RESERVED QUANTITYPromised but not yet committed — confirmed, unfulfilled orders. Available = on-hand − reserved.
MOVEMENT TYPESStock in (purchase receipt), stock out (sale fulfillment), adjustment (count corrections — always with a reason), transfer (between warehouses — two legs, one document).
STOCK LEDGERAppend-only movement history: who, what, why, when, referencing the order/purchase. Current quantity is the fold of the ledger — auditable forever.

The killer question: two users sell the last 5 at the same time

Both see 5 available. Both hit save. Naive code sells 10 of 5. How does the architect prevent it?

  1. Atomic reservation, not "check then write"Atomic means indivisible — no observer sees it halfway. The reservation is one operation: "reduce available by N only if available ≥ N". The database executes it as a single guarded update; one sale succeeds, the other gets a clean "insufficient stock" — not a negative number.
  2. Same transaction as the orderOrder lines and the reservation commit together. If either fails, both roll back — you can never have a confirmed order with unreserved stock or a reservation with no order.
  3. Optimistic concurrency where rows are hotEach stock row carries a version; an update must present the version it read. Losers retry with fresh data (and may then fail on the quantity check — which is correct behavior). Optimistic = check at write time; pessimistic = lock the row first. For typical order rates, optimistic is faster and simpler; if one hot product sees thousands of conflicts, that row may earn a short lock.
  4. Reservation with expiryA reservation is a promise with a timer: carts/hold times (e.g. 30 minutes) auto-release stock back to available via scheduled sweeper jobs. No reservation locks stock forever.
  5. Release on cancelCancelling an order atomically releases its reservation — the reverse operation, same guarantees, same transaction discipline.
  6. Adjustments and transfers with guardrailsAdjustments (count corrections) are privileged, reason-coded and audited; transfers commit as two moves (source out + target in) in one transaction so totals never drift.
THE GUARANTEE TO STATE OUT LOUD

"Stock can never go negative, because the only path that reduces it is a single guarded statement inside the same transaction as the order — and every reservation carries an expiry. Conflict is not a bug, it's a retry or a polite 'sold out.'" That's the entire answer to the concurrency question.

PART 12 — READ VS WRITE DESIGN

Two Workloads Wearing One Logo

This platform has two personalities: a write workload (create order, move stock, record payment — small, fast, must be exact) and a read workload (dashboards at 8 a.m., month-end reports, search — big scans, tolerant of slight delay). Fighting for one database is how month-end close freezes a sales team.

WORKLOAD EXAMPLES CHARACTER & HOME
Transaction (writes) Create/update order, reserve stock, record payment, receive purchase. Low volume (5k RPS total), latency-critical, correctness-critical. Lives in the transactional database, hot indexes, short transactions.
Read-heavy Dashboards, order history pages, product/customer search, analytics, heavy reports. High volume spikes, scan-heavy, seconds-old data acceptable. Served by cache → read replicas → dedicated read model, in that order.

A graduated strategy — use the cheapest layer that works

  1. Layer 0 — Same database is enough for:Single-company history pages, moderate dashboards, moderate company sizes. Don't build a read model until reads demonstrably hurt the writer.
  2. Layer 1 — Cache for stable hot reads:Company settings, permissions, product and customer masters, dashboard aggregates. Cheap, fast, TTL-managed (Part 16).
  3. Layer 2 — Read replicas for growing read volume:History, dashboards, and search feeds read replicas, keeping the primary free for orders. Replication lag (seconds) is acceptable for these views.
  4. Layer 3 — Dedicated reporting read model:When month-end report load or dashboard growth threatens the primary: events from the transactional core feed a read model database (or warehouse) shaped exactly like the reports — per-company aggregates, time series, denormalized for scans. Reports query their store; the writer never feels it.
  5. When reporting must be separated — the triggers:report jobs locking tables, dashboard p95 climbing during closes, primary CPU dominated by SELECTs, or queries running minutes over years of history. Any one of these is the go-signal.
THE SENTENCE FOR THE BOARD

"We serve reads from cache, then replicas, then a dedicated read model — in that order of cost. The transactional database is sacred ground for the day's business; everything analytical gets its own room."

PART 13 — EVENT-DRIVEN DESIGN

What Happened, Once, For Everyone Who Cares

An event is an immutable announcement: "X happened". A message broker is the post office that carries events to any number of consumers (subscribers). The order transaction commits its own changes, then publishes; consumers react independently. This is how a single order click serves ten systems without the order code knowing any of them.

EVENT PUBLISHER CONSUMERS IMMEDIATE? IF PROCESSING FAILS
OrderCreated Order module (on save) Audit, read model, notifications (draft notice), fraud/anomaly checks. No Retry with backoff; order itself already safe in DB.
OrderConfirmed Order module (state transition) Customer confirmation, warehouse task, read model, accounting sync, shipping integration. Warehouse task: soon; rest: no Queued retry; warehouse gets the task from queue state, not memory.
PaymentCompleted Payment module (provider confirmed) Order (mark paid), read model, audit, accounting, notification. Order mark: same tx where possible; rest: no Retry is safe (idempotent consumers keyed by payment id); reconciliation job backstops.
InventoryReserved / Released Inventory module Read model (available stock), low-stock alerts, forecasting data. No Retry; stock truth is in the DB — events are mirrors, never sources.
InventoryUpdated Inventory module (every movement) Read model, stock history views, reorder-point checks, analytics. No Retry + DLQ for poison events; ledger is the truth for replay.
PurchaseReceived Purchase module (receiving) Inventory (already in same tx for stock-in), supplier balance view, read model, notifications. No Retry; partial receipts emit per-line events with the purchase id for ordering.
NotificationRequested Any module via the notification API Notification module → email/SMS/push providers. No Retry with backoff; after max attempts, delivery log + alert; never blocks the business action.

The complete flow, in one breath

Sales user confirms an order → order module commits (order + reservation in one transaction) → publishes OrderConfirmed (+ InventoryReserved) → broker fans out → read model refreshes the dashboard within seconds → notification module emails the customer and creates the warehouse task → accounting integration posts the entry with retry → audit trail finalizes. If the notification queue is down, the customer's email waits in the queue — the order is still confirmed, paid and on the books. That's the point: the business committed first; everything else catches up.

THE DISCIPLINE RULE

Events are mirrors of committed facts, never the source of truth. A consumer can always rebuild its state from the transactional database + ledger. And every consumer is idempotent: events may arrive twice (at-least-once delivery), so consumers key on the business id and skip what they've done. Those two rules make event-driven safe at enterprise scale.

Indexing, partitioning, replication, backup

  • Partitioning: by time for history/audit (drop or offload old partitions); tenant-based separation exists via the premium-path databases (Part 8) — and, if one mega-tenant ever dominates, partitioning its data onto its own instance.   • Replication: primary → read replicas (dashboard/history fallbacks, search feeds, report pre-aggregation); primary → standby (failover); primary → remote replica (disaster, Part 25).
  • Backup: scheduled snapshots + continuous log/WAL backup; restore drills quarterly (a backup you've never restored is a rumor); per-tenant export capability for offboarding.
PART 16 — CACHING

Cache the Slow-Change, Never the Truth

A cache is a fast, small, throwaway copy in front of slow storage. At 5,000 RPS a decent cache absorbs most reads — but every cached value is a promise that being a little stale is acceptable.

WHAT TO CACHE WHY + TYPICAL LIFETIME
Company settings & configuration Read on almost every request; changes rarely. Minutes TTL + push invalidation when admin saves.
User permissions (per token) Checked on every action; TTL matched to token life, invalidated on role change (instant for removals).
Product & customer masters (per company) Order screen reads them constantly; minutes TTL; invalidation on master edits.
Dashboard aggregates Precomputed per company, refreshed by the read model every few minutes — the dashboard reads a number, not a scan.
Price lists / tax tables Reference data, low churn; invalidate on change event.

What must NEVER be cache-only truth

  • Stock quantities — the order path reads the authoritative row (hot index is enough); stale "5 available" becomes oversold "5 available".
  • Order status and payment status — decisions (can I cancel? is it paid?) read the durable record.
  • Credit limits and balances — money-adjacent decisions never trust a seconds-old copy.
  • User deactivation / permission removals — negative events invalidate immediately; the stale-allowed rule applies to reads, never to revocation.

The four cache disciplines

LIFETIME (TTL)Every key has an expiry matched to how long staleness is acceptable — settings: minutes; dashboard: 3–5 min; permissions: token life. No TTL = stale forever.
INVALIDATIONChange events clear the affected keys (save product → drop its cached copy). Writes invalidate; readers refill.
CACHE FAILUREIf the cache dies, reads fall through to the database — throttled and shed if needed, so a cache outage becomes slowness, not an outage. The system must have been load-tested with a cold cache.
STALE DATADecide per key: 4-minute-old dashboard is a feature; 4-minute-old stock quantity is an incident. Keys that touch stock, money or access get short TTLs and change-driven invalidation.
KEEP CACHE IN ITS LANE

Cache is a supporting cast: it makes 5,000 RPS comfortable and dashboards instant. It is not the architecture. If your answer to "it's slow" is "cache it" more than twice in one interview, you've missed the read-model and indexing answers.

PART 18 — BACKGROUND PROCESSING

The Work That Can Wait Should Wait — Politely

Anything that doesn't answer the user's immediate question belongs in a queue consumed by workers — separate processes that pull jobs, run them with retry, and report metrics. The user's click finishes in a second; the rest of the world catches up.

TASK TRIGGER WHY IT MUST NOT BLOCK THE MAIN REQUEST
Notifications (email/SMS/push) Business events (order confirmed, low stock). Mail/SMS vendors are slow and fail often; an order must not wait on a vendor's morning.
Report generation & exports User request for a heavy report; scheduled month-end jobs. Minutes-long scans; results delivered as a downloadable file or a link, not held open in a browser request.
Data synchronization (integrations) Events → accounting, shipping, external ERP. External systems have their own uptime; sync retries must never re-open the business transaction.
Import / export (bulk) User uploads product/customer files; export requests. Big, multi-stage, resumable jobs with progress; a 50,000-row import is a job, not an HTTP request.
Scheduled jobs Timers: reservation expiry sweeps, credit limit checks, reminder emails, reconciliation. Continuous housekeeping that must run unattended and idempotently.
Long-running calculations Stock valuation, forecast inputs, analytics refresh, read-model rebuilds. Resource-heavy; scheduled off-peak where possible, always with their own quotas.

Retry and failure handling for workers

  • Retry with backoff + jitter — 1s, 2s, 4s… so a struggling dependency recovers instead of being hammered (retry storms make failures worse, not better).
  • Bounded attempts, then dead-letter — after N failures the job moves to a dead-letter queue (quarantine): alerted, inspectable, replayable after the cause is fixed. Nothing silently dies.
  • Idempotent jobs — every job keyed by business id (order id, payment id); a redelivered job skips what it already did. At-least-once delivery + idempotent workers = effectively-once effect.
  • Progress & heartbeat — long jobs report progress and renew a lease; a crashed worker's job is picked up by another (no zombie jobs, no double execution of destructive work).
  • Concurrent execution with quotas — per-tenant and global concurrency limits so one company's 200 export requests don't starve the other nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine.
PART 19 — FILE MANAGEMENT

Bytes Live in Files, Facts Live in Rows

Product images, customer documents, invoices, import files, exported reports — five kinds of files, one principle: the database stores small metadata (name, owner, size, type, status, when); the bytes live in object storage behind secure, tenant-scoped access.

CONCERN DESIGN ANSWER
Where bytes live Object storage (a file store you address by key), organized per-tenant, with a CDN in front for images/exports (many small reads, mostly unchanged).
What the DB holds File metadata + ownership (TenantId!), access level, checksum, size, upload state, expiry. A query never returns bytes.
Secure access Users never get raw storage paths. The file service checks (user's company = file's company) and permission, then issues a short-lived signed URL (minutes). A leaked link dies on expiry; a cross-tenant request is a logged security event.
Backup Storage-level versioning + replication; metadata in the main backup. Restore drills cover files too (an invoice file without its row is a mystery).
Large files Resumable/chunked uploads; size limits per kind (imports vs images); streaming — never loaded fully into a worker's memory; processing (viral scan, thumbnail) is a background job.
Lifecycle Uploads without a referencing record within TTL are garbage-collected; deleted business records trigger file deletion per retention policy; disappearing policies for imports can be enforced by storage lifecycle rules.
THE ONE LINE

"Metadata in the transactional database, bytes in object storage, access via signed tenant-scoped URLs, cleanup by lifecycle jobs." Files are the boring cousin of stock — get the ownership and cleanup right and they never appear in an incident.

PART 20 — EXTERNAL INTEGRATIONS

The Outside World Is Always Unreliable — Design as If It's Hostile

Payment provider, email, SMS, accounting system, shipping — each is another company's product with its own outages, rate limits and bugs. The core system must complete its own business first and talk to the outside politely, with receipts.

INTEGRATION SYNC OR ASYNC FAILURE STORY
Payment provider Synchronous in the order flow (prepay); provider callbacks arrive async. Timeout = "unknown", not "failed": the payment stays pending, idempotency key reused on any retry, status polled/callback reconciled. A provider outage queues payments (order can still be on credit if policy allows).
Email provider Async (queued events). Vendor down → emails queue with retry and backoff; delivery log records each attempt; bulk sends are paced.
SMS provider Async; per-country vendors possible. Same as email, plus cost guardrails (rate limits, dedupe) — SMS is billed per message.
Accounting system Async sync from events (their API is slow; their uptime is theirs). Out-of-order-safe sync keyed by document id; nightly reconciliation of posted entries vs orders/payments; gap report if a day is missing.
Shipping system Async after order confirmation. Shipping failure never blocks the order; shipping status is a pending state with retry; customer sees "label preparing", not an error.

The five rules for every external call

  1. Timeouts on everythingEvery call has a bounded wait. A provider that doesn't answer in 3 seconds is, architecturally, dead — release the thread, don't hold it.
  2. Retries with backoff + jitter, boundedTransient failures get polite retries; permanent failures (4xx, invalid data) don't. Bounded attempts, then dead-letter + alert.
  3. Idempotency keys on every mutating callSame request sent twice = same effect once. The provider sees our key, not a new charge or a second label.
  4. Circuit breakersWhen a provider is clearly down, stop calling it (open the circuit) for a cooling period and fail fast — protecting our own threads and the provider's recovery. Auto half-open probes restore traffic gradually.
  5. Monitoring + reconciliationPer-integration metrics: success rate, latency, queue depth, failure types. Scheduled reconciliation compares our records to the provider's (settled payments, posted entries) — the backstop that catches the 0.1% no retry can fix.
PROTECTING THE CORE

The core system stays protected because its promises never depend on the outside completing: the order commits, the stock moves, the money is recorded as pending — and integrations catch up from events and queues. A down payment provider degrades the payment experience; it does not stop the platform from being the platform.

PART 21 — SECURITY ARCHITECTURE

How We Make Sure Company A Never Reads Company B

The single most damaging bug in a multi-tenant system is a cross-tenant leak. So it gets five independent layers — each one works alone, and together they make a leak a multi-system failure, not a forgotten filter. The rest of security supports that same idea: protect identity, boundaries, data, and evidence.

The five layers of tenant isolation

  1. Identity layer — tenant travels with the tokenAuthentication binds the user to exactly one company; the gateway resolves it into the request context. A client cannot declare "I am Company B" — the token is the only voice on tenant identity.
  2. Data-access layer — mandatory tenant filterThe data layer appends the tenant to every query as framework behavior (tenant-scoped context or query filters); composite keys and unique constraints include TenantId. "Forgot the WHERE clause" becomes structurally impossible in the normal path.
  3. Storage layer — physical escape hatchesDefault shared storage is the cheap case; regulated or premium tenants get dedicated databases where a SQL bug, a wrong connection string or an operator mistake cannot reach their neighbors at all.
  4. Verification layer — automated cross-tenant testsA permanent test suite: every read/write endpoint exercised with Company A's user against Company B's ids. Any success fails the build. Isolation is a tested property, not a policy PDF.
  5. Observability layer — detection of the impossibleAlerts on cross-tenant access attempts, anomalous query patterns (one connection scanning many tenants), bulk-export spikes and failed-permission storms. Even a bug that passes layers 1–4 gets caught by 5 before customers notice.

The rest of the security architecture

AUTHENTICATIONStrong credential policies, failed-login throttling per user/IP, session revocation, optional SSO for larger companies.
AUTHORIZATIONRole → permissions enforced server-side at every operation boundary, per tenant; platform admin on a separate audited path.
ENCRYPTION IN TRANSITTLS everywhere — client to gateway, gateway to services, service to database, every external API call.
ENCRYPTION AT RESTDatabases and storage encrypted; per-tenant key options for premium/regulated tenants; keys rotated on schedule.
SECRETSProvider keys, DB credentials in a secrets store — never in code or config files; least-privilege service identities; rotation on departure events.
API PROTECTION + RATE LIMITINGAuthenticated-only public surface, strict input validation, rate limits per user and per company (abuse isolation), request-size caps, versioned API so old clients can't call deprecated risky paths.
SECURE FILE ACCESSTenant-scoped signed URLs (Part 19); no public buckets; upload scanning; export actions audited.
AUDIT & DATA PROTECTIONImmutable audit trail (who did what, when, from where); data access reviews; right-to-delete/offboarding exports; retention schedules; PII minimization in logs (log the id, not the email).
THE INTERVIEW ANSWER, COMPRESSED

"Tenant identity lives in the token; the data layer can't be asked to skip the filter; premium tenants get their own storage; a test suite tries to leak every build; and monitoring screams if the impossible happens. Five layers, so no single forgotten line of code is a breach."

13 · API server failure Some requests fail; others land on healthy servers; state unaffected. Load balancer health checks route around the dead node; autoscaler replaces it. Auto-replacement; in-flight requests retried by clients (idempotent); no sticky state in API tier. Stateless API tier, health checks, autoscaling, zero-config deployments.
14 · Region failure All users lose the platform until failover; data at risk only within the RPO. DNS/anycast shifts to DR region; read-replicas promoted; queues and events drained after. Restore from most recent replica + continuous log replay; verify per-tenant data; cut over gradually; post-mortem. Regional DR (Part 25), replication, tested restore, declared RTO/RPO, chaos drills.
15 · Bad event processing (poison event / buggy consumer) One consumer loop-crashes on a malformed event; its queue stalls; others fine. Consumer dead-letters the poison after N attempts; queue resumes; alert names the event id and consumer. Fix consumer or data; replay the DLQ; verify downstream state (rebuild if the consumer was a read-model builder). Schemas + validation at publish, DLQ + replay tooling, per-consumer lag alerts, idempotent rebuilds.
THE PATTERN IN EVERY ROW

Detect (heartbeats, health checks, lag metrics) → contain (statelessness, queues, breakers, per-tenant limits) → degrade gracefully (honest states, "as of" data, queued beats failed) → recover (failover, replay, reconciliation, rebuild) → prevent (redundancy, idempotency, drills). Say the column headers and walk them — interviewers notice.

PART 23 — SCALING

Every Lane Scales On Its Own Curve

Horizontal scaling = adding more identical servers behind a load balancer. Each layer of this platform scales differently — by a different metric, at a different pace, on a different budget.

COMPONENT HOW IT SCALES TRIGGER TO ADD MORE
API layer Stateless instances, autoscale on CPU and queue length. p95 latency, CPU, 5xx rate.
Business modules With the API tier (modular monolith deploys as one unit); hot modules become extraction candidates, not more boxes of everything. Module-specific latency/CPU profiling.
Transactional database Scale up first (it's a writer); then read replicas; then partitioning — by time (history), and per-tenant isolation for the giants. Write latency, lock waits, CPU; replica lag for reads.
Reporting / read model Own instances scale independently; scheduled reports run as queued jobs with concurrency limits. Report queue age, dashboard p95, close-week load.
Cache Add cache nodes / memory; keys sharded; per-tenant quotas so one tenant can't evict everyone's hot keys. Hit ratio drop, memory pressure, eviction rates.
Message processing Add consumer instances per queue; per-tenant concurrency quotas; autoscale on lag. Queue depth, consumer lag, DLQ growth.
File storage / CDN Object storage scales itself; CDN absorbs reads; workers scale on job queue. Upload queue, egress cost, latency.

Independent scaling, and why it matters here

  • Dashboards spike at 8 a.m. in every timezone — cache and read replicas absorb it without the order path scaling a single server.
  • Month-end close spikes reports for two weeks a year — the reporting lane scales up, does its job, scales back. The core doesn't carry idle month-end capacity 10 months.
  • A mega-tenant's growth is its own curve — tenant-based scaling (premium database path, dedicated worker quotas) means Company Giant's 10× doesn't force the whole platform to re-size.
THE SINGLE VERY LARGE COMPANY PROBLEM

In a shared table design, one company with 500,000 SKUs, 1,000 daily orders and 200 users can become a hot index region, a cache evictor, and a worker-pool hog — and its growth drags every other company's percentiles. The architecture answers in three moves: per-tenant limits and fair queues (immediate), per-tenant metrics that see it early (detection), and the premium database path (structural exit). This is why Part 8 chose the hybrid model — scale and isolation share the same escape hatch.

PART 24 — THE NOISY TENANT PROBLEM

"One Company Suddenly Did 10× Normal Traffic"

Multi-tenancy's original sin, if unmanaged: one tenant's surge becomes every tenant's outage. A botched export script, a marketing push, an aggressive integration, or genuine growth — the platform must tell them apart while protecting everyone.

The defense stack

  1. Per-tenant rate limits (the first wall)Each company gets a budget (requests/sec, concurrent sessions, bulk operations) from its plan. Excess is shed for that company only, with honest "queued / slow down" responses — the other nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine never notice.
  2. Resource isolation (the structural wall)Worker pools and queue consumers run with per-tenant concurrency quotas (no one company can occupy all workers); cache keys carry tenant budgets; long report jobs get a per-tenant concurrency cap (e.g. 3 at a time).
  3. Separate queues for bulk workBulk operations (imports, exports, syncs) never ride the request path — they ride dedicated queues with their own quotas, so bulk storms are structurally separated from interactive traffic.
  4. Monitoring that sees tenantsPer-tenant dashboards: RPS, error rate, queue depth, export volume. Alerts on "tenant X at 5× its 30-day baseline" — the anomaly caught in minutes, not when a customer complains.
  5. Tiered responseLegit surge → scale out + temporary limit raise (with billing honesty: you're on the Enterprise plan now). Chronic load → premium database path (Part 8 hybrid). Abuse/attack → throttle, investigate, ban. The response differs by cause; the detection is the same metric.
WHY THIS EXISTS AT ALL

In single-tenant systems, "the customer has a big month" is a business conversation. In shared systems it's an availability incident for everyone else — so fairness isn't politeness, it's architecture: limits, quotas, separate lanes, per-tenant telemetry, and a contractual path to physical isolation when one tenant outgrows the shared floor.

PART 25 — DISASTER RECOVERY

The Fire Plan, Measured in Minutes and Money

Two honest numbers define disaster recovery (DR), and the architect states both out loud — they are business agreements, not technical trivia:

RPO — RECOVERY POINT OBJECTIVE"How much data can we lose?" The time gap between the last safe copy and the disaster. Seconds (continuous replication) costs far more than hours (nightly backup).
RTO — RECOVERY TIME OBJECTIVE"How long can we be down?" The time from declaration to service. Minutes (automated failover) costs more than hours (manual restore).

A realistic recovery strategy for this platform

  1. Backups, testedScheduled snapshots + continuous log (WAL) backup for the transactional database; storage snapshots for files; read-model rebuildable (not backup-worthy — it's a copy). A backup never restored in a drill is a rumor; drill quarterly on a copy, measure actual RTO.
  2. Replication for availabilityMulti-AZ primary/standby for the database (automatic failover, RPO ≈ seconds, RTO ≈ minutes) handles the common disaster: one data center dies.
  3. Regional DR for the big oneA warm/standby region with async replica + latest backups. Regional outage (whole zone/cloud region down) → promote replica, cut DNS, verify, drain events. Declared budget: RPO minutes, RTO hours — agreed with the business because true zero-loss multi-region is a 5× cost increase for a region-wide event.
  4. Stateless edges make recovery cheapAPI and workers rebuild from configuration in minutes; the only state that matters lives in databases, broker, and object storage — all covered by replication/backup. Fewer stateful things = shorter RTO by design.
  5. Runbooks and rehearsalsWritten, owned, tested failover runbooks (who declares, what's checked, how data integrity is verified per-tenant, how clients reconnect with idempotent retries). Chaos-style drills twice a year.
THE TRADE-OFF TRIANGLE MOVING IT
Cost Every order of magnitude of RPO/RTO improvement roughly multiplies infrastructure and operational cost (synchronous replication, standby regions, more DR engineering).
Recovery speed Automation buys minutes; automation you've never tested buys hope. Speed requires rehearsals, which cost engineering time.
Complexity Active-active multi-region is the complexity ceiling — and at this platform's scale it's over-buying. Prefer active-passive with fast promotion: one writer, one source of truth, cheaper and simpler, still within the agreed RTO.
THE SENTENCE FOR MANAGEMENT

"We lose at most N minutes of data and recover within M hours, for the cost of C — and we can buy each of those three numbers down further, on request, at a price we'll quote." DR is a product with a price list. That's how an architect sells it.

PART 26 — OBSERVABILITY

If You Can't See It, You Don't Operate It

Three lenses on one system: logs say what happened; metrics say how much and how often; traces say where a request spent its time. A platform this size is run from these three — especially per-tenant, which is the multi-tenant superpower no single-tenant system needs.

WHAT WE MEASURE WHY IT MATTERS ALERT WHEN…
API response time (p50/p95/p99) The user's felt experience; the first thing to move before anything breaks. p95 over threshold for 5+ minutes, or 5xx rate above baseline.
Error rate per endpoint Finds the broken path before customers file ten tickets. Spike vs trailing baseline — not absolute numbers (traffic explains some errors).
Database load Writer health: CPU, lock waits, slow queries, replica lag. Lock-wait spike (concurrency trouble), replica lag beyond dashboard tolerance, write latency up.
Cache failures / hit ratio A dying cache announces itself in hit ratio before the DB screams. Hit ratio drop, node loss, memory pressure / evictions climbing.
Queue size & consumer lag The early-warning system for background work (reports, notifications, syncs). Age of oldest message past tolerance; DLQ growth (anything there is an incident).
Worker failures Crash-looping workers and poison jobs. Restart count per interval, repeated job failure count, dead-letter arrivals.
Tenant traffic (per company) The noisy-tenant radar; also capacity planning and billing evidence. Tenant at N× its 30-day baseline; top-tenant share of total RPS climbing.
Payment failures Money-path health: provider errors, timeouts, pending-pile age. Provider error rate up, PENDING payments aging past threshold (reconciliation trigger).
Inventory anomalies Stock correctness tripwire: reservation pile-up, negative-stock attempts, conflict rate. Any negative-stock attempt (impossible by design → investigate immediately), high reserve-conflict rate on hot products.
Security signals Cross-tenant attempts, failed-permission storms, export spikes, auth anomalies. Any cross-tenant access attempt (even "impossible" ones), bulk-export anomalies.
TRACING'S JOB

A slow order creation is either the database, the cache, the payment call, or the queue — and a trace tells you which hop ate the 800ms. In a modular monolith, tracing per-module spans gives you this without services; in future microservices it becomes the glue. Invest early: untraced systems are debugged by guessing, and guessing is how incidents age.

PART 28 — COST THINKING

The Bill Is Part of the Architecture

An architect owns two budgets: the build and the run. The cost centers here, then the levers, then the trap — where a technically better architecture is a financially worse one.

COST AREA WHAT DRIVES IT
Compute (API/workers) Always-on request handling + autoscaling headroom + background fleets.
Database HA primary, replicas, storage growth (orders/history/audit), IOPS.
Cache Replicated memory for masters/settings/aggregates.
Message broker Clustered, durable queues for 10k tenants' events and jobs.
Storage + CDN Objects (files, exports), cold history tiers, egress for downloads/CDN.
Network Ingress/egress volumes, cross-AZ traffic (replication + service calls).
Monitoring High-cardinality per-tenant metrics, log volume, tracing at 5k RPS.
Backup + DR Snapshot frequency, retention, standby capacity, replication bandwidth.
External providers Per-transaction payments, per-message email/SMS, per-query search APIs.

How an architect controls the bill

  • Size for peak with burst, not for permanent over-provisioning: autoscale to 5k RPS at the hour it happens, run lean at 2 a.m. Multi-AZ redundancy is worth it; 3× idle headroom is not.
  • Tier data by age: hot partitions for ~12–24 months, compressed cold storage after; audit and history prune/archive per retention policy. The database bill follows the data bill.
  • Cache what's stable: settings/masters/permissions caching keeps the DB right-sized for writes, not reads.
  • Read replicas before bigger primaries: reads scale sideways cheaply; the writer stays small.
  • Per-tenant quotas are cost controls: unlimited bulk exports by one tenant is another tenant's infrastructure bill. Quotas protect the shared bill.
  • Log and metric discipline: per-tenant metrics are powerful but expensive — aggregate aggressively, retain hot detail for days, sample traces.
  • Provider economics: batch email where possible, prefer email over SMS (cost differential is 10–100×), cache search results, monitor per-tenant provider spend.
  • One deployable: the modular monolith's quiet cost win — one pipeline, one metric set, one on-call, versus N× for N services.
  • Unit economics per company: cost per tenant per month, tracked. The premium path must be priced to pay for itself — isolation is a product feature with a margin line.
WHERE "TECHNICALLY BETTER" BECAME "FINANCIALLY WORSE"
  • Full microservices: 13 services × (infra + tracing + alerts + on-call) to serve 5k RPS — a 5× run-cost increase for a scale problem the system doesn't have. Better architecture, wrong answer.
  • Database per company: maximum isolation, 10,000× backup/upgrade/monitoring cost — buying a 1% probability scenario at 100% of the budget.
  • Active-active multi-region: near-zero downtime for regional disasters the business priced at "hours is fine" — split-brain engineering on a platform that needed a read model more.
  • Instant live dashboards on the TX DB: "better" numbers, "worse" everything else — month-end freezes, oversold stock from write starvation, and a database sized for reports instead of orders.

The pattern: each one bought resilience or immediacy the business didn't price, at a cost the business didn't authorize.
