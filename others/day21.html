PART 1 — THE OTHER HALF OF THE JOB

Why Architecture Communication Matters

A design lives or dies on whether the people around it understand it. An architecture nobody understands is an architecture nobody will fund, build correctly, or defend when it is questioned.

Why a good design can still fail

  • It is never approved. The CTO cannot see the business case, so budget goes elsewhere.
  • It is built wrong. Developers did not understand the boundaries, so they cut through them.
  • It is blamed unfairly. When the first incident happens, nobody remembers why the trade-off was made, so the design gets the blame.
  • It is replaced. A louder, simpler-sounding idea wins the room, even if it is worse.

The six audiences and what each one actually wants

AUDIENCE WHAT THEY REALLY WANT TO KNOW HOW YOU CHANGE THE DEPTH (NOT THE DECISION)
Developer "How does it work? What do I build? Where are the boundaries? What must I not do?" Show the components, the contracts between modules, the data each one owns, the async paths. Concrete and precise.
Technical Lead "Will this hold up? Where will it break? How do we test and deploy it?" Focus on boundaries, failure modes, observability, deployment shape. Discuss alternatives briefly.
Engineering Manager "Can my team deliver and support this? How long? What skills do we need? What is the on-call burden?" Delivery phases, team fit, operational load, what is new vs known. Less protocol talk, more people and time.
Product Manager "What does the user get? What can we ship first? What becomes possible later?" User-visible outcomes: speed, reliability, features enabled or delayed. Hide the plumbing.
CTO "Is this the right bet? What is the risk, the cost, the scale ceiling, and the exit if we are wrong?" Decision, alternatives rejected, risk, cost over time, growth path, review trigger. One diagram, few words.
Business stakeholder "Will it make or save money? Will customers be happier? What could go wrong and what will it cost us?" Money, customers, time, risk. No component names unless they carry business meaning ("a backup copy", not "a read replica").
THE RULE

Change the words, never the decision. If you tell the CTO one thing and the developers another, you have two architectures and zero trust. The same design — a queue for notifications — is "an outbox with retries" to a developer, "a buffer so checkout never waits on email" to a manager, and "customers get their receipt even if the email provider has a bad day" to the business.

PART 2 — THE STRUCTURE

The 10-Minute Architecture Presentation

Ten minutes, ten beats. Learn this shape once and you can present any architecture — in a boardroom or an interview — without notes.

  1. Minute 1 — Business problemWhat hurts today, in the business's own units: lost orders, slow month-end, unhappy customers, cost. One or two sentences. No technology yet.SAY: "Today, when reports run, checkout slows down and we lose orders. That is the problem we are solving."
  2. Minute 2 — RequirementsWhat the system must do: create orders, manage stock, take payment, show dashboards, produce reports. Name the numbers: companies, users, orders per day.SAY: "10,000 companies, 1 million users, 50,000 orders a day, growing 5× in 18 months."
  3. Minute 3 — Important non-functional requirementsThe qualities that shape the design: how fast, how available, how secure, how consistent. Say which ones are gates.SAY: "Checkout under half a second, 99.9% uptime, strict company-to-company data isolation, payments always correct."
  4. Minute 4 — High-level architectureOne diagram, five to eight boxes. Name each box in one phrase. No arrows explained yet — just what exists.SAY: "Clients, an API, a modular application with these areas, one transactional database, a read copy for reports, a cache, a queue, and the payment provider."
  5. Minute 5 — Main business flowWalk one request end to end: place an order. Which box does what, in order. This is where the design becomes real.SAY: "A user places an order → API → Order module checks stock → Payment module charges → order saved → events queued for email and warehouse."
  6. Minute 6 — Data flowWhere data lives, where copies are, how they stay in sync, what is stale and by how much.SAY: "Truth is the main database. Reports read a copy that is seconds behind. The cache holds product data for one minute."
  7. Minute 7 — Scalability and performanceHow it grows: what you add at 2×, 5×, 10×. Where the first bottleneck is and what you do about it.SAY: "App servers scale sideways. Reads scale with more copies and cache. The write database is the first ceiling — partitioning is the next step."
  8. Minute 8 — Security and reliabilityTenant isolation, authentication, secrets, audit. Then: what happens when each major piece fails, and how you know.SAY: "Every query is filtered by company. If the cache dies, we fall back to the database with throttling. If the queue is down, messages wait in an outbox."
  9. Minute 9 — Trade-offs and rejected optionsThe two or three real decisions. What you chose, what you rejected, what the rejected option did better, and why it lost here.SAY: "We considered microservices. They would give independent releases, but with one DevOps engineer and 150 requests a second, the cost outweighs the benefit today."
  10. Minute 10 — Risks, cost, conclusionTop risks with mitigations. Rough monthly cost now and at growth. One-sentence recommendation and the trigger to revisit.SAY: "About $1,700 a month now, $4–5K at 5×. Biggest risk is boundary erosion — we enforce it in the build. I recommend we proceed, and review in 12 months or at 50 developers."
IF YOU ONLY HAVE 5 MINUTES

Keep minutes 1, 4, 5, 9 and 10: problem, diagram, main flow, trade-offs, conclusion. Everything else can be answered in questions.

PART 3 — THE FIRST 30 SECONDS

How to Start an Architecture Presentation

The opening tells the room whether to lean in or check their phone. Five kinds of opening, each shown three ways.

1. Open with the business problem

WEAK"So, this is the architecture diagram. On the left we have the clients…"
BETTER"The problem we are solving is that the current system is slow and hard to change."
ARCHITECT-LEVEL"Every month-end, reporting slows checkout to the point where we lose orders — last quarter that was roughly $80K. The design I am about to show removes that conflict, and sets us up for the 5× growth the business expects."

2. Open with the goal

WEAK"The goal is to make the system better."
BETTER"The goal is faster checkout and more reliable reporting."
ARCHITECT-LEVEL"Three goals drove this design: checkout under half a second even during reports, 99.9% uptime in business hours, and the ability to triple traffic without a rewrite. Everything you will see traces back to one of those three."

3. Open with the decision

WEAK"We decided to use a modular monolith with Redis and RabbitMQ."
BETTER"We chose a modular monolith instead of microservices because our team is small."
ARCHITECT-LEVEL"The headline decision is to keep one deployable application with strict internal boundaries, rather than split into services. I will show you why that is the right call for a 20-person team today, and how the design lets us split later if the business needs it."

4. Open with the constraints

WEAK"We have some limitations."
BETTER"We have a small DevOps team and a limited budget, so we kept things simple."
ARCHITECT-LEVEL"Three constraints shaped this: one DevOps engineer, a run budget under $5K a month, and a five-month deadline. Any design that ignores those is a design we cannot ship — so I treated them as hard boundaries, not preferences."

5. Open with what you will cover

WEAK"I'll go through the slides and then take questions."
BETTER"I'll cover the problem, the architecture, and the trade-offs."
ARCHITECT-LEVEL"In ten minutes I'll cover the problem, the three requirements that matter most, the architecture and one order flowing through it, the two decisions I want you to challenge, and the cost and risks. Stop me at any point — the decisions are more useful than the slides."
WHAT MAKES THE ARCHITECT-LEVEL OPENINGS WORK

They contain a number, a consequence, and a promise. A number makes it real. A consequence makes it matter. A promise tells the room what they will get for their attention.

PART 4 — READING THE PICTURE

Explaining Architecture Diagrams

A diagram is not self-explanatory. You are the narrator. Walk it the way a request travels: left to right, top to bottom, start to finish.

FIG. 21.1 — THE SHAPE YOU WALK, LEFT TO RIGHT

The seven things to say, in order

  1. Where the request starts. "A user on the mobile app taps Place Order."
  2. How it moves. "It reaches the API, which checks who they are and which company they belong to."
  3. Which component does what. "The Order area validates, asks Inventory to hold stock, asks Payment to charge."
  4. Where data is stored. "The order and the stock change are saved together in the main database — that is the source of truth."
  5. Where asynchronous work happens. "Once saved, events go to the broker. Email, warehouse notification and reporting updates happen from there — the user does not wait for them."
  6. Where failures can occur. "Payment provider can be slow — we time out and tell the user. Broker can be down — events sit in an outbox until it returns."
  7. Why each major component exists. One sentence per box: "The broker exists so checkout never waits on email."
DO NOT EXPLAIN EVERY BOX

If your diagram has 25 boxes, your audience will remember none of them. Explain the five to eight that carry a decision. The load balancer, the DNS, the logging agent — they exist, they are not interesting, skip them unless asked. Detail on request, not by default.

PART 5 — THE QUESTION BEHIND EVERY BOX

Explain the "Why"

For every component, be ready for: "Why is this here?" If you cannot answer in two sentences, either you do not understand it or it should not be there.

Why an API Gateway?

WEAK"It's standard for APIs."
STRONG"It handles authentication and routing in one place so every module does not repeat it."
ARCHITECT-LEVEL"It is the one front door. Authentication, per-company rate limits and request logging happen once, there — so a noisy tenant can be throttled without touching business code, and later, if we extract a service, we re-route a path without changing clients."

Why Redis?

WEAK"Redis is fast."
STRONG"It caches product and dashboard data so the database is not hit on every read."
ARCHITECT-LEVEL"Product reads outnumber writes about 500 to 1 and the data changes a few times an hour. A one-minute cache removes roughly 90% of that load from the database for about $150 a month. We accept a briefly stale price and we have a fallback with throttling if the cache disappears."

Why a message broker?

WEAK"For decoupling."
STRONG"So checkout does not wait for email and warehouse updates."
ARCHITECT-LEVEL"Three things after checkout do not need to block the user: email, warehouse sync and report refresh. Putting them on a queue gives a 200 ms checkout, automatic retries when the email provider is flaky, and a buffer for Black Friday spikes. The price is idempotent consumers and one more thing to monitor."

Why separate reporting?

WEAK"Reports are heavy."
STRONG"Reports read from a copy so they do not slow down orders."
ARCHITECT-LEVEL"Month-end reports were the direct cause of checkout timeouts — they compete for the same database. Reading from a replica that is seconds behind costs about $300 a month and removes the conflict entirely. Reports can be a minute old; lost orders cannot be recovered."

Why database replication?

WEAK"For high availability."
STRONG"If the primary fails we can switch to the copy, and reads can use it too."
ARCHITECT-LEVEL"The replica does two jobs: it serves reports and dashboards, and it is the failover target if the primary dies — that is how we meet 99.9% without a second region. It is not a backup: mistakes replicate too, so we also keep tested point-in-time backups."

Why a modular monolith?

WEAK"Microservices are too complex."
STRONG"One deployment is easier for a small team, but modules keep the code organised."
ARCHITECT-LEVEL"We get clean ownership boundaries and the option to extract a service later, without paying for distributed transactions, network failures and a platform team we do not have. With 20 developers and 150 requests a second, that is the right trade. At 50 developers or a module needing 10× scale, I would revisit."

Why microservices? (when you did choose them)

WEAK"They scale."
STRONG"Teams can release independently and scale parts separately."
ARCHITECT-LEVEL"Eight teams were blocking each other on one release train, and search needed 40× the capacity of checkout. Independent deployment and targeted scaling were worth the network cost — and we already had a platform team to carry it. Without those two facts I would not have made this choice."

Why asynchronous processing?

WEAK"It's more scalable."
STRONG"The user gets a faster answer and failures can be retried in the background."
ARCHITECT-LEVEL"We split by one question: does the user need this answer now? Payment — yes, so it stays synchronous. Email and stock sync — no, so they go on a queue. The user gets a fast, honest confirmation, and a slow email provider can never fail a sale."
PART 6 — SAYING THE TRADE-OFF

Explaining Trade-offs Out Loud

From Day 20 you know how to make a trade-off. Here is how to say one. The shape is always: we chose → because → we gain → we accept → the risk is.

DECISION SAY IT LIKE THIS
1. Async notifications "We chose asynchronous processing for notifications because email does not need to block order creation. We gain a faster checkout and resilience to a slow provider. We accept that the email may arrive a few seconds later. The risk is a duplicate message on retry, which we handle with idempotency keys."
2. Cache for products "We chose to cache product data for one minute because reads vastly outnumber changes. We gain 90% less database load and faster pages. We accept that a price change takes up to a minute to appear. The risk is a cache outage causing a load spike — we throttle and fall back."
3. Read replica for reports "We chose to run reports from a replica because they were slowing checkout. We gain isolation of the revenue path from analytics. We accept that reports are seconds to a minute behind. The risk is someone routing a transactional read to the replica — we prevent that in the data layer."
4. Modular monolith over microservices "We chose a modular monolith because our team and traffic do not justify distribution. We gain simple deployment, in-memory speed and real transactions. We accept one release train and a shared failure domain. The risk is boundaries eroding — we enforce them with automated tests."
5. Shared database with tenant column "We chose one database with strict tenant filtering because 10,000 separate databases are unmanageable for our team. We gain low cost and simple operations. We accept a noisy-neighbour risk and a weaker isolation story. The risk is a missed filter — we enforce it centrally and test it, and offer dedicated databases to regulated tenants."
6. Synchronous payment "We chose to keep payment synchronous because the user must know if they were charged. We gain a clear, honest result at checkout. We accept that a slow provider slows checkout. The risk is a hung request — we cap it with a timeout and a circuit breaker."
7. Managed PaaS over Kubernetes "We chose a managed platform because we have one DevOps engineer and five deployables. We gain patching, scaling and slots without operating a cluster. We accept less control and some platform limits. The risk is outgrowing it — we revisit at 15–20 services."
8. Buy notifications, build order rules "We chose to buy the notification service and build the order engine because order rules are our advantage and email is a commodity. We gain speed to market and reliability we did not have to earn. We accept per-message cost and a vendor dependency. The risk is price increases — we keep a thin interface so we can swap."
THE TELL OF A SENIOR ARCHITECT

Juniors say what they chose. Seniors say what they gave up. The sentence "we accept that…" is the most credible sentence in any architecture presentation.

PART 7 — ONE DECISION, THREE ROOMS

Technical vs Business Explanation

Same decision — asynchronous processing for notifications — explained to three audiences. Watch what changes and what stays fixed.

TO DEVELOPERS"When an order commits, we write an OrderPlaced event to an outbox table in the same transaction. A background publisher pushes it to the queue. The notification consumer is idempotent on order ID, retries with backoff, and dead-letters after five attempts. Checkout never calls the email provider directly. Correlation ID flows through the message headers so we can trace end to end."
TO THE ENGINEERING MANAGER"Checkout no longer depends on the email provider being up or fast — that removes a whole class of incidents from on-call. It adds one queue and one worker to monitor, which the team already knows how to run. It also means the notification feature can be changed and deployed without touching checkout, so the two teams stop blocking each other."
TO THE BUSINESS"Customers get an instant order confirmation on screen, every time — even when our email provider has a bad day. The email itself arrives a few seconds later, and if it fails the first time we automatically retry, so nobody misses a receipt. This protects sales during peak periods and reduces support tickets about missing confirmations."

Why the wording changes

  • Developers need to build it correctly, so they get mechanisms: outbox, idempotency, dead letter, correlation.
  • Managers need to staff and support it, so they get consequences: incidents removed, things to monitor, team independence.
  • Business needs to trust it, so they get outcomes: instant confirmation, no missed receipts, protected sales.
  • What never changes: the decision (queue the email), the reason (checkout must not wait), and the trade (email is slightly later).
PART 8 — THE NARRATIVE

Architecture Storytelling

People remember stories, not diagrams. Every architecture has this shape: Problem → Goal → Constraints → Options → Decision → Reason → Result → Risk. Three complete examples.

Story 1 — The reports that ate checkout

Problem: Every month-end, finance ran reports and checkout timed out. Support tickets spiked; orders were lost.
Goal: Checkout under 500 ms at all times, reports still delivered same day.
Constraints: One DevOps engineer, six weeks, no new licences.
Options: Tune indexes only; a read replica for reports; a separate reporting service and warehouse.
Decision: Read replica, with report queries routed to it.
Reason: Solved the conflict in two days at $300/month, inside the team's skills. The warehouse would take three months to fix a problem that a replica fixes now.
Result: Zero report-caused incidents for six months; checkout p95 dropped from 2.1 s to 280 ms at month-end.
Risk that remained: Replica lag. We show "as of" timestamps on dashboards and alert if lag exceeds five minutes.

Story 2 — The email provider that failed sales

Problem: A third-party email outage made checkout hang for 30 seconds per order. Customers abandoned carts.
Goal: No external dependency may fail or slow a sale.
Constraints: Keep the existing email provider; small team; no big rewrite.
Options: Add a timeout and carry on; fire-and-forget in a background thread; a proper queue with outbox and retries.
Decision: Queue with outbox.
Reason: A timeout still slows every order during an outage; a background thread loses messages on restart. The queue costs one extra component but guarantees delivery and removes the dependency from the request path.
Result: Checkout 200 ms regardless of email health; during the next provider outage, 14,000 emails queued and delivered when it recovered — no lost sale.
Risk that remained: Duplicate sends on retry — handled with idempotency; queue backlog — alerted on depth and age.

Story 3 — The microservices mandate

Problem: Leadership asked for "microservices" after reading a competitor's blog. The real pain: slow regression testing and one team blocking another on releases.
Goal: Faster, safer releases without slowing feature delivery.
Constraints: 20 developers, one DevOps engineer, no Kubernetes experience, $5K/month run budget.
Options: Keep the monolith as is; refactor into a modular monolith and extract one service; split into seven microservices.
Decision: Modular monolith with strict boundaries, plus Notifications extracted as the single independent service.
Reason: Module boundaries and architecture tests gave the teams independence inside one deployment; extracting Notifications gave the one team that truly needed it a separate release cadence and validated our container pipeline at low risk. Seven services would have tripled cost and required skills we did not have.
Result: Deployment lead time fell from 9 days to 2; the notification team ships daily; run cost rose by $200/month instead of $6K.
Risk that remained: Boundaries erode under deadline pressure — mitigated with build-breaking architecture tests and a 12-month review.
PART 9 — WHEN THE ROOM PUSHES BACK

Handling Challenging Questions

Hard questions are not attacks. They are the audience asking you to prove you thought about it. Answer the question behind the question.

THE QUESTION WHAT THEY ARE REALLY ASKING HOW TO THINK STRONG RESPONSE COMMON MISTAKE
Why did you choose this? "Is this a decision or a habit?" Requirement → options → why this won. "The driving requirement was X. I compared A, B and C. This one won because it meets X within our constraints; B was close but failed on team capacity." Naming the technology's features instead of the requirement.
Why not a simpler solution? "Are you over-engineering?" Name the simpler option and the specific reason it fails. "I started with the simpler option — just indexes. It helps but does not remove the conflict during reports. The replica is the smallest step that actually solves it." Getting defensive; not having tried the simple option.
Why do we need this component? "What breaks if we remove it?" State the job it does and the consequence of removing it. "Remove the queue and checkout waits on the email provider again — that is the outage we had in March. It costs $100 a month to never repeat that." Saying "it's best practice".
Isn't this too expensive? "Compared to what, and what does the alternative cost us?" Price both the design and the failure it prevents. "It is $1,700 a month. The cheaper single-server option is $600, but one lost day of orders costs $80K. Over three years this is the lower total cost." Only quoting the monthly bill.
What if this service goes down? "Did you design for failure or only for success?" Blast radius, detection, fallback, recovery. "If the notification worker dies, checkout is unaffected; messages queue up; we alert on backlog age; when it restarts, it drains the queue. Nothing is lost." "It won't go down" or "we'll restart it".
What happens at 10× traffic? "Where is the ceiling and do you know the next step?" Walk each tier; name the first bottleneck and the staged response. "App tier scales sideways. Reads scale with replicas and cache. The write primary is the ceiling — partitioning by tenant is the next step, sharding after that. None of those is a rewrite." "It scales horizontally" with no specifics.
What if Redis fails? "Is the cache a convenience or a dependency?" Fallback path and protection against the herd. "Everything falls through to the database with a request cap so we do not stampede it. Pages are slower, not broken. Nothing that affects money lives only in the cache." Not having a fallback.
What if the broker fails? "Do you lose messages?" Outbox, durability, catch-up. "Events are written to an outbox in the same transaction as the order. If the broker is down they wait there and publish when it returns. Delayed, never lost." Publishing directly from the request.
Why not use one database? "Are the separate stores earning their cost?" What each store does that the other cannot. "We do use one transactional database. The replica is a copy for reads, not a second source of truth. That is the minimum separation that fixes the reporting conflict." Defending separation as a principle.
Why microservices? "Do you have the team and the problem they solve?" Organisational and load reasons, plus operational readiness. "Because eight teams were blocked on one release train and one component needs 40× the capacity. And because we have a platform team to run it. Without those, I would not." "Scalability."
Why not a modular monolith? "Did you consider the middle option?" Acknowledge it was the default and say exactly why it lost. "It was my default. It lost because two teams need different runtimes and release cadences that a single deployment cannot give. If that were not true, I would have chosen it." Not knowing the option exists.
How much will this cost? "Have you done the maths?" Build, run now, run at growth, and what is avoided. "Build: about 12 weeks with the current team. Run: ~$1,700 a month now, ~$4–5K at 5×. That avoids two hires and a $9K platform we do not need yet." "It depends" with no number.
How long will this take? "Can we plan around you?" Phases with value delivered at each. "Week 2: replica live, reporting pain gone. Week 8: modules and boundaries in place. Week 12: notifications extracted. Each phase delivers value on its own." One big date with nothing before it.
What is the biggest risk? "Are you honest about weaknesses?" Name one real risk and its mitigation. "Boundary erosion under deadline pressure. We enforce it with tests that fail the build, and we review at 12 months. Second is a missed tenant filter — enforced centrally and tested." "I don't see major risks."
PART 10 — THE MOST HONEST ANSWER

Handling "I Don't Know"

Nobody knows everything. Interviewers and executives do not expect you to. What they watch is what you do next: invent, or reason.

Admit the uncertainty"I don't have that exact number." Say it early, plainly, once.
State what you do know"What I do know is the read/write ratio is roughly 500:1 and the data changes hourly."
State your assumption"If I assume a 4 KB record and 200K products, that is under a gigabyte of cache."
Say how you would verify"I would confirm with a week of production metrics before sizing the cluster."

Example 1 — "What is your exact p99 latency under peak?"

BAD"Around 50 milliseconds." (invented — and the follow-up will expose it)
ARCHITECT"I don't have the peak p99 figure to hand. Our target is under 500 ms and the last load test showed p95 around 280 ms at 3× current traffic. p99 will be higher — I would expect a tail from cold cache and GC pauses. I would pull the real number from monitoring before committing to it."

Example 2 — "How does this broker handle a network partition?"

BAD"It handles it automatically." (vague and possibly wrong)
ARCHITECT"I am not certain of the exact behaviour of this broker under partition. What I designed for is that our side does not depend on it: events are in an outbox and republished. If you need the broker's own guarantees, I would read the vendor's consistency documentation and test it in staging before relying on it."

Example 3 — "What is the cost of this cloud service at 10× volume?"

BAD"About the same." (no thought given)
ARCHITECT"I have not priced 10× precisely. My assumption is roughly linear on compute and storage, with some discount at volume, which puts it around $5K a month — but managed services sometimes have pricing steps. I would run the vendor calculator with real numbers and present a proper figure."

Example 4 — "Does this design meet our compliance standard?"

BAD"Yes, it should be fine." (dangerous on a compliance question)
ARCHITECT"I am not the authority on that standard and I will not guess. What I can say is the design isolates tenant data, encrypts at rest and in transit, and keeps a full audit trail. I would walk it through with the compliance team against the specific controls before we claim it."

Example 5 — "How does your competitor's system do this?"

BADMakes up a plausible architecture.
ARCHITECT"I don't know their internal design. Based on public information they appear to use a service-based approach, but I would not build our decisions on that. Our constraints are different, and the design has to fit us."
WHY THIS WORKS

An honest "I don't know, here is what I do know, here is how I'd find out" raises your credibility. An invented number that gets caught destroys everything else you said.

PART 11 — WHEN PEOPLE DISAGREE

Handling Disagreement

Disagreement is information. Somebody sees a risk, a cost, or a constraint you may have missed. Facts, goals, trade-offs, collaboration — in that order.

1. A developer disagrees with the architecture

Situation: "This modular thing is over-engineering. Let's just call the other module's repository directly."
WRONG"I'm the architect. This is how we're doing it."
BETTER"The boundaries matter for the future. Please follow the pattern."
ARCHITECT"Tell me what is slowing you down — if the module interface is missing something, let's add it today. The reason we keep the boundary is that last year a direct query from Billing into Orders broke checkout when Orders changed a column. I'd rather pay a small cost now than that again. If you see a cleaner way to keep the boundary and unblock you, I'm genuinely open."

2. The Product Manager wants a faster solution

Situation: "Can we skip the queue and just send the email inline? We need this feature next week."
WRONG"No. That's bad architecture."
BETTER"We can, but it will cause problems later."
ARCHITECT"We can ship inline next week, and here is the trade: if the email provider slows down, checkout slows down with it — that is exactly the outage from March. Option B: ship inline behind a flag now, add the queue in sprint two, and I will document that as deliberate debt with a date. Which risk would you rather carry for two weeks?"

3. The Manager wants lower cost

Situation: "Can we drop the replica? That's $300 a month."
WRONG"Then don't blame me when reports break checkout."
BETTER"The replica is important for performance."
ARCHITECT"We can. Here is what it buys: it is the reason month-end no longer costs us orders — roughly $80K a quarter before it. It is also our failover if the primary dies. If $300 must go, I would rather cut the second cache node or defer the reporting warehouse. Let's look at the list together and cut where the risk is lowest."

4. The Security team rejects the design

Situation: "Shared database across tenants is unacceptable. Every customer needs its own."
WRONG"That's not practical. 10,000 databases is impossible."
BETTER"We enforce tenant filtering in code. It's safe."
ARCHITECT"Help me understand the specific control you need to satisfy — is it logical isolation, physical isolation, or audit evidence? Today we enforce tenant filtering at the data layer, test it automatically, and log every cross-tenant attempt. For regulated customers we already offer a dedicated database. If a control requires physical isolation for everyone, that changes the design and the cost significantly, and we should take that to the business together with the number."

5. Operations says it is too difficult to support

Situation: "A queue, a cache, a replica — that's three more things to page us about."
WRONG"It's standard infrastructure. You'll manage."
BETTER"We'll add monitoring for each."
ARCHITECT"You're right that it is three more things, and I should have brought you in earlier. Here is what I propose: each one gets a runbook, a health check and a single meaningful alert before it goes live; all three are managed services so patching is not on you; and I will sit the first two on-call rotations with you. If after a month any of them is creating noise without value, we change it."

6. The Business wants a feature that conflicts with the architecture

Situation: "We want real-time stock levels across all warehouses on every product page."
WRONG"The architecture doesn't support that."
BETTER"That's a big change; it will take time."
ARCHITECT"That is a real requirement change — today stock is strongly consistent at checkout and near-real-time elsewhere. True real-time on every page view means we cannot cache product pages the way we do, which changes cost and load. Let me ask: is 30 seconds old acceptable? If yes, it is a small change. If it must be instant, I will come back with a design and a cost, and we decide together whether the value justifies it."
PART 12 — THE WORKED EXAMPLE

A Complete 10-Minute Presentation

The Multi-Company Inventory & Order Management System, presented to a CTO and an Engineering Manager. Natural speaking style. Read it aloud and time yourself.

MINUTE 1 — BUSINESS PROBLEM"Thanks for the time. The problem we're solving is simple to state: our order platform slows to a crawl whenever heavy reports run, and it's getting harder to change without breaking something. Last quarter that cost us roughly $80K in abandoned checkouts and two weekend incidents. We're also expecting five times the traffic within eighteen months, and the current setup won't get us there."
MINUTE 2 — REQUIREMENTS"The platform serves 10,000 companies and about a million users. It has eight areas: authentication, customers, products, orders, inventory, payments, notifications and reporting. The busiest paths are dashboards and reporting — lots of reads — and order creation, around 50,000 orders a day today. Every company's data must be completely separate from every other company's."
MINUTE 3 — WHAT MATTERS MOST"Four qualities shape the design. Checkout must stay under half a second, even at month-end. We need 99.9% uptime in business hours. Company data isolation and a full audit trail are non-negotiable — those are gates, not preferences. And payments and stock at the moment of sale must always be exactly right. Dashboards, on the other hand, can be a minute old — and that distinction is what makes the design affordable."
MINUTE 4 — THE ARCHITECTURE"Here's the picture. Clients — web and mobile — talk to one API. Behind it is a single application, but built as separate modules: each business area owns its own code and its own database schema, and they only talk through defined interfaces. One main database holds the truth. A read-only copy serves dashboards and reports. A cache holds product and configuration data. A queue handles work that can happen after checkout. And there's the external payment provider. That's seven things — I'll explain why each is there."
MINUTE 5 — THE MAIN FLOW"Let's place an order. The user taps Buy. The API checks who they are and which company. The Order module validates the cart, asks Inventory to hold the stock, asks Payment to charge the card — that call goes to the provider and we wait, because the user needs to know the result. Order and stock are saved together in one transaction. Then, and only then, we drop three events on the queue: send the confirmation email, tell the warehouse, refresh the reporting copy. The user sees 'Order confirmed' in about 200 milliseconds. The email arrives a few seconds later."
MINUTE 6 — DATA FLOW"There's one source of truth: the main database. Everything else is a copy with a known delay. The reporting replica is seconds behind — fine for a dashboard, never used for checkout. The cache holds product data for one minute, and we clear it the moment a product changes. Audit records are written on the same transaction as the change, so they can't be lost or altered separately. Every table carries the company ID and every query is filtered by it at one central place — not left to each developer to remember."
MINUTE 7 — SCALING AND PERFORMANCE"How it grows: the application servers are stateless, so at 2× or 5× we add instances behind the load balancer — that's a setting, not a project. Reads grow with more replicas and a higher cache hit rate. The one real ceiling is the write side of the main database. At today's numbers we're using about 15% of it; 5× keeps us under the limit. If we approach it, the next step is partitioning orders by company and date, and only after that would we consider splitting the database. None of those steps requires rewriting the application."
MINUTE 8 — SECURITY AND RELIABILITY"Security: single sign-on at the API, company isolation enforced in the data layer and tested on every build, secrets in a managed vault, everything encrypted in transit and at rest, and a full audit trail. Reliability: if the cache dies, we fall back to the database with a request cap so we don't stampede it — slower, not broken. If the queue is down, events sit in an outbox table and publish when it returns — delayed, not lost. If the main database fails, the replica is promoted automatically within minutes; we test that quarterly. And we keep point-in-time backups, because a replica copies mistakes too."
MINUTE 9 — TRADE-OFFS AND WHAT I REJECTED"Two decisions I want you to challenge. First: one application with modules, not microservices. Microservices would give fully independent releases and isolation — real benefits. But we have twenty developers and one DevOps engineer, and 150 requests a second. Microservices would roughly triple our run cost, require skills we don't have, and force us to split transactions that must stay together. The module boundaries give us most of the ownership benefit, and if a team needs true independence later, we can extract that module — Notifications is the obvious first candidate. Second: one shared database with strict company filtering, not a database per company. Ten thousand databases is not something this team can operate. We accept a noisy-neighbour risk, which we manage with per-company limits, and we offer a dedicated database to regulated customers who need it."
MINUTE 10 — RISKS, COST, RECOMMENDATION"Risks: the biggest is module boundaries eroding under deadline pressure — we enforce them with tests that fail the build. Second is a missed company filter — enforced centrally, tested automatically, monitored. Third is replica lag confusing users — we show 'as of' times and alert above five minutes. Cost: about $1,700 a month today; roughly $4–5K at five times the traffic. The alternative — a full microservices platform — would be around $9K a month plus two additional hires. Delivery: the replica goes live in week two and removes the reporting pain immediately; modules and boundaries by week eight; notifications extracted by week twelve. My recommendation is to proceed with this design, and to review it in twelve months or when we pass fifty developers — whichever comes first. Happy to take questions."
NOTICE

Every technical term is explained in the same breath ("a read-only copy", "an outbox table"). There is a number in almost every minute. The two real decisions get the most time. And it ends with a recommendation and a review trigger — not with "any questions?"

PART 13 — THE INTERVIEW ROOM

Whiteboard Interview Technique

The whiteboard is not a drawing test. It is a thinking-out-loud test. What you draw matters less than what you say while drawing it.

1. What to draw firstNothing. Ask three questions first. Then write the key numbers and the top three non-functional requirements in a corner. They are your anchor for the whole session.
2. What to draw nextThe simplest end-to-end shape: client → API → application → database. Five boxes. Get one request flowing before adding anything.
3. When to ask questionsBefore drawing (scope, scale, constraints), before every major addition ("is stale data acceptable for dashboards?"), and whenever you are about to assume something that changes the design.
4. When to stop drawingWhen the main flow works, the main failure cases are covered, and the growth path is clear. More boxes after that is noise. Say "I'll stop here unless you want depth on something."
5. How much detail is enoughEnough that another engineer could start. Not enough to configure a server. Components, responsibilities, data ownership, sync vs async, failure handling.
6. When the interviewer changes a requirementThank them — it is the real test. Say what changes and what does not: "Real-time stock changes the cache strategy and the Inventory boundary; the rest holds." Then adjust the drawing, not restart it.
7. How to communicate assumptionsOut loud and on the board. "I'm assuming 10% of users are active daily — tell me if that's wrong." Write it in the corner. Assumptions stated are reasoning; assumptions hidden are mistakes.
The one habitNarrate everything. Silence while drawing is the most common way strong candidates lose points.

A practical 30-minute flow

  1. Minutes 0–4 — ClarifyRestate the problem. Ask: who uses it, how many, what is the peak, what must never be wrong, what is the budget or team constraint. Write numbers and the top three qualities in the corner.
  2. Minutes 4–8 — Simplest shapeDraw client → API → application → database. Walk one request through it. Say what each box is responsible for.
  3. Minutes 8–14 — Add only what the requirements demandHeavy reads? Add cache and replica, explain why. Slow external calls? Add a queue, explain why. Multi-tenant? Say how isolation is enforced. Each addition answers a stated requirement.
  4. Minutes 14–19 — Failure and scalePoint at each major box: what if it fails, how do we know, what happens to the user. Then: what changes at 5×, where is the ceiling, what is the next step.
  5. Minutes 19–24 — Trade-offsName the two biggest decisions. For each: what you chose, what you rejected, what the rejected option does better, why it lost here. Invite challenge.
  6. Minutes 24–28 — Handle the curveballThe interviewer changes something. State the impact, adjust the board, keep what still holds. Stay calm — this is the part that gets you hired.
  7. Minutes 28–30 — SummariseThirty seconds: the shape, the two key decisions, the top risk, and when you would revisit. End on a sentence, not a shrug.
PART 14 — WHAT TO AVOID

15 Architecture Presentation Mistakes

Each of these has cost a good candidate an offer or a good design its approval.

MISTAKE WHY IT HURTS BETTER APPROACH
1. Starting with technology The room does not know why any of it exists. Decisions look like preferences. Open with the business problem and the numbers. Technology arrives as the answer to a stated need.
2. Drawing too many boxes Nobody can hold 25 things. The important decisions get lost among logging agents. Five to eight boxes that carry decisions. Detail on request.
3. Unnecessary technical detail The CTO hears protocols, not risk. You lose the room and the time. Match depth to audience. Keep the mechanism for developers; give outcomes to everyone else.
4. Not discussing requirements Without the requirement, no decision can be judged right or wrong. State the three qualities that matter most before the first box. Refer back to them.
5. Not asking questions You design for a system that does not exist. Interviewers read it as arrogance or inexperience. Ask before drawing and before every major addition. Write the answers down.
6. Not explaining trade-offs A design with no stated costs sounds naive. Everyone knows there are costs. "We chose… we gain… we accept… the risk is…" for every real decision.
7. Ignoring cost Every executive is thinking it. If you do not say it, they assume you have not. Give a rough monthly number now and at growth, and what the alternative would cost.
8. Ignoring failure cases A happy-path-only design is a design for a system that will surprise you in production. For every major component: what if it fails, how do we know, what does the user see.
9. Speaking too fast Nervous speed reads as uncertainty. The audience cannot process and stops trying. Pause after each decision. Silence is not a gap; it is emphasis.
10. Memorised answers The first follow-up question breaks them. Interviewers can hear a script. Know the reasoning, not the words. Answer the question that was actually asked.
11. Defending bad decisions Digging in when shown a better option signals ego over judgement. "That's a better option for this constraint — let me update." That sentence wins interviews.
12. Saying "best practice" without why It means "I did not think about it." Every practice is a trade-off in some context. Replace with the actual reason: "…because it prevents X, which happened to us in March."
13. Unnecessary jargon It excludes half the room and hides whether you understand it yourself. Use the plain phrase, then the term if needed: "a read-only copy — a replica."
14. Not summarising The last thing said is the thing remembered. Ending on a detail wastes that. Close with: the shape, the two key decisions, the top risk, the recommendation, the review trigger.
15. Not confirming concerns Stakeholders leave with unspoken worries, which become objections later. Ask directly: "What concerns you most about this?" Then address it or write it down with an owner.

Architecture Defense Exercise

I am the interviewer. You designed the Multi-Company Inventory & Order Management System shown above. Answer each question aloud before revealing the model answer guide.

THE INTERVIEWER'S 16 QUESTIONS

  1. Why this architecture?
  2. Why not a simpler design — one app, one database, done?
  3. Why Redis at all?
  4. Why asynchronous processing — what is wrong with just calling the email service?
  5. Why separate reporting?
  6. What happens when Redis fails?
  7. What happens when the main database fails?
  8. What happens when the queue is unavailable?
  9. What happens when traffic increases 10×?

  12. What would you change if the user count doubled overnight?

  14. How do you stop one company's traffic hurting the others?
  15. Your team has never run a message queue in production. Still the right call?

Model Answer Guide (keep yours this length)

1. Why this architecture? It fixes the two real problems — reports slowing checkout and slow, risky releases — inside our constraints: 20 developers, one DevOps engineer, a small budget. Everything else follows from those two problems and three constraints.

2. Why not simpler? One app and one database is where we are, and it is the thing that fails at month-end. The replica is the smallest addition that removes that conflict. The modules cost nothing to run — they are code discipline, not infrastructure.

3. Why Redis? Product and dashboard reads outnumber writes hundreds to one. A one-minute cache removes most of that load for about $150 a month. Nothing that affects money lives only in the cache.

4. Why async? Calling the email service inline means checkout waits on it and fails when it fails — that was the March outage. The queue gives a 200 ms checkout and retries. Payment stays synchronous because the user must know the result.

5. Why separate reporting? Reports were the direct cause of lost orders. Reading from a replica seconds behind is acceptable for a report and removes the conflict entirely.

6. Redis fails? Reads fall through to the database with a request cap so we do not stampede it. Slower, not broken. We alert and warm the cache on recovery.

7. Database fails? Managed failover promotes the replica within minutes; the app reconnects with retries; the queue holds post-checkout work. We test this quarterly and keep point-in-time backups.

8. Queue unavailable? Events are written to an outbox in the same transaction as the order. They wait and publish when the queue returns. Delayed, never lost. Checkout is unaffected.

9. 10× traffic? App tier: add instances. Reads: more replicas, higher cache hit. Writes: the ceiling — partition by company and date first, shard only if still needed. Staged, not a rewrite.

10. Cost? Roughly $1,700 a month today: app tier, database plus replica, cache, queue, monitoring. About $4–5K at 5×. A full microservices platform would be ~$9K plus two hires.

11. Cut 30%? Keep backups and the replica — they protect revenue. Drop the second cache node, defer the reporting warehouse, run reports off-peak. Document what was removed and the trigger to restore it.

12. Users double overnight? Add app instances immediately; raise cache size; watch database write utilisation and connection pool. We are at ~15% of the write ceiling today, so 2× is comfortable — but I would bring the partitioning work forward.

13. Why Notifications, not Payments? Notifications is asynchronous, has no transactional tie to orders, and is the team that needs independent releases. Payments must stay atomic with orders and stock — extracting it first would force distributed transactions on the most sensitive path.

14. Noisy company? Per-company rate limits at the API, tenant as a dimension on every metric so we see it, and the option to move a heavy tenant to a dedicated pool or database.

15. Team never ran a queue? Fair concern. That is why it is a managed queue with an outbox — the failure mode is "delayed", not "lost". I would start with the single notification flow, add a runbook and one alert, and sit the first on-call rotation with the team.

16. One sentence. It removes the outage we already had, keeps payments exactly right, fits the team we actually have, costs a fifth of the alternative, and leaves the door open to split later if the business grows into it.

DAY 21 — CLOSE

10 Rules for Explaining Architecture

Short enough to remember in the room. True enough to use for a career.

01

Start with the problem, not the picture.

A number and a consequence in the first thirty seconds. Technology arrives as the answer, never as the opening.

02

Change the words, never the decision.

Developers get mechanisms, managers get consequences, the business gets outcomes. The design is the same in every room.

03

Walk one request through the diagram.

Left to right, start to finish. Skip every box that does not carry a decision.

04

Every box must answer "why is this here?"

In two sentences, tied to a requirement. If you cannot, remove the box or learn why it exists.

05

Say what you gave up.

"We accept that…" is the most credible sentence in any presentation. Juniors say what they chose; seniors say what it cost.

06

Praise the option you rejected.

If you cannot say what it does better, nobody believes you considered it.

07

Put a number in every minute.

Users, requests, milliseconds, dollars, weeks. Numbers turn opinions into decisions.

08

Design for failure out loud.

For every major component: what if it fails, how do we know, what does the user see. Happy paths do not need architects.

09

"I don't know" plus a method beats a guess.

What you know, what you assume, how you would verify. An invented number, once caught, erases everything else you said.

10

End with a recommendation and a review date.

Not with "any questions?". The last sentence is the one they remember — make it the decision.

ONE-MINUTE ARCHITECT ANSWER — "HOW DO YOU EXPLAIN A COMPLEX ARCHITECTURE TO A BUSINESS STAKEHOLDER?"

"I start from what they care about, not from the system. For a business stakeholder that is usually money, customers, time and risk — so I open with the problem in those terms: 'month-end reports were costing us around $80K a quarter in lost orders.' Then I show one simple picture — five or six boxes — and walk one customer action through it in everyday words: 'the order is saved here, this is the copy we run reports from so they never slow down checkout, this is the queue that sends the email a few seconds later so a slow email provider can never fail a sale.' I explain the two decisions that actually matter and what we gave up for each, because they will trust the design more when they hear its costs. I give them the monthly number now and at growth, and what the alternative would have cost. I name the biggest risk and how we handle it. And I end with a clear recommendation and when we would look at it again. The test I use is simple: if they can explain the design to someone else in two sentences afterwards, I did my job. The decision never changes between audiences — only the depth and the words."

THE ARCHITECT'S POWER STATEMENT

"A system is only as good as the understanding around it. My job is not to make people feel the complexity — it is to make the complexity feel simple enough to decide on, build correctly, and defend when it is tested. If the room leaves able to explain it in two sentences, the architecture is finished. If they leave impressed but confused, it is not."

Twenty-one days in, you can design a system, choose its trade-offs, and now stand in front of anyone — a developer, a CTO, an interviewer — and make them see it the way you do. That is the whole job.
