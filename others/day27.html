PART 01 — WHAT ARCHITECTURE LEADERSHIP REALLY MEANS

Knowing technology is not the job. Helping people choose well is.

Technical expertise is knowing how things work — how a queue behaves, why a database locks, what a cache costs. Architecture leadership is helping the organization choose the right solution when smart people disagree and nobody has all the facts.

A doctor can know every drug and still be a bad doctor if they never listen to the patient. An Architect can know every pattern and still be a bad Architect if they cannot bring a room to a decision that the business can afford, the team can build, and operations can run.

Why an Architect does NOT need to know everything

The system is bigger than one head. The senior developer knows the code better. Operations knows the 3am pain better. Security knows the threats better. Finance knows the money better. If you must know everything before you can lead, you will either freeze or pretend. Both are worse than asking. Your value is not owning every fact. Your value is collecting the right facts and turning them into a decision.

  1. Understand the problemBefore you defend a design, be sure you and the other person are talking about the same problem. Half of all “disagreements” are two people solving different problems.
  2. Ask questions“What worries you most about this?” “What happens if we are wrong?” “Which number would change your mind?” Questions are how a leader finds the real issue.
  3. Guide decisionsGuide means: give structure, options, and evidence — not “here is my answer, please accept it.”
  4. Compare optionsNever present one option. One option is an order. Two or three with trade-offs is a decision.
  5. Identify risksSay the risks of your own design first. That builds more trust than any slide.
  6. Communicate clearlyProduct and Finance do not want diagrams. They want: what it costs, what it protects, what could go wrong, when it ships.
  7. Get people alignedAligned does not mean everyone agrees. It means everyone understands why, and will support the decision after the meeting.
  8. Protect important system qualitiesReliability, security, data correctness, operability. These rarely have a loud voice in the room. You are their voice.
  9. Accept feedbackWhen a developer finds a hole in your design, you gained a better design. Say thank you and mean it.
  10. Make decisions when necessarySometimes there is no consensus and the clock is running. Then you decide, explain, document, and accept responsibility.
  11. Own the outcomeIf the decision fails, you do not point at the team. You lead the fix and the review.
WHY “SMARTEST IN THE ROOM” IS NOT THE GOAL

If you win every argument, people stop bringing you problems and start hiding them. Then you make decisions with less information every month. The Architect who needs to be right becomes the Architect who is last to know. The goal is the best decision for the business and the system — not the best score for you.

PART 02 — ARCHITECT VS TECHNICAL LEAD

Two jobs, one goal. Do not do theirs.

A Technical Lead leads the building of a system with a team. A Technical Architect leads the shape of a system across teams, time, and money. Confusing the two creates either a bossy Architect or an Architect nobody needs.

Dimension Technical Lead Technical Architect
Decision scope How this team builds this feature. Code structure, libraries, patterns inside the service. How systems fit together. Boundaries, data ownership, integration style, non-functional targets, what we pay for.
Team interaction Daily. Pairing, reviews, unblocking, mentoring. Regular but not daily. Design sessions, reviews, cross-team alignment. Should not be a surprise visitor.
Business involvement Understands the sprint goal and the feature. Understands the business model, growth plan, cost limits, compliance, and which failures hurt revenue.
Technical ownership Owns code quality, delivery, and technical health of their area. Owns technical direction, cross-cutting standards, and the quality attributes of the whole system.
Architecture ownership Owns architecture inside the service, within agreed boundaries. Owns architecture between services and the long-term evolution.
Risk ownership Delivery risk, defects, local technical debt. Systemic risk: scaling, security posture, vendor lock-in, single points of failure, cost surprises.
Stakeholder communication Product owner, engineering manager, team. Product leadership, security, operations, finance, CTO, vendors, other Architects.

Working with a Tech Lead instead of replacing them

  • Agree the boundary. “Inside the service is yours. Between services and shared data is where I need to be involved.”
  • Bring problems, not finished answers. Give the Tech Lead the constraints and the target; let them shape the internal design.
  • Review, do not rewrite. If you keep redoing their design, you have hired a typist, not a lead.
  • Share credit, absorb blame. When it works, it was the team. When it breaks at the system level, it was your call.
  • Make them stronger. The best Architect leaves behind Tech Leads who could do the Architect’s job on a small system.
INTERVIEW LINE

“I try to be the person the Tech Lead calls before a decision, not the person who reverses it after.”

PART 03 — THE ARCHITECTURE DECISION CONVERSATION

Nine steps you can run in any disagreement

This is the backbone of the whole day. Every scenario later uses it. Memorise it until it is how you naturally talk.

  1. Understand the concernLet them finish. Do not prepare your reply while they speak. Example: a developer says “this design is over-engineered”. The concern might be time, not design.SAY: “Tell me more about what worries you.”
  2. Ask questionsFind the fact behind the feeling. “Which part costs the most time?” “Have we seen this fail before?” “What would you do instead?”SAY: “What would need to be true for this to be fine?”
  3. Restate the problemSay it back in one sentence and get a nod. “So the problem is: orders must never be lost, and we have six weeks.” Now you are solving the same thing.SAY: “So if I have this right…”
  4. List the constraintsTime, money, people, skills, compliance, existing systems, growth target. Constraints turn opinion into engineering.SAY: “Here is what we cannot change.”
  5. Compare optionsAt least two. “Option A: module inside the app. Option B: separate service. Option C: module now with a clean boundary, split later if X happens.”SAY: “We have three real choices.”
  6. Discuss trade-offsWhat each option gives and costs, in terms the room cares about: delivery time, risk, cost, operability, future change.SAY: “A is faster now; B is safer at 5×.”
  7. Agree on the decisionPrefer agreement. If you cannot get it, decide and say why. Ask: “Can you support this even if it is not your first choice?”SAY: “Can we live with C?”
  8. Document the decisionA short record: context, options, decision, reasons, who agreed, what would make us revisit. This is an ADR — an Architecture Decision Record. One page, not a novel.SAY: “I will write this up today.”
  9. Define how we will know it workedA number and a date. “If p95 latency stays under 300ms and we ship in six weeks, C was right. If the module needs its own scaling by Q3, we split.”SAY: “Here is how we check ourselves.”
WHY THIS WORKS

Steps 1–4 make people feel heard and make the problem real. Steps 5–6 turn a fight into a choice. Steps 7–9 make the decision survive the meeting. Skip step 1 and you are arguing. Skip step 9 and you are guessing.

PART 04 — DEVELOPER DISAGREES WITH YOUR ARCHITECTURE

“We don’t need a separate service. A simple module is enough.”

A senior developer says this in front of the team. Everyone looks at you. What you say in the next 30 seconds sets your credibility for the year.

BAD RESPONSE

We already decided this. The architecture says separate service. Let’s move on.

Uses authority instead of reasons. Teaches the team not to raise concerns. If the developer is right, you just protected a mistake.

BETTER RESPONSE

I hear you. A module is simpler. But we expect this part to scale differently and have its own release cycle, so I lean towards a service. Can we discuss after stand-up?

Respectful and gives a reason. But it still starts from defending, and the reason is an assumption (“we expect”) rather than a fact.

STRONG ARCHITECT RESPONSE

That might be right — a module is cheaper and faster, and I do not want a service for its own sake. Help me check two things. First, this part is expected to take 40% of traffic after the Q3 launch and it changes weekly while the rest changes monthly. Does a module handle that without slowing releases? Second, if we go module, what would make us split later, and how hard would that be?

If we can keep a clean boundary inside the app — own tables, own interface, no shared internals — I am happy to start as a module and split when the traffic number proves it. Let’s write that trigger down so it is not a feeling later. Fair?

Respect (“that might be right”) · Curiosity (two real questions) · Business context (Q3, 40%, weekly changes) · Evidence (numbers, not adjectives) · Trade-offs (cheap now vs split later) · Decision (module with boundary + written trigger).

When you should change your own decision

  • They have a fact you did not have. Real traffic is lower, the team has no experience with distributed debugging, the deadline moved.
  • Your reason was an assumption. “It will need to scale separately” — says who? If no one, you were guessing.
  • The cheaper option keeps the door open. A module with a clean boundary can become a service. A service cannot easily become simple again.
  • The risk of being wrong is small and reversible. Then choose the simpler option and measure.
WHEN YOU SHOULD NOT CHANGE IT

When the objection is about comfort, not evidence. When the quality you are protecting — data isolation between companies, payment correctness, a legal requirement — has no voice on the team. Then you hold the line, explain the why, and offer to revisit with data.

PART 05 — PRODUCT MANAGER WANTS FASTER DELIVERY

“We don’t have time for this architecture. Just build it quickly.”

Product is not your enemy. Product is under pressure you may not see. Your job is to separate what cannot be skipped from what can wait — and to say it in delivery language.

Bucket Meaning Example in an order system Decision
Must-have architecture Without it, we lose money, data, or trust — and cannot fix later cheaply. Tenant id on every table. Idempotent payment. Backups. NEVER SKIP
Useful but optional Makes life better; can be added later without rewriting. Read replica. Fancy dashboards. Auto-scaling rules. PHASE 2
Technical debt A known shortcut we accept on purpose, with a plan to repay. Hard-coded config. One big service instead of two. Manual deployment. ACCEPT + TICKET + DATE
Future work Only needed if a future event happens. Multi-region. Event sourcing. Second message broker. DO NOT BUILD YET
BAD RESPONSE

If we skip architecture it will all fall apart. I cannot sign off on that.

Vague fear. No options. Product hears “blocker”.

STRONG ARCHITECT RESPONSE

Understood — the date matters. Let me split the architecture into three piles. Two things I cannot drop because they are almost impossible to add later: tenant isolation in the data model and idempotent payment — together about four days. Three things I can move to after launch: replica, dashboards, auto-scaling. And two shortcuts I will accept on purpose with a repayment ticket. That takes the plan from nine weeks to six. Can we agree the two must-haves stay?

Business respect · Concrete buckets · Numbers in days · Protects what cannot be undone · Gives Product a real win.

Five practical examples

  1. Multi-tenant data. Must-have: tenant column and filtering everywhere. Optional: separate databases per big customer. Decide: column now, separate DB only for customers who pay for it.
  2. Payment. Must-have: no double charge (idempotency key). Optional: automatic refunds workflow. Decide: idempotency now; refunds manual for the first month.
  3. Reporting. Must-have: reports do not slow orders. Optional: a full data warehouse. Decide: run reports at night or on a replica; warehouse later.
  4. Notifications. Must-have: order confirmation not lost. Optional: templated multi-channel engine. Decide: queue + one email template now.
  5. Deployment. Must-have: rollback possible. Optional: blue/green with automated checks. Decide: simple versioned deploy with a tested rollback script.
THE RULE

Never block the business with “architecture”. Block only the specific shortcut that cannot be undone — and name the cost of undoing it. Everything else is negotiable, and you should say so first.

PART 06 — MANAGEMENT WANTS LOWER COST

“Your architecture is too expensive.”

Management is right to ask. Your answer must show that you understand all five kinds of cost, not only the cloud bill.

Cost type Simple meaning Where it hides
Infrastructure Servers, databases, cache, network. The monthly bill. Idle replicas, oversized nodes, three environments that mirror production.
Development People-time to build it. Distributed systems take longer to build and test than a monolith.
Maintenance People-time to keep it running and change it. Every extra service is another thing to patch, monitor, upgrade, and understand.
Failure Money lost when it breaks: downtime, refunds, penalties, lost customers. The “cheap” design with one database node and no backups.
Business impact Revenue gained or lost because of what the design allows. Cannot onboard a large customer because there is no tenant isolation.
BAD RESPONSE

Good architecture costs money. If we go cheap, we will pay later.

True and useless. No numbers, no options.

STRONG ARCHITECT RESPONSE

Agreed, it is more than we need on day one. I costed three versions. The full design is about 18k a month. A simplified version is about 9k and keeps everything that protects revenue: backups, one standby database, tenant isolation. What we remove: the second region, the dedicated reporting cluster, and two of the six services — I merge them. What we lose: a regional failure would mean about two hours of downtime instead of ten minutes. Given we do 30k a day in orders, I think 9k is the right choice today, and I have a trigger for adding the region if daily revenue passes 100k.

Numbers · Options · Names what is removed and what that risks · Ties it to revenue · Gives a trigger to revisit.

High-cost vs simplified — the same system

HIGH-COST ARCHITECTURE

~18k / month

6 microservices · each with its own database · Redis cluster · dedicated reporting cluster · two regions active-active · full-size staging · 24/7 on-call for six services.

Cost of running: six deploy pipelines, six dashboards, cross-service debugging, data sync between regions.

SIMPLIFIED ARCHITECTURE

~9k / month

Modular monolith with 3 clear modules · one SQL primary + one standby · Redis with a replica · reports on the standby at night · one region, multi-zone · small staging · one on-call rotation.

What survived: backups, failover, tenant isolation, queue for async work, monitoring. What we accept: regional outage = hours not minutes.

HOW TO SIMPLIFY WITHOUT DAMAGE

Remove duplication first (services that could be modules, environments that could be smaller). Remove “insurance” second, only after you say what it insured. Never remove the things that protect data and money: backups, failover, isolation, idempotency. Those are cheap compared to the failure they prevent.

PART 07 — SECURITY REJECTS THE ARCHITECTURE

“Your design does not meet our security requirements.”

Security saying no is information, not an insult. The Architect who fights Security wastes weeks. The Architect who asks what the actual requirement is usually finds a design that satisfies both in days.

  1. Do not treat Security as an enemyThey carry risk you do not see: audits, breaches, regulators. Start with “help me understand”, not “this will slow us down”.
  2. Understand the actual concern“Does not meet requirements” is a headline. Ask which one. Often it is one specific control, not the whole design.
  3. Identify the requirementSeparate the requirement (“secrets must not be in source control”) from a suggested solution (“you must use vault product X”). You can meet the requirement in several ways.
  4. Find alternativesOffer two or three ways to meet the requirement, with cost and delivery impact for each.
  5. Adjust the architecture where requiredIf the requirement is real, change the design. Do not negotiate on “must never happen” items; negotiate on how.
  6. Document the decisionRecord the requirement, the chosen control, any accepted risk, who accepted it, and the review date.
Area Security says Actual requirement Architect’s adjusted design
Authentication “Custom login is not acceptable.” Passwords must be handled by a proven identity provider; MFA must be possible. Use the company identity provider with standard tokens; keep our own user profile table. Two days of work, less code to own.
Authorization “Roles in the UI are not enough.” Every API call must be checked on the server for what this user may do. Central permission check in the API layer; UI hides buttons only for comfort. Add tests that call APIs as the wrong role.
Tenant isolation “A shared database is a risk.” One company must never read another company’s data, even with a bug. Tenant id enforced at the data-access layer plus row-level filtering in the database; separate databases only for regulated customers. Isolation tests in the pipeline.
Secrets “Connection strings in config files are rejected.” Secrets must not be in source control, must rotate, and access must be logged. Secrets pulled at runtime from a managed secret store; app identity instead of passwords where possible.
External APIs “You cannot call third parties from the core service.” Outbound calls must be limited, logged, and unable to leak customer data. A small integration layer with an allow-list, timeouts, and field-level filtering of what leaves the system.
File access “Users uploading files directly to storage is rejected.” Files must be scanned, sized, typed, and stored per tenant with access control. Upload via signed short-lived links to a quarantine area; a worker scans and moves to tenant storage; downloads via time-limited links.
THE SENTENCE THAT CHANGES THE MEETING

“Which requirement am I failing, and what are the acceptable ways to meet it?” Security teams almost always have more than one acceptable way. Ask for the menu.

PART 08 — OPERATIONS REJECTS THE ARCHITECTURE

“We cannot support 25 services.”

Operations is the team that gets paged at 3am. If they say the design is too hard to run, that is an architecture defect, not a staffing complaint. Operability is a system quality like performance or security.

Concern Question to evaluate 25 services Modular monolith (3–5 deployables)
Monitoring How many dashboards, alerts, and log streams must a human understand at 3am? 25 dashboards, tracing across hops, noisy alerts. A few dashboards, one trace per request, simpler alerting.
Deployment How many pipelines, versions, and compatibility rules exist at once? 25 pipelines; version drift between services. Few pipelines; one version to reason about.
Incident response When checkout fails, how many teams and systems are in the blast radius? Which of 8 services in the call chain? Who owns it? One codebase; one on-call rotation; faster root cause.
Support effort How many people are needed to keep it healthy — patching, upgrades, certificates? Linear with service count. Much lower; effort goes into one platform.
Operational knowledge Does the team already know how to run this style safely? Needs distributed-systems experience the team may not have. Matches most .NET + SQL teams today.
Infrastructure cost How much is spent on the “glue” — gateways, service discovery, per-service databases? High fixed overhead per service. Low; scale a few processes horizontally.
BAD RESPONSE

Microservices are the industry standard. Operations needs to upskill.

Fashion instead of fit. Ignores the people who will run it.

STRONG ARCHITECT RESPONSE

You are right to push back — 25 services is 25 things to monitor, deploy, and wake up for, and we have a five-person ops team. What I actually need is not 25 services. I need three things: the reporting workload isolated from orders, the ability to scale the order path separately, and independent deploys for the two teams. I can get that with a modular monolith: one codebase, clear module boundaries, three or four deployables, one database with a reporting replica. If a module proves it needs to be its own service, we split that one. Does four deployables fit what you can run?

Validates the concern · Restates the real need · Offers a design that meets the need with less operational load · Keeps a path to split later.

MODULAR MONOLITH INSTEAD OF MICROSERVICES

A modular monolith is one application with strict internal boundaries: each module owns its tables and talks to others through clear interfaces. You get most of the design cleanliness of services with a fraction of the running cost. Choose services when a module has proven it needs different scaling, different release speed, or different ownership — not because a conference said so.

PART 09 — BUSINESS REQUIREMENT CONFLICTS WITH ARCHITECTURE

Business wants A. Architecture needs B. Find C.

Most “conflicts” are not A versus B. They are an unexamined A and an over-specified B. The Architect’s job is to find the version of A that still keeps the property B protects.

Business wants Architecture needs What B is really protecting Middle path
Instant feature delivery — new discount rules live this week. Change should go through design, test, and review. Not breaking pricing for every customer. Ship behind a feature flag to one pilot tenant. Full rollout after a week of real data. Speed and safety.
Fewer login steps — “customers hate MFA”. Security requires additional controls. Account takeover and fraud. Risk-based MFA: skip it on known devices and low-risk actions; require it for payouts, exports, and admin.
Real-time data in every dashboard. Reporting must not slow transactions. Order throughput and database health. Define “real time” per tile. Stock: near-live from a read model. Revenue trends: 5-minute refresh from a replica. Most users cannot tell.
Lower infrastructure cost by 30%. Redundancy for availability. Revenue during a failure. Cut duplication (environments, idle services), keep failover for the database and queue. Tie the second region to a revenue trigger.
Fewer components — “why do we have a queue?” Async processing for spikes and isolation. Orders surviving when email or reporting is slow. Keep one queue; remove the other three tools nobody needs. Explain the queue as “the reason checkout works when email is down”.
One customer wants their own custom logic in the core. Core must stay shared and multi-tenant. Not forking the product per customer. Extension points: configurable rules and webhooks per tenant. Custom code lives outside the core.
HOW TO FIND THE MIDDLE PATH

Ask three questions. What outcome does the business actually want? (not the feature — the outcome). What property is the architecture protecting? (say it in money or risk). Is there a smaller, staged, or scoped version of the ask that keeps the property? There almost always is.

PART 10 — WHEN TWO TEAMS DISAGREE

Team A says RabbitMQ. Team B says Kafka. Do not pick a side.

If you choose immediately, you have chosen a team, not a solution — and the losing team will remember. The Architect changes the question from “which product” to “which requirements”.

  1. Define the actual problem“We need to move order events from the order module to notifications, inventory, and reporting without losing any.” Notice: no product name yet.
  2. Define requirementsMust not lose messages. Must handle a retry without duplicating side effects. Must let a new consumer be added without changing the producer.
  3. Define expected trafficToday 200 messages per second at peak. Growth plan says 5× in two years: 1,000 per second. Not 100,000.
  4. Define message behaviourDo consumers need strict ordering per order id? Is it work-queue style (one consumer takes each job) or broadcast (everyone gets every event)? Both, in different places.
  5. Define retentionDo we need to replay last month’s events to rebuild a report? Or is “deliver once, then gone” enough? Replay is a genuine differentiator.
  6. Define operational constraintsWho will run it? What does the team already know? What does the cloud provider manage for us? What is the on-call reality?
  7. Compare optionsNow score both against the list above — plus the managed cloud option many teams forget. Score with the teams in the room, not in your office.
  8. DecideOften the answer is: the simpler tool the team knows meets every requirement at 1,000 per second, and replay can be solved by keeping events in the database. Sometimes replay and scale genuinely push you to the log-style tool. Either way, the requirements decided — not the loudest engineer.
  9. DocumentADR: context, the requirement table, both options scored, decision, the trigger that would make us revisit (for example, sustained 10,000 messages per second or a replay requirement from Finance).
SAME METHOD, OTHER FIGHTS

SQL vs NoSQL · REST vs gRPC · monolith vs services · build vs buy · this cloud vs that cloud. Every time: problem → requirements → numbers → behaviour → constraints → compare → decide → document. The product is the last word in the sentence, never the first.

PART 11 — EVIDENCE OVER OPINION

“I think this is better” vs “Based on these measurements, this is more suitable”

Opinion is what you say when you have no data. It is fine for the first ten minutes. It is dangerous as a basis for a two-year decision. The Architect’s move is to convert opinion into a cheap test.

Evidence tool What it answers Cost Use when
Proof of concept Can this approach work at all, with our stack and our data? Days. The debate is about feasibility or team learning curve.
Load testing Does it hold at our peak and at 5×? Days, needs an environment. The debate is about scale.
Benchmarking Which of two options is faster or cheaper for our specific workload? Hours to days. Two tools, one clear metric.
Production metrics What does the real system already do? Traffic, error rate, slow queries, cache hit ratio. Almost free. Always start here. Most arguments end when someone opens the dashboard.
Cost estimates What will each option cost per month and per change? Hours. Finance or management is in the room.
Failure testing What happens when this dependency dies? Do we lose data? Days. The debate is about resilience.
Small experiments Ship the small version to one tenant and measure. A sprint. The debate is about user behaviour or business value.

When evidence beats debate

  • The decision is expensive to reverse. Database engine, message platform, tenancy model. Spend a week measuring to save a year regretting.
  • The room is split along team lines. Data is neutral. People can lose to data without losing face.
  • The debate has lasted longer than the test would. If you have argued for three meetings, a two-day benchmark was cheaper.
  • Someone says “everyone knows”. That is the signal to measure.
WHEN EVIDENCE IS NOT ENOUGH

Evidence tells you what happens. It does not tell you what matters. You still need requirements and business priorities to interpret it. “Option B is 20% faster” is useless if nobody needed faster and B costs twice as much to run.

PART 12 — HANDLING STRONG PERSONALITIES

Calm is a skill. Evidence is a shield. Curiosity is the weapon.

Six people who will test you. For each: what a defensive Architect says, what a polite Architect says, and what a leading Architect says.

Senior developer who strongly disagrees

BAD

I have been doing this longer than you. We are going with the service.

BETTER

I understand your concern. Let me explain my reasoning again.

Polite, but “again” means you are repeating, not listening.

ARCHITECT

You clearly feel strongly, so I probably missed something. Walk me through what breaks with my approach. If it is time, let’s size it. If it is risk, let’s name it. If we still disagree after that, I will make the call and own it — but I want your objection in the decision record so we can check who was right in three months.

Manager who pushes a preferred technology

BAD

That tool is wrong for us. I will not use it.

BETTER

It is a good tool. I am not sure it fits this case, but I can look into it.

Avoids the conflict, but leaves the decision floating.

ARCHITECT

Happy to evaluate it seriously. Can we agree the criteria first — must handle our peak, team can run it, fits the budget, meets Security? I will score it against the two other options using the same criteria and bring the table next week. If it wins, I will recommend it. If it loses, you will see exactly where.

Product manager who wants shortcuts

BAD

Every time you do this we end up with a mess I have to clean up.

BETTER

I understand the pressure. Let’s see what we can trim.

ARCHITECT

Let’s split it: what we can skip forever, what we can skip until after launch, and the one or two things that would cost ten times more to add later. I will protect only that last group and give you a date for the middle group. You get the launch; we do not get a rewrite.

Security architect who rejects the design

BAD

Security always says no. This will delay us a month.

BETTER

Can you send me the policy so I can check what we need to change?

ARCHITECT

Thanks for catching this early. Which specific requirement fails — is it the shared database, the secret handling, or the external call? For each I can offer two or three ways to meet it. I would rather you pick from options than we guess. And if there is a residual risk, tell me who needs to accept it so we document it properly.

Operations engineer who challenges complexity

BAD

That is what the platform team is for.

BETTER

Fair point. We can add more monitoring.

Treats a design problem as a tooling problem.

ARCHITECT

If you cannot run it, it does not work — full stop. Show me what a bad night looks like with this design. Then let’s see which parts I can merge or remove without losing the isolation I need. I would rather have four things you can run well than twenty-five you can only watch.

External vendor who recommends their own product

BAD

You are just trying to sell us something.

BETTER

Interesting. Send us the deck and pricing.

ARCHITECT

Here are our five requirements and our current numbers. Show me how your product meets each one, what it costs at 5× our volume, and which of your customers run it at that size with a team our size. If it passes, we will run a two-week proof of concept on our data with our team. That is how we buy.

THE PATTERN

Every strong response does the same four things: acknowledge the person, ask for the specific concern, convert it into criteria or a test, and commit to a next step with a date. Tone stays flat. Emotion stays outside the room.

PART 13 — WHEN YOU ARE WRONG

“I was wrong.” Three words that build more credibility than any diagram.

You will be wrong. The question is only whether people find out from you, or from the outage. An Architect who cannot say “I was wrong” forces the whole organisation to pay for their ego.

How to say it without losing credibility

  1. Say it early and plainly. “The decision I made in March about X was wrong.” Not “mistakes were made”. Not “the team implemented it differently”.
  2. Say what you knew then and what you know now. “I assumed 500 requests per second. Real traffic is 3,000.” This shows the decision was reasonable with the facts you had — and that you update on facts.
  3. Say what it cost. Be honest about impact. People trust people who count their own damage.
  4. Bring the correction. “Here is the new option, the cost to change, and the risk if we do not.” You are not confessing; you are leading the fix.
  5. Say what you will do differently. “Next time I will load-test before committing to a data model.” Turn it into a rule.

WHY CHANGING YOUR MIND IS STRENGTH

A decision is a bet made with the information available at the time. When the information changes and you do not, you are no longer deciding — you are defending. Teams follow Architects who are loyal to the outcome, not to their previous slide. Interviewers listen for a real story where you changed course, what it cost, and what you learned.

PART 14 — ARCHITECTURE DECISION OWNERSHIP

Who actually owns an architecture decision?

The honest answer: the Architect owns the recommendation and the outcome; the organisation owns the decision. Most real decisions need several people to agree, because several people carry the consequences.

Role Owns Must be consulted on Can veto
Architect The recommendation, the trade-off analysis, the decision record, the system qualities, the outcome. Anything crossing team or system boundaries. Designs that break core qualities: data isolation, integrity, resilience — with escalation if overruled.
Technical Lead Design inside the team boundary; delivery of the agreed architecture. Feasibility, effort, team skills. Estimates and internal design.
Engineering Manager People, capacity, hiring, team health. Any decision that changes team load or skills needed. Staffing commitments.
Product Manager Priorities, scope, dates, what the customer gets. Any trade-off that affects features or timeline. Scope and sequence.
Security Security requirements, risk acceptance process, compliance posture. Auth, data handling, external access, secrets, isolation. Designs that violate policy without an accepted risk.
Operations Runbooks, on-call, platform, production standards. Anything that changes what must be run and how it fails. Designs that cannot be operated with current capability.
Business leadership Budget, risk appetite, strategic direction. Major cost, major risk acceptance, strategic vendor choices. Spend and accepted business risk.
WHY COLLABORATION IS NOT WEAKNESS

If you decide alone, you own all the blame and none of the support. If everyone who carries a consequence is part of the decision, the decision survives the first bad week. Collaboration is how a decision becomes durable. The Architect still has to push it to a conclusion — collaboration is not the same as consensus by exhaustion.

PART 15 — ESCALATION

Escalate with facts, not with feelings

Escalation is not tattling. It is moving a decision to the person who actually owns that risk. Done well, it is a sign of maturity. Done badly, it burns every relationship in the room.

Trigger Solve in team? Involve Why
Security risk — a design leaks tenant data under a bug. No. Security first, then management if unresolved. Only Security can accept or reject a security risk.
Compliance issue — data residency, retention, audit. No. Security / compliance + business leadership. Legal exposure is a business decision.
Major cost impact — the plan costs 2× the budget. No. Engineering management + Finance. You do not own the money.
Business-critical reliability risk — checkout has a single point of failure and nobody will fund the fix. Try first. Management, then business leadership with a number. Someone must accept the risk in writing or fund the fix.
Major disagreement with no agreement — after real discussion and evidence. Try twice. Engineering management, with both options written down. A decision must be made; a stalemate is the worst outcome.
Architecture conflicts with company standards — a team wants a tool outside the approved list. Usually yes. Architecture group / standards owner if an exception is needed. Exceptions should be visible and time-boxed.
Local design disagreement — module vs service, naming, patterns. Yes. Nobody above the team. Escalating this destroys trust and wastes leadership time.

How to escalate

BAD ESCALATION

The team refuses to listen and Product is being unreasonable. I need you to tell them to follow the architecture.

Emotion. Blame. Asks the manager to use force. You will win once and lose the team.

ARCHITECT ESCALATION

I need a decision that is above my authority. Context: checkout depends on one database node with no failover. Fact: a node failure means roughly four hours of downtime; at current volume that is about 120k in lost orders. Options: fund a standby now (about 2k a month, two weeks of work), or accept the risk until Q3. The team and Product prefer to wait for delivery reasons; I recommend funding now. Either is defensible — I need you or Finance to choose and record it.

Facts · money · two options · your recommendation · a clear ask · no villains.

THE ESCALATION TEST

Before escalating ask: “Have I had the real conversation with the person I disagree with? Do I have the facts written down? Am I asking for a decision, or for a punishment?” If you cannot answer yes, yes, decision — you are not ready to escalate.

PART 16 — HANDLING ARCHITECTURE SHORTCUTS

“Let’s skip proper validation and fix it later.” The deadline is tomorrow.

Not every shortcut is bad. Some are the smartest thing you can do. The Architect’s job is to classify the shortcut before anyone takes it — because the team will take it anyway if you just say no.

SAFE TEMPORARY SHORTCUT

Reversible, contained, cheap to fix

A manual step instead of automation. A hard-coded limit. A single template. One report run by hand for a week.

Decision: take it. Ticket it. Date it.

TECHNICAL DEBT

Costs more each month it stays

Skipping tests for a module. Duplicated logic. Two modules sharing tables. A queue consumer without idempotency.

Decision: take it only with a named owner, a repayment sprint, and a cost estimate that grows if ignored.

DANGEROUS SHORTCUT

Cheap today, very expensive or impossible to undo

No tenant id in the data model. Storing card data “temporarily”. Skipping input validation on money or file uploads. No backups.

Decision: refuse, and explain the cost of undoing in numbers.

PRODUCTION RISK

Could hurt customers tomorrow

Disabling authorization checks. Untested migration on live data. Removing rate limits. Deploying with no rollback.

Decision: refuse. If overruled, escalate with the risk in writing and a named acceptor.

Applying it to “skip validation”

Ask: validation of what? Skipping validation of an optional description field is a safe shortcut. Skipping validation of quantity and price on an order is a production risk — negative quantities and free orders by tomorrow lunchtime. Skipping validation on file uploads is a security hole. Same word, three very different decisions.

ARCHITECT RESPONSE

Let’s be specific. Which validations? The cosmetic ones — skip them, I will write the ticket. The order amount and tenant checks — those stay, they are two hours and without them we can ship free orders. The file scanning — that stays too; a bad upload is a security incident. So we skip six of nine and ship tonight. Agreed?

What must be documented

  • What the shortcut is and where in the system it lives.
  • Why it was taken — the date, the business reason.
  • The risk while it exists, in plain words.
  • The owner and the repayment date — a ticket in the backlog with priority, not a comment in code.
  • Who accepted the risk if it is more than a safe shortcut.
THE HONEST TRUTH ABOUT “FIX IT LATER”

Later rarely comes on its own. It comes when you put a date, an owner, and a visible ticket on it — and when you, the Architect, bring it back to the planning table with the cost of leaving it.

PART 17 — GOVERNANCE WITHOUT BECOMING A BOTTLENECK

Guide teams. Do not sit between them and every decision.

Governance means keeping the system coherent — same security rules, same data principles, same operability standard — across many teams. Bad governance is an Architect approving every pull request. Good governance is teams making good decisions without asking you.

PRINCIPLESA short list of “we always / we never” statements.

“Every table has a tenant id.” “No service calls another service’s database.” “Every external call has a timeout.” Ten lines. Teams can apply them alone.

STANDARDSConcrete, checkable rules.

Approved authentication method. Logging format. Naming for queues. Standards should be checkable in a pipeline where possible — not in a meeting.

REFERENCE ARCHITECTURE“This is what a normal service looks like here.”

A picture and a template repository. Most teams should be able to start without a design review because they start from the reference.

REUSABLE PATTERNSSolved problems with names.

“Outbox for reliable events.” “Tenant-scoped repository.” “Degraded mode for cache loss.” Teams reach for a pattern instead of reinventing.

ARCHITECTURE REVIEWSOnly for decisions that are expensive to reverse or cross boundaries.

New data store, new integration, new tenancy model, new external dependency. Not for “which library for JSON”.

ADRsArchitecture Decision Records — one page each.

Teams write their own. You read them. Context, options, decision, consequences. The record makes the decision reviewable without a meeting.

TEAM AUTONOMYInside the boundary, the team decides.

Your job is to make the boundary clear and the guardrails strong enough that autonomy is safe.

THE TEST OF GOOD GOVERNANCE

If you went on holiday for three weeks, would good decisions still get made? If yes, your governance works. If everything stops, you are not governing — you are a bottleneck with a title. Good governance should guide teams, not slow every team down.

PART 18 — ARCHITECTURE REVIEW MEETING

A 30-minute review that ends with a decision

Most architecture reviews fail because they are presentations. A good review is a structured argument that ends in a written decision. Send the document two days before. The meeting is for questions, not for reading.

Min Agenda What the Architect asks
0–2 Problem “In one sentence, what business problem does this solve? Who is hurt if we do nothing?”
2–5 Requirements “What are the numbers — users, volume, latency, availability? What is non-negotiable?”
5–10 Proposed solution “Walk me through one request end to end. Where is the data owned? What is new versus reused?”
10–13 Alternatives “What else did you consider and why did you reject it? What is the simplest thing that could work?”
13–17 Risks “What breaks first? What happens when the dependency is down? What is hard to reverse?”
17–20 Security “How is tenant data isolated? Where are secrets? What leaves the system?”
20–23 Cost “Monthly run cost? People to run it? What does it cost at 5×?”
23–26 Open questions “What do we not know yet? Which of those blocks the decision and which can be answered after?”
26–29 Decision “Approved, approved with conditions, or not yet? Say the conditions out loud.”
29–30 Action items “Who does what by when. Who writes the ADR today?”

PRACTICAL EXAMPLE — “ADD A NOTIFICATION SERVICE”

Problem: customers do not get order confirmations reliably; support tickets up 30%. Requirements: 20k emails a day, delivered within 5 minutes, never lost, 40 tenants with their own templates. Proposal: a notification module reading events from the existing queue, storing delivery state, retrying with backoff, one provider. Alternatives: a full separate service (rejected: no separate scaling need), a third-party notification platform (deferred: cost and data-sharing review). Risks: provider outage — mitigated by queue retention and a status page; duplicate sends — mitigated by idempotency on order id + template. Security: templates per tenant; no customer data to provider beyond email and order number; API key in the secret store. Cost: ~150 a month provider fees, one engineer for three weeks. Open: SMS later? Not blocking. Decision: approved with condition — delivery-state table must include tenant id and retention rule. Actions: Tech Lead writes ADR today; Security reviews provider contract by Friday.

THE ARCHITECT’S ROLE IN THE ROOM

You are not the judge. You are the person making sure every question above gets an honest answer, that quiet people speak, and that the meeting ends with a decision and an owner — not with “let’s take it offline”.

PART 19 — DIFFICULT STAKEHOLDER SCENARIOS

Ten rooms you will walk into

Open each one. Say your response out loud before you read the recommended one.

SITUATION

You proposed a separate inventory service with its own database. The developer says one module and one database is enough and faster to build.

WHAT NOT TO DO

Quote “best practice”. Pull rank. Agree just to avoid conflict without checking the qualities you were protecting.

QUESTIONS TO ASK

What is the expected growth of inventory traffic vs the rest? Does inventory need separate release cadence? Can we keep a clean boundary inside the app? What would trigger a split later?

RECOMMENDED RESPONSE

“You may be right. If we keep a strict module boundary — own tables, own interface — we get most of the benefit at a fraction of the cost. Let’s start there and write down the trigger for splitting.”

POSSIBLE DECISION

Module with an enforced boundary; ADR records the split trigger (for example, inventory traffic above 40% of total or a second team owning it).

SITUATION

Launch is in six weeks. Your plan needs nine. Product says “cut the architecture”.

WHAT NOT TO DO

Say “it is all necessary”. Or silently drop tenant isolation to hit the date.

QUESTIONS TO ASK

What is driving the date? Which customers are in the first release? What is the smallest scope that still wins the deal? What can ship two weeks after launch without anyone noticing?

RECOMMENDED RESPONSE

“Here are three buckets: must-keep (four days), post-launch (two weeks), and accepted debt with tickets. That gets us to six weeks. The must-keep items are the ones that cost ten times more to add later.”

POSSIBLE DECISION

Reduced scope launch; two must-have architecture items kept; documented debt with a repayment sprint in the plan.

SITUATION

Your design costs 18k a month. The CFO wants under 10k.

WHAT NOT TO DO

Argue that quality costs money. Or cut backups and failover to hit the number.

QUESTIONS TO ASK

Is the limit monthly, annual, or total? What does an hour of downtime cost the business? Which environments are truly needed? Which components are duplication?

RECOMMENDED RESPONSE

“Here is a 9k version. I removed the second region, merged services, shrank staging. I kept backups, standby, and isolation because they protect revenue. Here is what we accept: longer regional recovery. Here is the revenue trigger to add the region back.”

POSSIBLE DECISION

Simplified architecture approved; risk of longer regional recovery accepted in writing by the CFO; trigger documented.

SITUATION

Security rejects your shared-database multi-tenant model.

WHAT NOT TO DO

Treat it as politics. Complain to Product. Rebuild everything as separate databases without asking what the requirement is.

QUESTIONS TO ASK

Which requirement fails — isolation, auditability, encryption? Is row-level enforcement plus tests acceptable? Which customers need physical separation by contract?

RECOMMENDED RESPONSE

“Let me offer three ways to meet the isolation requirement: enforced tenant filter plus database row-level security plus automated isolation tests; a separate database per regulated tenant; or full separation for all. I recommend the first with the second for regulated customers. Which meets the policy?”

POSSIBLE DECISION

Hybrid model accepted; isolation tests required in the pipeline; residual risk recorded and signed by Security.

SITUATION

Ops says they cannot run 25 services with five people.

WHAT NOT TO DO

Say it is a platform problem. Promise “better tooling” instead of changing the design.

QUESTIONS TO ASK

What does a bad night look like today? How many deployables can you run well? Which boundaries do I really need, and which are habit?

RECOMMENDED RESPONSE

“If you cannot run it, it is not done. I need three isolations, not 25 services. Modular monolith, four deployables, one on-call. We split a module only when it proves the need.”

POSSIBLE DECISION

Modular monolith with 3–4 deployables; a documented rule for when a module may become a service.

SITUATION

Sales wants a cross-company benchmark report: “show each customer how they compare to others”. That means one tenant’s data visible to another.

WHAT NOT TO DO

Say “impossible”. Or build it because Sales is loud.

QUESTIONS TO ASK

What is the customer really asking — to see others, or to know if they are doing well? Do our contracts allow aggregated, anonymised use? What minimum group size prevents identifying a company?

RECOMMENDED RESPONSE

“We cannot show one company another company’s data — that breaks contracts and trust. We can show anonymised industry averages built from groups of at least ten companies, with legal approval. That gives the customer the answer they want without the risk.”

POSSIBLE DECISION

Aggregated benchmark feature with legal sign-off and minimum group size; raw cross-tenant access rejected and recorded.

SITUATION

A vendor tells your CTO their platform will “solve scalability” for 200k a year.

WHAT NOT TO DO

Dismiss it as sales. Or accept because the CTO seemed keen.

QUESTIONS TO ASK

Which of our actual problems does it solve? What does it cost at 5×? Who runs it at our size with our team size? What is the exit cost?

RECOMMENDED RESPONSE

“I will evaluate it against the same criteria as the other options and run a two-week proof of concept on our data. If it wins on our numbers, I will recommend it.”

POSSIBLE DECISION

Time-boxed PoC with written success criteria; decision by scorecard, not by demo.

SITUATION

A respected senior wants to introduce a new database engine they used at a previous company.

WHAT NOT TO DO

Say no because “we use SQL here”. Or say yes to keep them happy.

QUESTIONS TO ASK

Which requirement does the current engine fail? Who else can operate the new one? What is the cost of two engines — backups, skills, on-call? Can we test on one bounded use case?

RECOMMENDED RESPONSE

“Show me the requirement SQL cannot meet. If there is one, let’s pilot the new engine on that one bounded problem with a run-cost estimate. If there is not, adding an engine is cost without benefit — and I will say that in writing so it is not personal.”

POSSIBLE DECISION

Either a scoped pilot with exit criteria, or a documented “not now” with the trigger that would reopen it.

SITUATION

Orders team designed events one way; Inventory team designed them another. Both are half built.

WHAT NOT TO DO

Pick the team you like. Let them fight it out. Design a third thing nobody built.

QUESTIONS TO ASK

What does each consumer need from the event? What is the cost to change each side? Which design handles the next two known use cases?

RECOMMENDED RESPONSE

“Let’s put the requirements on one page and score both designs together. Whichever wins, the other team gets help migrating. Then we write the event standard so this does not happen a third time.”

POSSIBLE DECISION

One design chosen by criteria; migration support; new event standard added to governance.

SITUATION

The CTO says in a leadership meeting: “I do not think this will scale to 5×.”

WHAT NOT TO DO

Get defensive. Agree instantly to look agreeable. Bury them in diagrams.

QUESTIONS TO ASK

Which part worries you — database, queue, the tenancy model? What number does 5× mean to you? What evidence would make you comfortable?

RECOMMENDED RESPONSE

“That is the right question. The database is the first thing to hit a wall — my model says at about 3.5× on the current instance. I have a plan: read replica at 2×, reporting split at 3×, partitioning at 4×. I will load-test the 5× path next sprint and bring you the numbers. If they are wrong, I will change the design.”

POSSIBLE DECISION

Architecture approved with a load-test condition; scaling triggers documented.

PART 20 — ARCHITECT COMMUNICATION FRAMEWORK

Eight lines for any difficult conversation

When the room gets hot, structure keeps you calm. Fill these eight lines before the meeting, and speak them in order.

FactsWhat do we know? Numbers, incidents, measurements. No adjectives.
RequirementWhat must the business achieve? In business words.
ConstraintWhat limits us? Time, money, people, law, existing systems.
OptionsWhat choices exist? At least two. Include “do nothing”.
Trade-offWhat do we gain and lose with each?
RiskWhat could go wrong, how likely, how bad?
DecisionWhat are we choosing? Say it in one sentence.
Next stepWhat happens now, who, by when, and how we will measure it.

EXAMPLE 1 — SEPARATE SERVICE OR MODULE?

Facts: Inventory is 12% of traffic today; changes twice a week; the rest changes monthly. One team owns both. Requirement: ship the new inventory features in six weeks; do not slow order releases. Constraint: five developers, ops team of five, no distributed-tracing experience yet. Options: (A) separate service; (B) module with enforced boundary; (C) module with no boundary. Trade-off: A isolates releases but adds ops load and latency; B gives boundary at low cost, split later possible; C is fastest but becomes a tangle. Risk: B — boundary erodes if not enforced; mitigate with architecture tests. Decision: B. Next step: Tech Lead sets up module boundary tests this sprint; ADR written; revisit if inventory traffic passes 40% or a second team takes ownership.

EXAMPLE 2 — SECURITY REJECTS SHARED DATABASE

Facts: 40 tenants; two are in regulated industries; one isolation bug in the last year, caught in test. Requirement: no tenant can ever read another’s data; regulated tenants need contractual separation. Constraint: 40 separate databases would triple database cost and ops load. Options: (A) shared DB with enforced filters and row-level security; (B) database per tenant; (C) hybrid — shared for most, separate for regulated. Trade-off: A cheapest, one bug away from exposure; B safest, most expensive; C balances. Risk: C — routing complexity; mitigate with a single tenant-resolution layer and automated isolation tests. Decision: C. Next step: Security reviews the isolation test suite; ADR with residual risk signed; quarterly isolation audit.

EXAMPLE 3 — FINANCE WANTS 30% LOWER COST

Facts: 18k a month; 30% is idle — a full-size staging and a second region with 0.5% traffic. Requirement: cut to under 12.6k without raising business risk beyond what Finance will accept. Constraint: 99.9% on checkout stays. Options: (A) shrink staging, drop second region, keep standby DB — 11k; (B) also drop standby — 9k; (C) do nothing. Trade-off: A meets target, regional outage becomes hours; B cheaper but a database failure becomes a multi-hour data-loss risk; C misses target. Risk: A — regional event; mitigate with tested restore and a revenue trigger to re-add region. Decision: A. Next step: Finance signs regional-recovery risk; ops runs a restore drill this month; review at 100k daily revenue.

PART 21 — TECHNICAL ARCHITECT INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

Thirty questions. Only leadership and conflict.

The interviewer is not testing what you know. They are testing how you behave when someone disagrees with you. Every strong answer contains: listen, ask, evidence, trade-off, decision, ownership.

TESTING

Ego control. Curiosity. Can you be challenged without pulling rank?

WEAK ANSWER

“I explain my reasoning and if they still disagree, the architect decides.”

STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER

“I assume they know something I do not. I ask what breaks with my approach and what they would do instead. We restate the problem and the constraints together, then compare the options on evidence — traffic, cost, risk, reversibility. Often I change my mind or we find a third option. If we still disagree, I decide, record their objection in the ADR, and set a trigger to review. I have changed my design because of a developer more than once, and I say so.”

COMMON MISTAKE

Presenting “I listened, then I decided” as if the listening was a formality.

TESTING

Humility and diagnosis. Do you ask why, or do you push harder?

WEAK ANSWER

“I would get management to back the decision.”

STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER

“If a whole team rejects it, either the design is wrong, or I explained it badly, or I did not involve them early enough. All three are my problem. I would go back to the problem statement with them, find out which quality they think I am over-protecting, and rebuild the options together. A design the team does not believe in will not be built well anyway.”

COMMON MISTAKE

Treating rejection as a communication failure only, never as a design failure.

TESTING

Business empathy. Can you translate architecture into delivery language?

WEAK ANSWER

“Product does not understand technical debt; I would explain the long-term risks.”

STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER

“Product usually disagrees with time and cost, not with architecture. I split the work into must-have, post-launch, and accepted debt, with days for each. I protect only the things that are very expensive to add later and I say why in money. Product gets the date; the system keeps its spine.”

COMMON MISTAKE

Positioning Product as the opponent.

TESTING

Do you separate requirement from suggested solution? Do you respect the risk owner?

WEAK ANSWER

“I would follow whatever Security says.”

STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER

“I ask which requirement fails, then offer two or three ways to meet it with cost and delivery impact. Security owns the requirement; I own the design that meets it. If a residual risk remains, we document who accepts it. I involve Security at design time so this is a conversation, not a rejection at the end.”

COMMON MISTAKE

Either fighting Security or surrendering without exploring alternatives.

TESTING

Cost literacy. Can you simplify without breaking core qualities?

WEAK ANSWER

“Good architecture is an investment.”

STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER

“I bring three costed versions. I cut duplication first, insurance second, and never the things that protect data and money. I state what risk we accept with the cheaper version, in hours of downtime and revenue, and I give a trigger for when to add capacity back. Management chooses with eyes open.”

COMMON MISTAKE

Talking about infrastructure cost only, ignoring people and failure cost.

TESTING

Honesty. Ownership. Do you have a real story?

WEAK ANSWER

“I try to make decisions that cannot be wrong by gathering enough data.”

STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER

“I say it plainly and early: what I assumed, what turned out to be true, what it cost, and the correction with numbers. Then I fix how I decide — for example, adding write load to every proof of concept. A decision is a bet on the information available. Updating it when evidence changes is the job. I can give you an example where I reversed a service split I had pushed for.”

COMMON MISTAKE

Claiming you have never been wrong, or telling a story where the “mistake” was someone else’s.

TESTING

Judgement. Do you know which quality is non-negotiable?

WEAK ANSWER

“It is always a balance; we try to find the middle.”

STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER

“I do not balance them evenly. I decide which qualities are irreversible for this system — data isolation, payment correctness, backups — and protect those completely. Everything else I trade for speed openly, with a ticket and a date. Speed on reversible things, rigour on irreversible ones.”

COMMON MISTAKE

Treating all quality as equal, so either everything slows down or nothing is protected.

TESTING

Do you treat debt as a managed decision or a moral failing?

WEAK ANSWER

“We should avoid technical debt.”

STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER

“Debt is fine when it is chosen, visible, and priced. Each item has an owner, a ticket, and a cost that grows if ignored. I bring the top debt items to planning with business impact — slower releases, incident risk — so Product can prioritise them like features. Hidden debt is the problem; declared debt is a tool.”

COMMON MISTAKE

Asking for a “debt sprint” with no business case.

TESTING

Neutral process. Can you avoid picking a team?

WEAK ANSWER

“I would choose the one I have more experience with.”

STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER

“I change the question from ‘which product’ to ‘which requirements’. Problem, requirements, traffic, message behaviour, retention, operational constraints — written with both teams. Then we score the options together. The criteria decide, and the losing team can see why without losing face. I document it with the trigger that would reopen it.”

COMMON MISTAKE

Splitting the difference (“use both”) to avoid deciding.

TESTING

Scalable leadership. Do you know the difference between guiding and gatekeeping?

WEAK ANSWER

“I review all designs quickly so I do not block anyone.”

STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER

“I put the rules where teams can use them without me: ten principles, checkable standards, a reference architecture, named patterns, and team-written ADRs. Reviews only for expensive-to-reverse or cross-boundary decisions. My test: if I disappeared for three weeks, would good decisions still get made?”

COMMON MISTAKE

Describing yourself as the approval step for everything.

TESTING

Influence. Most Architects have no direct reports.

WEAK ANSWER

“I build relationships and explain things well.”

STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER

“I speak in their currency. To Product: dates and scope. To Finance: monthly cost and failure cost. To Security: controls and residual risk. To Ops: what a bad night looks like. I bring options, not one answer, and I make the trade-offs visible so they are choosing, not obeying. Evidence is the authority I do have.”

COMMON MISTAKE

Relying on charisma or seniority instead of translation and evidence.

TESTING

Courage with respect. Can you say “here is the risk” upward?

WEAK ANSWER

“I do what leadership decides; they have the full picture.”

STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER

“I give them a decision, not a complaint: facts, options, my recommendation, and the risk of each path in business terms. If they choose the riskier path, that is their right — I make sure the risk is written down with a name on it, and I build the mitigations I can. I do not sulk, and I do not silently do something different.”

COMMON MISTAKE

Either caving completely or being seen as the person who always says no.

TESTING

Judgement about ownership of risk.

WEAK ANSWER

“When the team will not listen.”

STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER

SUGGESTED ARCHITECT APPROACH

1. Conflicts. Dev (more services) vs Ops (fewer). Product (speed) vs Security (extra controls) and vs any re-architecture. Finance (−30%) vs Management (5×) vs Security (isolation costs). Reporting vs transactions is not a conflict — it is a shared pain everyone can agree to fix first.

2. Facts vs opinions. Facts: reporting slows orders (measurable); one isolation near-miss; current cost; current traffic; team size. Opinions: “the monolith slows us down” (why — build time, deploy coupling, ownership?), “microservices will fix it”, “we cannot run more things” (how many can you run?), “5× growth” (a forecast — plan for it, do not pre-pay for it).

3. Business priorities. Ask leadership to rank: (1) do not lose or leak customer data, (2) keep checkout fast and available, (3) deliver features to the three waiting customers, (4) cost, (5) growth readiness. Most companies rank close to this. Write it down; it settles half the arguments.

4. Options. (A) Full microservices — meets Dev’s wish, fails Ops, Finance, and Product this year. (B) Keep monolith as is, fix nothing — fails everyone soon. (C) Modular monolith: enforce module boundaries inside the app, move reporting to a read replica, add row-level tenant isolation plus isolation tests, separate the order path as the one deployable that scales independently, cut idle infrastructure. (D) C plus extracting one service where a proven need exists (for example reporting/export as its own worker pool).

5. Recommendation. Option C now, D when a module proves the need. It addresses the shared fact (reporting) first, gives Dev the boundaries they actually want, gives Ops few deployables, gives Security enforced isolation, gives Finance savings by removing duplication, and gives Management a scaling path with triggers.

6. Trade-offs. Dev does not get independent services yet — they get independent modules and a clear split rule. Security gets logical isolation plus separate databases only for regulated tenants, not full separation. Finance gets about 20–30% this year, not by removing failover. Product loses roughly two sprints to the reporting split and isolation work, and gains faster delivery after because releases are not fighting the database.

7. Handling disagreements. Dev: “What specifically is slow — build, deploy, ownership? Let’s fix that inside the monolith first and measure.” Ops: “How many deployables can you run well? Design to that.” Security: “Which requirement, and which of these three options meets it?” Finance: “Here are three costed versions and the risk each accepts.” Product: “Two sprints now, and here is the delivery speed after.” Management: “Here are the scaling triggers and the tests that prove them.”

8. What happens first. Week 1–2: reporting to a replica (removes today’s pain, wins credibility with everyone). Week 3–6: tenant isolation enforcement and tests (closes the Security risk). In parallel: module boundaries with architecture tests; cut idle environments and the unused components. Then: load test to 5× and set triggers.

9. Documentation. One ADR per decision: modular monolith over microservices; reporting on replica; hybrid tenant isolation; cost reductions and accepted risks; scaling triggers. Each with dissenting views recorded.

10. Success measures. Order p95 latency during reporting hours back to baseline within 4 weeks. Zero isolation test failures; Security sign-off. Infrastructure cost down 25–30% by quarter end. Release frequency up (measure lead time). Load test passes 3× within the quarter, with a written plan to 5×.

1. Real problem. Not “microservices or not”. The real problem: win two enterprise deals in four months and be allowed to onboard them — which requires isolation and audit the CISO will sign — and not build something 3 ops people cannot run. Scaling to 5× is a two-year problem; it must not be solved in a way that kills the four-month one.

2. Clarifying questions. What exactly do the two buyers need in month four — full workflow or a defined approval path? Is the month-end 10× invoice peak already hurting, or forecast? What did the CISO mean by “proven” — tests, audit, certification? Which of Engineering’s pain points are real today: build time, deploy coupling, incidents? What return does the CFO measure — deal revenue, cost avoided?

3. Stakeholders. The five in the room, plus the two seniors, the 3 ops engineers, the two enterprise buyers’ security teams, and the existing 60 customers who must not be disrupted.

4. Facts vs opinions. Facts: 4-month deadline; isolation and audit are contractual; 3 ops; 15% budget; team skill profile; current integrations are fragile; month-end peaks exist. Opinions: “we will never scale without microservices”; “no rewrites ever”; “twenty services” (nobody proposed a number yet). Turn “never scale” into a load-test question.

5. Decision criteria (ranked). (1) Isolation and immutable audit that the CISO signs. (2) Approvals shipped in 4 months. (3) Operable by 3 people. (4) Within 15% infra growth. (5) A credible, staged path to 5× companies and 10× invoice peaks. (6) Team skill fit.

6. Options. (A) Event-driven microservices rewrite — fails criteria 2, 3, 4, 6. (B) Build approvals inside the current monolith with no structural change — meets 2, fails 1 and 5. (C) Modular monolith: enforce module boundaries; approvals as a new module; tenant isolation via scoped access + row-level security + tests, separate databases for the two regulated buyers; audit trail as an append-only store (write-once table or append-only log) with retention; invoice processing moved to a background worker pool fed by one queue to absorb month-end peaks; reporting to a replica. (D) C plus extracting audit as the one separate deployable, since immutability and retention differ from everything else.

7. Resolving disagreements. Head of Engineering: “Which pain is real today? Let’s measure build and deploy coupling; boundaries fix most of it, and I have written split triggers for when a service earns its keep.” Product: “Approvals ships as a module in the same app — no rewrite — and the isolation work is what lets you onboard the buyers you are chasing.” CISO: “Three layers plus tests plus physical separation for regulated buyers, and an append-only audit store; you review the test suite and sign the residual risk.” CFO: “15% covers a replica, a queue, and one worker pool; the return is two enterprise contracts we cannot sign otherwise; here is the number.” Ops: “Two new things — a queue and a worker pool — plus a replica you already know how to run. You sign off every new deployable.”

8. Recommendation. Option C, with D (separate audit deployable) only if the audit store’s operational profile genuinely differs — decided in month two with Ops.

9. Trade-offs. Engineering does not get services now; they get boundaries, a queue, and a rule. Product loses about three weeks to isolation and audit work in the first two months. CISO gets logical isolation for most tenants, not physical for all. CFO spends the full 15%. Ops takes on two new components with sign-off rights. Scaling beyond ~4× may require partitioning or an extraction later — planned, not pre-paid.

10. Risks. Boundaries erode (mitigate: architecture tests, ADRs, Ops veto on new deployables). Month-end peak overwhelms the worker pool (mitigate: load test at 10× invoices in month two; queue absorbs bursts). Team weak on messaging (mitigate: one queue, one pattern, a senior paired on it; no exotic features). CISO does not accept row-level model (mitigate: agree the criteria in week one, not month four). Deadline slips (mitigate: approvals scope defined as the buyers’ must-have path, flags for the rest).

11. Action plan. Week 1: criteria and priority ranking signed by the five; CISO agrees isolation acceptance criteria; approvals must-have scope agreed with buyers. Weeks 2–5: isolation layers, audit store, boundary tests. Weeks 3–8: approvals module behind flags. Weeks 6–9: queue + worker pool for invoices; reporting to replica; load test at 10× invoices. Weeks 10–14: buyer security review, pilot with one buyer, hardening. Week 16: go-live. Month 5: review split triggers with Engineering and Ops.

12. Communicating the decision. One page, sent before a 30-minute meeting: the ranked criteria, the four options scored against them, the recommendation, the trade-off each stakeholder accepts, the risks with owners, and the plan. Then separate 15-minute conversations with the Head of Engineering and the CISO, because they carry the biggest changes of mind. Record dissent in the ADR. Follow up monthly with the measures: isolation tests green, approvals progress, page count, infra cost, load-test results. Decisions people helped make, and can see measured, survive.

Honest self-check — did I lead, or did I win?

  • I defended before I understood — I answered the objection before I asked about it.
  • I used my title — “as the Architect…” appeared in my answer.
  • I offered one option — an order dressed as a decision.
  • No numbers — I said “faster”, “risky”, “expensive” with no figure.
  • I treated Security or Ops as a blocker — instead of a risk owner.
  • I skipped the trade-off — I did not say what each person gives up.
  • I could not name my design’s weakest point — the CTO had to find it.
  • I had no trigger — no measured condition to revisit the decision.
  • I would not have written it down — no ADR, no dissent recorded.
  • I could not say “I was wrong” — I had no story of changing course.
DAY 27 — CLOSE

20 rules for architecture leadership

Short enough to remember in the meeting. Hard enough to practise for years.

01

Understand before defending.

The first minute of a disagreement is for listening. If you are preparing your reply, you are not listening.

02

Ask why before choosing how.

Most fights about tools are fights about unstated requirements.

03

Use evidence when opinions conflict.

A two-day test ends a three-week argument. People can lose to data without losing face.

04

Do not confuse authority with leadership.

“Because I am the Architect” wins today and loses the team for a year.

05

Never present one option.

One option is an order. Two or three with trade-offs is a decision.

06

Explain trade-offs openly.

Say what each person gives up. Hidden costs become betrayals later.

07

Speak in their currency.

Dates to Product. Money to Finance. Controls to Security. Bad nights to Ops.

08

Protect the irreversible. Trade the rest.

Isolation, integrity, backups — hold the line. Everything else is negotiable, and say so first.

09

Security and Ops are risk owners, not blockers.

Ask which requirement fails and offer a menu of ways to meet it.

10

Change your decision when evidence changes.

Loyalty to the outcome, not to your previous slide.

11

Say “I was wrong” early and plainly.

What you assumed, what is true, what it cost, what changes. Then lead the fix.

12

Classify every shortcut before someone takes it.

Safe, debt, dangerous, or production risk. Same word, four decisions.

13

Write it down.

One-page decision records, with dissent included. Memory is not governance.

14

Every decision gets a trigger.

A number and a date that tell you when to revisit — so it is not a feeling later.

15

Escalate for decisions, never for punishment.

Facts, options, your recommendation, a clear ask. No villains.

16

Guide teams. Do not stand between them and every decision.

If good decisions stop when you go on holiday, you are a bottleneck with a title.

17

Neutral process beats picking a team.

When two teams disagree, change the question from “which product” to “which requirements”.

18

Own the outcome, share the credit.

When it works, it was the team. When it fails at the system level, it was your call.

19

Calm is a decision.

Flat tone, specific questions, a next step with a date. Emotion stays outside the room.

20

Protect the business, not your ego.

The goal is the best decision for the system and the company — not the best score for you.

ONE-MINUTE INTERVIEW ANSWER

“How do you handle disagreement with a senior developer about an architecture decision?”

I start by assuming they know something I do not — they are closer to the code and usually to the pain. So I ask what specifically breaks with my approach and what they would do instead, and I make sure we are solving the same problem with the same constraints. Then we compare the options on evidence rather than preference: traffic, cost, risk, how hard each is to reverse. Quite often that changes my mind, or we find a third option — for example, a module with an enforced boundary instead of a separate service, with a written trigger for splitting later. If we still disagree after a real conversation, I make the call, because someone has to, and I record their objection in the decision record so we can check who was right in three months. I have reversed my own designs because of a developer, and I say so openly — that is what makes people keep telling me the truth.

ONE POWERFUL ARCHITECT STATEMENT

An Architect who needs to win the argument will eventually be the last to know the truth. An Architect who helps the room reach the best decision for the business and the system will never be short of people willing to tell it.

Day 27 is over when you can hear “your design is wrong” and your first reaction is a question, not a defence. Tomorrow’s architecture will be better for it.
