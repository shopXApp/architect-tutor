PART 1 · THE FOUNDATION

Three Ways to Build the Same System

Before any architecture decision, we must agree on what the three options actually mean. These are not buzzwords — they are three different ways of organizing the same code and the same data.

1 · What is a monolith?

A monolith is one big application that does everything. One codebase. One deploy. One database. All the business logic — customer, product, order, payment — lives inside the same box. If you change something, you redeploy the whole thing. If one part crashes, the whole thing may stop.

Think of it like a small restaurant where the same person is the chef, the waiter, the cashier, and the cleaner. It works when the restaurant is small. It breaks when the restaurant gets busy.

2 · What is a modular monolith?

A modular monolith is still one deployable application, but the code inside is organized into clear business areas. The customer module does not touch the payment module's tables. Each module has its own responsibility. They talk through clean internal boundaries, not by reading each other's data directly.

Same restaurant, but now the kitchen is separate from the dining area, and the cashier has their own station. Still one building. Still one team. But everyone knows where their job ends and the next person's begins.

3 · What is a microservice?

A microservice is one small application that does one business job, deployed independently, with its own database. The Order Service handles orders. The Payment Service handles payments. They are separate processes, separate deploys, separate teams — and they talk over the network.

Now imagine three different restaurants: one only cooks, one only takes orders, one only delivers. Each can change its menu without asking the others. But they have to coordinate — and coordination costs.

4 · The main difference

MONOLITHOne deploy, one database, all code together. Simple to run. Hard to scale independently.
MODULAR MONOLITHOne deploy, one database, but clear business modules inside. A middle path — organized without being distributed.
MICROSERVICESMany deploys, many databases, each service owns its job. Maximum flexibility, maximum operational cost.

5 · Why did microservices become popular?

Three things happened at the same time: large companies like Amazon and Netflix talked about them; cloud platforms made it cheaper to run many small servers; and large engineering teams (100+ developers) hit real problems that only independent deploys could solve. The pattern worked for them — so many others assumed it would work for everyone.

6 · What problems do microservices solve?

  • Big teams blocking each other. When 100 developers share one deploy, every release needs coordination. Microservices let each team ship on its own schedule.
  • One part needing more power than the others. If the payment service gets slammed but the reporting service is idle, microservices let you scale only the busy one.
  • One failure breaking everything. In a monolith, a bug in the notification code may crash the order page. In microservices, notification fails — orders keep working.
  • Technology lock-in. Different services can use different languages, different databases, different frameworks — if it really helps.

7 · What new problems do microservices create?

  • Network instead of function calls. Two functions used to call each other in a millisecond. Now they talk over the network — slower, and the network can fail.
  • Data consistency. In a monolith, one database transaction makes everything consistent. In microservices, data is spread across services. Keeping it consistent is hard.
  • Operational complexity. You now deploy, monitor, log, and debug 20 things instead of 1. Each can fail in its own way.
  • Debugging is harder. When something breaks, you have to trace it across multiple services and their logs.
  • Cost. More servers, more infrastructure, more tooling, more cloud bills.
THE ONE SENTENCE

Microservices trade operational complexity for team and scale independence. If you don't need that trade, you pay the complexity for no benefit. If you do need it, the complexity is the price of solving a real problem.

PART 2 · THE MOST IMPORTANT QUESTION

Why Not Every Application Should Use Microservices

This is the most important section of the day. The architect who can confidently say "we should not use microservices for this" — and explain why — is more valuable than the architect who always says yes.

Reason 1 · The application is small

SITUATIONAn internal tool used by 50 employees. Simple forms, simple reports, low traffic.
WHY IT'S BADMicroservices add infrastructure, network calls, and operational overhead to a problem that a single application solves in a day.
BETTER ALTERNATIVEA simple monolith. One deploy. Done.
ARCHITECT THINKS"Does this problem even need an architect?" If the answer is no, don't add architectural complexity.

Reason 2 · The team is small

SITUATION3 to 5 developers maintaining the whole system.
WHY IT'S BADMicroservices are designed for many teams working independently. One small team managing 20 services is a disaster — every service is a context switch, a deploy, a monitoring dashboard.
BETTER ALTERNATIVEModular monolith. Clean code, clear modules, one deploy.
ARCHITECT THINKS"How many services can this team maintain?" A rough rule: one service per 5–10 developers. With 5 developers, that's one service. Full stop.

Reason 3 · Traffic is low

SITUATION100 users a day. Peaks of 10 concurrent.
WHY IT'S BADIndependent scaling only matters when something needs more power than the rest. With low traffic, a single server handles everything with room to spare.
BETTER ALTERNATIVEMonolith on one small server. Costs almost nothing.
ARCHITECT THINKS"Am I solving a scaling problem that doesn't exist yet?" Solve it when traffic makes it real.

Reason 4 · Business rules are simple

SITUATIONA to-do list app. A blog. A simple inventory tracker.
WHY IT'S BADMicroservices shine when business logic is complex and distributed across many domains. Simple rules don't need distributed logic.
BETTER ALTERNATIVEMonolith. Keep everything in one place.
ARCHITECT THINKS"How many business domains does this system have?" One or two? Monolith. Five or more, with different scaling needs? Maybe microservices.

Reason 5 · Budget is limited

SITUATIONEarly-stage company. Tight budget. Need to ship fast.
WHY IT'S BADMicroservices cost more — more servers, more infrastructure, more monitoring, more engineering time. A small company cannot afford that.
BETTER ALTERNATIVEModular monolith. Ship fast, stay cheap, split later if needed.
ARCHITECT THINKS"Can we afford to maintain this in six months?" If the answer is no, don't build it today.

Reason 6 · Product is early stage

SITUATIONNew startup. Product-market fit not yet found. Business model may change in three months.
WHY IT'S BADMicroservices lock you into your first domain boundaries. If the business changes — and it will — you have 20 services that need to change together. A monolith can pivot in a day.
BETTER ALTERNATIVEMonolith. Move fast, change direction freely, split when the business stabilizes.
ARCHITECT THINKS"Do we even know what the business domains are yet?" If the answer is no, don't draw hard boundaries around them.

Reason 7 · Low operational maturity

SITUATIONNo DevOps team. No CI/CD pipeline. No monitoring. Manual deploys.
WHY IT'S BADMicroservices demand strong operational foundations: automated deploys, service discovery, distributed tracing, centralized logging. Without these, microservices are a nightmare.
BETTER ALTERNATIVEModular monolith. Build operational maturity first. Migrate later.
ARCHITECT THINKS"Can we even deploy one service reliably?" If the answer is no, don't try to deploy 20.

Reason 8 · No need for independent scaling

SITUATIONAll parts of the system grow at roughly the same rate.
WHY IT'S BADIndependent scaling is one of the main reasons for microservices. If everything scales together, one server scaling horizontally is enough.
BETTER ALTERNATIVEMonolith with read replicas if needed. Scale the whole thing.
ARCHITECT THINKS"Does one part of this system get 10× more traffic than the rest?" If yes, maybe split it. If no, stay together.

Reason 9 · Transactions must be strongly consistent

SITUATIONA banking system where a money transfer must succeed or fail as one atomic operation.
WHY IT'S BADMicroservices make distributed transactions hard. Saga patterns, compensation, eventual consistency — all add complexity. Sometimes a single database transaction is the right answer.
BETTER ALTERNATIVEModular monolith with strong database transactions. Split only the parts that don't need strong consistency.
ARCHITECT THINKS"Which operations cannot tolerate partial failure?" Keep those in one service or one database.

Reason 10 · Business logic is tightly connected

SITUATIONOrder, inventory, and payment always change together. A change to one always affects the others.
WHY IT'S BADSplitting tightly coupled logic into separate services creates constant network chatter and distributed transaction problems. The services become "distributed monoliths" — hard to change, hard to deploy, no real independence.
BETTER ALTERNATIVEKeep them in one service. Only split what truly changes independently.
ARCHITECT THINKS"Do these two things change together?" If yes, they belong together. This is the single most important test for service boundaries.
THE RULE TO REMEMBER

Start simple. Split when you have a measured reason. Every reason above is about avoiding complexity until the problem is big enough to justify it. The architect who resists the urge to microservice too early saves the company money, time, and pain.

PART 3 · THE BUSINESS EXAMPLE

One System, Three Architectures

Let's design the same system three different ways. This makes the trade-offs visible.

THE SYSTEM: ENTERPRISE INVENTORY AND ORDER MANAGEMENT

BUSINESS AREA RESPONSIBILITY
Authentication User login, sessions, tokens
Company Tenant accounts, settings, users
Customer End-customer profiles, addresses
Product Catalog, categories, pricing
Sales Order Orders, line items, status
Purchase Purchase orders from suppliers
Inventory Stock levels, warehouses, movements
Payment Payment processing, refunds
Notification Email, SMS, push notifications
Reporting Sales reports, inventory reports
Audit Action log for compliance

Approach 1 · Simple monolith

Everything lives in one application, one database. All modules share the same codebase, the same deploy, the same tables.

FIG. 18.1 · SIMPLE MONOLITH
AdvantagesSimplest to build and deploy. One codebase, one database. Easy to debug — everything is in one place. Low cost. Fast to ship. Good for small teams and early-stage products.
DisadvantagesAny bug can break the whole system. No independent scaling. All teams deploy together. As the codebase grows, it becomes harder to understand and change. One part can slow down all parts.

Approach 2 · Modular monolith

Still one application, one database, one deploy — but the code is organized into clear business modules. Each module has its own tables. Modules talk through defined internal interfaces, not by reading each other's data directly.

FIG. 18.2 · MODULAR MONOLITH
AdvantagesStill one deploy, still cheap. Clear business boundaries — each module owns its data. Easy to find code. Modules can be extracted into services later if needed. One team can ship fast without coordination overhead.
DisadvantagesStill one deploy — teams cannot release independently. Still one database — heavy reports still compete with transactions. Scaling is all-or-nothing.

Approach 3 · Microservices

Each business area is its own small application, with its own database, its own deploy, its own team ownership. They talk over the network.

FIG. 18.3 · MICROSERVICES
AdvantagesEach service deploys independently. Each team owns its service. Each part can scale on its own. Failure in one service does not break the others. Different technologies per service.
DisadvantagesComplex to operate. Distributed transactions. Network failures. Data consistency challenges. High infrastructure cost. Requires strong DevOps. Harder to debug. Slower local development.
THE KEY INSIGHT

Notice the progression: Approach 1 has almost no infrastructure — just one app and one DB. Approach 2 adds organization inside the app — modules, APIs, events — but no new infrastructure. Approach 3 adds an entire infrastructure layer — API gateway, service mesh, message broker, distributed tracing. That infrastructure is the cost of the flexibility. If you don't need the flexibility, you're paying the cost for nothing.

PART 4 · THE FORGOTTEN OPTION

Modular Monolith Is Not a Bad Architecture

Many architects treat modular monolith as a "compromise" — something you do before you grow up to microservices. This is wrong. A well-designed modular monolith is a legitimate, professional, production-grade architecture. For many companies, it is the right choice forever.

Why a modular monolith can be excellent

Clear business areasEach module represents a business capability. The Customer module does customer things. The Inventory module does inventory things. The code is organized the way the business is organized.
Separate responsibilitiesEach module owns its own tables. The Payment module cannot read the Customer module's tables directly — it must go through the Customer API. This prevents tight coupling without needing distributed transactions.
Shared deploymentOne deploy, one artifact, one rollback. Simple. When something breaks, you know which deploy caused it. No coordination between teams needed.
Simple communicationModules call each other through in-process method calls. No network. No timeouts. No retry logic. A function call takes microseconds, not milliseconds.
Simple database accessOne database means simple transactions. If order creation needs to update inventory and payment in one transaction, you can. No saga patterns. No compensation logic.
Easier debuggingWhen something breaks, all the logs are in one place. All the code is in one place. Stack traces make sense. You do not need distributed tracing to find the root cause.
Lower operational costOne server, one database, one monitoring setup. No service mesh. No API gateway. No message broker. The infrastructure bill is a fraction of microservices.
Easier for small teamsFive developers can understand and maintain one codebase. They can understand the whole system. They cannot understand 20 services.
Can split laterThe modules are already separated. If the Notification module needs its own deploy, you can extract it. If Reporting needs its own database, you can move it. The architecture can grow without being rewritten.

When a modular monolith is the better choice

  • Small to medium teams — 5 to 30 developers. Not enough people to own 20 services.
  • One business domain — when the system is mostly one thing, not many independent things.
  • Limited DevOps — when the company cannot operate 20 services, 20 databases, 20 deploys.
  • Early to mid stage — when the business is still changing, the domain boundaries are not yet stable.
  • Cost-sensitive — when the infrastructure budget is limited.
  • Simple transactional requirements — when strong consistency across modules matters more than independent scaling.
THE FAMOUS EXAMPLE

Shopify runs one of the largest e-commerce platforms in the world — and for years ran as a modular monolith (a Ruby on Rails application). They split into services only when specific pain points forced them to. The lesson: if it works for Shopify, it can work for you.

THE MINDSET SHIFT

Modular monolith is not "monolith done badly". It is a deliberate choice — the same way microservices is a deliberate choice. The architect who treats modular monolith as a legitimate option is making a mature decision. The architect who treats it as a failure of courage is chasing fashion.

PART 5 · WHEN THEY ARE WORTH IT

The Eight Real Benefits of Microservices

Microservices are not magic. They solve specific problems. When those problems are real and painful, microservices pay off. When they are not, microservices add cost for no benefit.

1 · Independent deploymentEach team can ship when ready, without coordinating with other teams. Example: the Order team ships a fix at 2 a.m., the Payment team ships a feature at 4 a.m. No one waits for anyone else. Condition: you need multiple teams, each owning different parts of the system.
2 · Independent scalingThe part that gets the most traffic gets the most resources. Example: the Order service gets slammed during Black Friday — scale it to 100 instances. The Audit service is idle — leave it at 3 instances. Condition: you need one part of the system to require much more capacity than the rest.
3 · Failure isolationA bug in one service does not break the whole system. Example: the Notification service has a memory leak and crashes. Customers can still place orders. Orders still get saved. Email just doesn't go out for a while. Condition: you need the system to keep working when parts fail.
4 · Team ownershipEach team owns a service end-to-end. Example: the Payment team owns the Payment service — code, database, deploy, monitoring, on-call. No one else touches it. Condition: you have multiple teams and want each to be autonomous.
5 · Technology flexibilityDifferent services can use different stacks. Example: the Search service uses Elasticsearch and Go because it needs performance. The Reporting service uses Python and a data warehouse because the team knows it. Condition: the different technology must provide real benefit — not just preference.
6 · Smaller codebasesEach service is a focused, manageable codebase. Example: the Payment service has 50,000 lines of code — one person can understand it. The original monolith had 2 million lines — no one understood it. Condition: the codebase is large enough that no one can hold it in their head.
7 · Independent release cyclesTeams can release on their own schedule. Example: the Inventory team releases every day because stock changes constantly. The Audit team releases every two weeks because compliance changes are slow. Condition: different parts of the system change at different speeds.
8 · Different performance needsEach service can be optimized for its own workload. Example: the Search service is memory-bound — it uses in-memory caches. The Reporting service is CPU-bound — it uses heavy query engines. Each gets what it needs. Condition: different parts of the system have genuinely different performance profiles.
THE WARNING

Microservices do not automatically give you scalability. They do not automatically give you reliability. They give you the ability to scale parts independently and isolate failures — but only if you design, operate, and maintain them correctly. A badly-designed set of microservices can be less reliable than a well-designed monolith.

PART 6 · THE PRICE YOU PAY

Fifteen Costs of Microservices

This is the most important section for an architect to understand. Every benefit has a price. The architect who ignores these costs is building a system that will fail in production.

1 · Network Communication

PROBLEMTwo services used to call each other in a millisecond inside one process. Now they talk over the network.
EXAMPLEOrder service calls Inventory service to check stock. In a monolith: 0.1 ms. In microservices: 10–50 ms. The network is 100–500× slower.
ARCHITECT MANAGESDesign for network failures: retries, timeouts, circuit breakers. Batch calls when possible. Cache responses. Accept that the system is slower for some operations.

2 · Service Failures

PROBLEMAny service can fail at any time — crash, hang, slow down. The system must keep working.
EXAMPLEPayment service is down. Customers cannot complete orders. What does the Order service do? Fail? Queue the order? Show a message?
ARCHITECT MANAGESCircuit breakers. Graceful degradation. Async processing where possible. Health checks. Clear failure-mode documentation.

3 · Distributed Transactions

PROBLEMAn order involves three services: Order, Payment, Inventory. All three must succeed, or all must fail. There is no single database transaction.
EXAMPLEOrder saved. Payment charged. Inventory fails. Now we have a paid order without stock. How do we fix it?
ARCHITECT MANAGESSaga patterns (orchestration or choreography). Compensation transactions. Event-driven processing. Manual recovery processes. This is one of the hardest problems in microservices.

4 · Data Consistency

PROBLEMData is spread across many databases. Keeping it consistent is hard.
EXAMPLEA customer changes their address. The Customer service has the new address. The Order service still has the old address in its local copy. Orders go to the wrong place.
ARCHITECT MANAGESEvent sourcing. Eventual consistency where acceptable. Strong consistency where required. Clear ownership of data. Synchronization mechanisms.

5 · Monitoring

PROBLEMYou now have 20 services to monitor. Each has its own metrics, logs, alerts. You need to see the whole system, not just one service.
EXAMPLEAn order is slow. Is it the Order service? The Payment service? The Inventory service? The database? You need distributed tracing to find out.
ARCHITECT MANAGESCentralized monitoring. Distributed tracing (Jaeger, Zipkin, OpenTelemetry). Service-level dashboards. System-level dashboards. Alert aggregation.

6 · Logging

PROBLEMLogs are scattered across 20 services. Finding a single user's journey requires searching all of them.
EXAMPLEA customer says "my order was charged twice." You need to find the log of their order across the Order, Payment, and Inventory services.
ARCHITECT MANAGESCentralized logging (ELK stack, Loki, CloudWatch). Correlation IDs on every request. Structured logging. Log retention policies.

7 · Deployment

PROBLEM20 services = 20 deploys. Each needs its own pipeline, its own tests, its own rollback plan.
EXAMPLEThe Order service has a bug. You need to roll it back. But it depends on the Payment service version. Do they work together? You need contract testing.
ARCHITECT MANAGESCI/CD pipelines for each service. Contract testing. Blue-green deployments. Canary releases. Automated rollback. Version management.

8 · Testing

PROBLEMTesting a distributed system is much harder than testing a monolith. Services depend on each other.
EXAMPLEHow do you test the Order service without running the Payment service? You need mocks, contract tests, and integration tests that span services.
ARCHITECT MANAGESContract testing (Pact). Service virtualization. Integration test environments. End-to-end tests. Clear test strategy per service.

9 · Security

PROBLEMEach service is a separate attack surface. Service-to-service communication must be secured.
EXAMPLEThe Order service calls the Payment service. How do they trust each other? How do you prevent an attacker from calling the Payment service directly?
ARCHITECT MANAGESService mesh (Istio, Linkerd) for mTLS. API gateway for external access. OAuth tokens between services. Security scanning per service. Zero-trust architecture.

10 · Version Management

PROBLEMWhen you change an API, you must support both old and new versions until all clients migrate.
EXAMPLEYou change the Order service API. The Mobile app uses v1. The Web app uses v2. You must support both for months.
ARCHITECT MANAGESAPI versioning. Backwards-compatible changes. Deprecation policies. API gateways. Clear migration plans.

11 · Infrastructure

PROBLEMMicroservices need infrastructure that monoliths do not: service discovery, API gateways, message brokers, configuration servers.
EXAMPLEHow does the Order service find the Payment service? You need a service registry. How do services share configuration? You need a config server.
ARCHITECT MANAGESInvest in infrastructure early. Use managed services where possible (AWS, Azure, GCP). Kubernetes for orchestration. Treat infrastructure as code.

12 · Service Discovery

PROBLEMServices need to find each other. Their addresses change as they scale up and down.
EXAMPLEThe Payment service has 10 instances. How does the Order service know which one to call?
ARCHITECT MANAGESService registry (Consul, Eureka, Kubernetes services). Client-side load balancing. Health checks. Automatic failover.

13 · Operational Support

PROBLEMEach service needs on-call support, runbooks, escalation paths. 20 services = 20 on-call rotations.
EXAMPLEThe Payment service is failing at 3 a.m. Who responds? The Payment team? The on-call rotation? How do they know the runbook?
ARCHITECT MANAGESClear on-call per service. Runbooks for every failure mode. Post-mortem process. Knowledge sharing. SRE practices.

14 · Debugging

PROBLEMA bug can involve five services. You need to follow the request across all of them to find the root cause.
EXAMPLEAn order has the wrong price. Is it the Product service? The Order service? The Payment service? You need distributed tracing to find out.
ARCHITECT MANAGESDistributed tracing. Correlation IDs. Centralized logging. Debugging tools that work across services. Clear ownership of each service.

15 · Increased Cloud Cost

PROBLEMMicroservices cost more to run: more servers, more databases, more network traffic, more managed services.
EXAMPLEMonolith: 3 servers, 1 database = $500/month. Microservices: 20 services, 20 databases, API gateway, message broker = $5,000/month.
ARCHITECT MANAGESCost monitoring. Right-sizing. Reserved instances. Serverless where appropriate. Budget alerts. Cost attribution per team.
THE ARCHITECT'S QUESTION

Before choosing microservices, ask: "Can my team handle all fifteen of these costs?" If the answer is no — if you cannot afford the monitoring, the deployment, the distributed transactions — then microservices will make your system worse, not better. A well-run modular monolith beats a poorly-run microservices architecture every time.

PART 7 · THE DECISION FRAMEWORK

  • How complex is the business?Simple rules, one domain → A. Multiple related domains → B. Many independent domains with different rules → C.BUSINESS COMPLEXITY DRIVES BOUNDARIES
  • How many developers are working on it?1–10 developers → A. 10–30 → B. 30+ across multiple teams → C.TEAM SIZE DRIVES COORDINATION NEEDS
  • Do teams need independent releases?No → A. Sometimes, but can coordinate → B. Yes, every team ships on their own schedule → C.RELEASE INDEPENDENCE IS A TOP REASON FOR MICROSERVICES
  • Does one area need much more scaling?No, all parts scale together → A. Somewhat, but manageable → B. Yes, one area gets 10× the traffic → C.INDEPENDENT SCALING DRIVES SERVICE SPLITTING
  • Are different areas owned by different teams?One team owns everything → A. Modules have owners but one team ships → B. Each team fully owns their area → C.OWNERSHIP DRIVES BOUNDARIES
  • How strong is the DevOps capability?Manual deploys, no automation → A. Basic CI/CD → B. Strong DevOps, Kubernetes, service mesh → C.OPERATIONAL MATURITY IS NON-NEGOTIABLE FOR MICROSERVICES

    PART 8 · DRAWING THE LINES

    Where Should One Service End and Another Begin?

    This is one of the hardest decisions in microservices. Get it wrong, and you end up with a "distributed monolith" — all the costs of microservices with none of the benefits.

    The golden rule

    A service should have a meaningful business responsibility, not just a small amount of code. If you cannot describe what the service does in one business sentence ("it handles payments," "it manages inventory"), the boundary is wrong.

    Possible services for the Order Management System

    Order Service

    WHY SEPARATEOrders are the core business capability. They have their own lifecycle, their own rules, their own scaling needs. During sales events, order traffic can be 10× normal.
    DATA IT OWNSOrders, order lines, order status history.
    WHAT IT DOES NOT OWNProducts (Product service owns these), inventory (Inventory service), payments (Payment service).
    COMMUNICATES WITHProduct service (to get prices), Inventory service (to check stock), Payment service (to charge), Notification service (to confirm).
    BAD BOUNDARY RISKIf Order also owns inventory, it becomes a distributed monolith with Inventory. Every change to one requires a change to the other.

    Inventory Service

    WHY SEPARATEInventory has different scaling needs than orders. It changes constantly (every stock movement). It may need real-time updates that orders do not.
    DATA IT OWNSStock levels, warehouse locations, stock movements, reservations.
    WHAT IT DOES NOT OWNOrders (Order service), products (Product service), warehouses (may be shared).
    COMMUNICATES WITHOrder service (reserves stock on order), Purchase service (receives stock from suppliers), Product service (knows what products exist).
    BAD BOUNDARY RISKIf inventory shares a database with Order, you have a distributed monolith. If inventory is split into Stock Service and Reservation Service, you have too many services.

    Payment Service

    WHY SEPARATEPayments have strict compliance requirements (PCI DSS). They have different scaling (fewer transactions, but each is critical). They integrate with external payment gateways.
    DATA IT OWNSPayment records, refund records, payment method tokens.
    WHAT IT DOES NOT OWNOrders (Order service), customer credit cards (tokenized only).
    COMMUNICATES WITHOrder service (charges for orders), external gateways (Stripe, PayPal), Notification service (payment confirmations).
    BAD BOUNDARY RISKIf Payment also owns orders, PCI compliance applies to the whole service. Keeping Payment separate limits the compliance scope.

    Notification Service

    WHY SEPARATENotifications are async and independent. An order can be placed, the notification can go out later. If notifications fail, the order is still saved. Different scaling profile — bursty traffic.
    DATA IT OWNSNotification records, delivery status, templates.
    WHAT IT DOES NOT OWNCustomers (Customer service), orders (Order service).
    COMMUNICATES WITHOrder service (subscribes to order events), Payment service (subscribes to payment events), external services (email, SMS, push).
    BAD BOUNDARY RISKIf Notification is embedded in Order service, a bug in email code breaks the whole order flow.

    Reporting Service

    WHY SEPARATEReporting has completely different characteristics: heavy reads, complex queries, long-running jobs. It should not compete with transactional services for resources.
    DATA IT OWNSAggregated reports, data warehouse tables, report cache.
    WHAT IT DOES NOT OWNRaw orders, raw customers — it reads those through events or read replicas.
    COMMUNICATES WITHAll other services (subscribes to events to build reports). Reads from event streams or read replicas, never writes to transactional databases.
    BAD BOUNDARY RISKIf Reporting runs on the Order database, heavy reports slow down orders. Separate Reporting service + data warehouse solves this.
    THE TEST FOR A GOOD BOUNDARY

    Ask: "Can I change this service without changing the others?" If the answer is yes, the boundary is good. If changing the Order service forces changes to the Inventory service, the boundary is wrong — either merge them or redraw the line.

    PART 9 · THE OVER-ENGINEERING TRAP

    Too Many Microservices

    A common mistake, especially among architects new to microservices, is to split everything. One entity, one service. One table, one service. This is over-engineering — and it creates a distributed monolith with all the costs of microservices and none of the benefits.

    The bad example

    FIG. 18.4 · OVER-ENGINEERING VS SENSIBLE DESIGN

    Why this is a problem

    • Distributed monolith. Every operation requires multiple service calls. The services are so coupled that they must deploy together in practice.
    • Network overhead. Creating a customer now requires three network calls instead of one database insert.
    • Operational nightmare. Eight services to deploy, monitor, and maintain — but they change together, so the benefit of independent deploy is gone.
    • Debugging hell. When something breaks, you trace through eight services to find the cause.
    • Cost explosion. Eight databases, eight deploys, eight monitoring dashboards — for what should be three services.

    The sensible design

    Group related capabilities into one service. A Customer service owns customer data, addresses, and contacts — because they change together. A Product service owns products, prices, and categories. An Order service owns order headers and order lines. The services are still independent, but each has a meaningful business responsibility.

    THE RULE

    If two things change together, they belong together. Customer Address and Customer Contact always change when Customer changes. They should be one service. Order Header and Order Line are always updated together — they should be one service. The "bounded context" concept from Domain-Driven Design captures this: a service should align with a bounded context, not with a database table.

    PART 10 · THE DATABASE QUESTION

    Should Multiple Microservices Share One Database?

    Short answer: no. Long answer: almost always no, with very specific temporary exceptions. This is one of the most violated rules in microservices — and one of the most damaging.

    Why teams do it

    • It's easier at first. One database is simpler to set up than five.
    • They want transactions. "If everything is in one database, we can do transactions across services."
    • They don't want data duplication. "Why store customer data twice?"
    • Management pushes for speed. "Just get it done. We'll fix it later." (They never do.)

    Why it is dangerous

    Loss of independenceIf the Order service and Inventory service share a database, changing the database schema requires coordinated changes to both services. Independent deployment is gone.
    Tight couplingService A reads Service B's tables directly. When Service B changes its schema, Service A breaks. The services are coupled through the database, even if the code is separate.
    No data ownershipWho owns the customer table? If three services read and write it, no one owns it. Data quality degrades. Bugs multiply.
    Impossible to scale independentlyIf one service needs more database power, you have to scale the whole database — including all the other services' data.
    Transactions are a trapCross-service transactions feel safe but create hidden coupling. When the Payment service locks a row, the Order service waits. Distributed transactions are worse than microservices were supposed to solve.
    Cannot replace technologyIf the Order service wants to move to MongoDB but the shared database is PostgreSQL, it cannot. Technology choice is locked in for everyone.

    When shared database access may be temporarily acceptable

    • During migration. When splitting a monolith into services, the new service may temporarily share the old database while data is being moved. This should be a short phase, not a permanent state.
    • Reporting read replicas. Reporting services often read from a replica of the transactional database. This is read-only and does not create coupling — the reporting service does not change the data.
    THE ARCHITECT'S TEST

    Ask: "Can I delete Service A's database without breaking Service B?" If the answer is no, the services are coupled through the database. This is a distributed monolith in disguise. Fix it before it becomes permanent.

    PART 11 · THE HARDEST PROBLEM

    Distributed Transactions

    In a monolith, when you save an order, charge a payment, and update inventory, you wrap them in one database transaction. Either all three succeed, or all three roll back. In microservices, there is no single transaction. This is the hardest problem in microservices — and every architect must understand the solutions.

    The scenario

    STEP 1: ORDER SAVEDThe Order service creates an order record. Success.
    STEP 2: PAYMENT CHARGEDThe Payment service charges the customer's card. Success.
    STEP 3: INVENTORY UPDATEDThe Inventory service tries to reserve stock — and fails. No stock available.

    The problem: We have a saved order and a charged payment, but no stock. The customer has been charged for an order that cannot be fulfilled. What do we do?

    Five possible approaches

    1 · RetryWhat it is: If the Inventory service failed, try again. Maybe it was a temporary glitch. When it works: Transient failures — network blips, temporary locks. When it does not: When the stock is genuinely not available, retry will not help.
    2 · Compensation (Saga)What it is: For every forward action, there is a reverse action. If the Order was saved but Inventory failed, run a "cancel order" action. If the Payment was charged, run a "refund" action. When it works: Most cases — orders, payments, bookings. When it does not: Some operations cannot be reversed (e.g., an email that was already sent).
    3 · Workflow (Orchestration)What it is: A central orchestrator service coordinates the steps. It tells Order to save, then Payment to charge, then Inventory to reserve. If any step fails, the orchestrator tells the previous services to compensate. When it works: Complex business flows with many steps. When it does not: The orchestrator becomes a single point of failure.
    4 · Event-based processing (Choreography)What it is: Each service publishes events. Order publishes "OrderCreated." Payment listens and charges. Inventory listens and reserves. If Inventory fails, it publishes "StockUnavailable," and Order listens and cancels. When it works: Loosely coupled flows. When it does not: Hard to follow what happened. Hard to debug.
    5 · Manual recoveryWhat it is: Sometimes, a human has to fix it. A support agent sees the failed order, refunds the customer, and apologizes. When it works: Rare failures, low volume, high-value transactions. When it does not: High volume — you cannot manually fix thousands of orders a day.
    THE PRACTICAL ANSWER

    Most real microservices use a combination: saga patterns (compensation) for the critical flows, event-based processing for looser flows, and manual recovery for the rare cases that cannot be automated. The architect's job is to choose the right pattern for each flow — and to document the failure modes clearly.

    PART 12 · THE REALISTIC PATH

    How to Migrate from a Monolith

    Management says: "We have a large monolith. We want microservices." The worst thing an architect can do is rewrite the whole application as 20 services in one big project. That project will fail. The right approach is gradual.

    1. Understand the current systemMap the business capabilities. Find out which parts of the code change together and which change independently. Find out where the pain points are.DO NOT SPLIT WHAT YOU DO NOT UNDERSTAND
    2. Identify business areasDraw the bounded contexts. Customer is one. Order is another. Inventory is another. These will become your modules — and later, possibly, your services.BUSINESS BOUNDARIES BECOME SERVICE BOUNDARIES
    3. Find problem areasWhere is the monolith hurting? Is reporting slowing down orders? Is one team blocked by another? Is one part scaling differently? The problem areas become migration candidates.MIGRATE TO SOLVE A PROBLEM, NOT TO REACH A STATE
    4. Improve internal modules firstBefore splitting into services, turn the monolith into a modular monolith. Clean the boundaries. Make each module own its tables. Expose internal APIs. This is 80% of the benefit at 20% of the cost.MODULAR MONOLITH IS THE BRIDGE
    5. Identify a good first servicePick the easiest, most independent area. Notification service is often a good first — it is async, independent, and does not break the main flow. Extract it, learn from the process.START WITH LOW RISK, HIGH LEARNING
    6. Extract graduallyOne service at a time. Get it working in production. Measure the result. Learn. Then extract the next one. Never try to split the whole monolith at once.INCREMENTS, NOT BIG BANG
    7. Measure the resultDid the extracted service improve things? Did the team ship faster? Did the system become more reliable? If the answer is no, stop. You may not need more microservices.MEASURE BEFORE CONTINUING
    8. Continue only when benefit is provenIf each extracted service brings clear benefit, continue. If not, stop at the modular monolith. A stable modular monolith beats a broken microservices architecture every time.THE GOAL IS A BETTER SYSTEM, NOT MORE SERVICES
    THE BIGGEST MISTAKE

    The "rewrite from scratch" project. A team takes a year to rewrite the monolith as 20 microservices. They ship. The system breaks in ways no one expected. Data consistency problems. Network failures. Operational overload. The company loses a year and has a worse system than before. Do not do this. Migrate gradually. Prove each step. Keep the option to stop.

    THE STRANGLER PATTERN

    The industry term for this gradual migration is the Strangler Fig Pattern (Martin Fowler). Like a strangler fig growing around a tree, the new system grows around the old one, gradually replacing it. The old monolith keeps running while new services are built. Eventually, the monolith shrinks to nothing. This is the safe path.

    PART 13 · TEN REAL DECISIONS

    Real Scenarios, Real Answers

    Ten situations. For each, the architect decides: Monolith (A), Modular Monolith (B), or Microservices (C). Notice the pattern — most real systems land on B.

    Scenario 1 · Startup with 3 developers

    CHOICEA — Monolith.
    WHYThree developers, small budget, unproven product. Speed matters more than architecture. Every minute spent on infrastructure is a minute not spent on the product.
    MAIN RISKOver-engineering. A startup that ships slowly dies faster than a startup with messy code.
    WHAT CHANGES ITIf the startup grows to 30 developers and 1 million users, revisit — but not before.

    Scenario 2 · Banking platform with many teams

    CHOICEC — Microservices (for most parts), with careful strong-consistency services for money operations.
    WHYMany teams, each owning a domain. Regulatory requirements for auditability. Different scaling needs per domain. Compliance boundaries that match service boundaries.
    MAIN RISKDistributed transactions on money flows. The Payment and Account services must be designed with extreme care — possibly kept in one service with strong transactions.
    WHAT CHANGES ITIf DevOps is weak, start with modular monolith and migrate.

    Scenario 3 · Internal HR application

    CHOICEA — Monolith or B — Modular Monolith.
    WHYInternal users, predictable traffic, limited budget. One team maintains it. The system does not need to scale to millions of users.
    MAIN RISKOver-engineering for a problem that does not exist. A simple modular monolith is the right answer.
    WHAT CHANGES ITOnly if the company grows to 100,000 employees and the system becomes business-critical.

    Scenario 4 · Global e-commerce platform

    CHOICEC — Microservices with multi-region deployment.
    WHYMillions of users, many teams, global scale, Black Friday spikes, different regions with different requirements. Every microservice benefit applies here.
    MAIN RISKData consistency across regions. Inventory in Europe vs inventory in Asia. This requires careful design.
    WHAT CHANGES ITOnly if the company shrinks dramatically.

    Scenario 5 · Large reporting application

    CHOICEB — Modular Monolith with a separate reporting database or data warehouse.
    WHYReporting is read-heavy and can be separated from the transactional side without full microservices. A data warehouse solves the problem at a fraction of the cost.
    MAIN RISKSplitting into microservices when one reporting service plus a data warehouse is enough.
    WHAT CHANGES ITIf the reporting needs real-time analytics across many business domains.

    Scenario 6 · Small CRM

    CHOICEB — Modular Monolith.
    WHYSmall team, moderate traffic, clear business domains (customers, deals, activities). A modular monolith gives clean code without operational overhead.
    MAIN RISKJumping to microservices because "CRM should be modern." Modern does not mean microservices.
    WHAT CHANGES ITIf the CRM grows to 100,000 companies and needs independent scaling per module.

    Scenario 7 · Multi-company SaaS

    CHOICEB — Modular Monolith at first, then C — Microservices as the platform scales.
    WHYMulti-tenant SaaS has clear domains (tenant management, billing, core product). Start as modular monolith, split services as traffic grows or teams form around domains.
    MAIN RISKSplitting too early, before the domains are stable. Multi-tenant design is complex enough without adding microservices complexity on top.
    WHAT CHANGES ITWhen one domain (e.g., billing) needs to scale independently or has different compliance requirements.

    Scenario 8 · High-volume payment platform

    CHOICEC — Microservices with Payment as a strongly-consistent service.
    WHYHigh volume, strict compliance, clear separation of concerns (authorization, settlement, reconciliation, fraud). Each domain has different characteristics.
    MAIN RISKSplitting the transaction flow itself. The Payment authorization must be strongly consistent — keep it in one service with strong transactions.
    WHAT CHANGES ITIf transaction volume is low, modular monolith may be enough.

    Scenario 9 · Early-stage product

    CHOICEA — Monolith.
    WHYProduct-market fit is not proven. The business model may pivot in three months. Speed of iteration matters more than architectural elegance.
    MAIN RISKOver-engineering. An early-stage product that cannot pivot quickly will die.
    WHAT CHANGES ITWhen the product finds its market and grows, move to modular monolith. Only then consider microservices.

    Scenario 10 · Existing monolith with 100 developers

    CHOICEC — Microservices, migrated gradually using the Strangler pattern.
    WHY100 developers on one codebase is the classic case for microservices. Teams block each other. Releases take weeks. The monolith is a bottleneck.
    MAIN RISKBig-bang rewrite. Do not try to split everything at once. Migrate gradually, starting with the most painful areas.
    WHAT CHANGES ITIf DevOps maturity is low, invest in DevOps first. Microservices without DevOps is a recipe for disaster.
    THE PATTERN

    Out of ten scenarios, only three clearly need microservices (banking, global e-commerce, 100-dev monolith). Two more might migrate there. The rest are monolith or modular monolith. This matches reality: most systems do not need microservices. The architect who can say "no" to microservices saves companies millions.

    PART 14 · THE INTERVIEW

    Twenty Questions an Architect Must Answer

    These are not definitions. They are decisions. For each question, we show four layers: what the interviewer is checking, a simple answer, a strong architect answer, and a common mistake.

    1. Why would you use microservices?DECISION ▸ FOUR-LAYER ANSWER

      STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWERI use microservices when the benefits — independent deployment, independent scaling, failure isolation, team ownership — justify the operational and technical complexity. I need multiple teams, clear domain boundaries, and strong DevOps before I reach for microservices.
      COMMON MISTAKE"Because they are scalable." Scalability is not automatic — it requires design. Many monoliths scale just fine.
    2. Why would you NOT use microservices?DECISION ▸ FOUR-LAYER ANSWER

    3. When is a monolith better than microservices?TRADE-OFF ▸ FOUR-LAYER ANSWER

      COMMON MISTAKE"It's just a monolith." It is not — the internal structure matters. A modular monolith can become microservices later if needed.
    5. How do you define service boundaries?THINKING ▸ FOUR-LAYER ANSWER

Order Header and Order Line always update together — one service.
      COMMON MISTAKE"One entity per service." That creates a distributed monolith with constant network chatter.
    6. How many microservices would you create?DECISION ▸ FOUR-LAYER ANSWER

      STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWERThe number of services should match the number of independent business capabilities — not the number of tables or entities. A rough rule: one service per 5–10 developers.       COMMON MISTAKECreating 50 services for 10 developers. The team cannot maintain that.
    7. Should every business area become a service?DECISION ▸ FOUR-LAYER ANSWER

      STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWERNo. A business area should become a service only if it needs independent deployment, independent scaling, or independent team ownership.       COMMON MISTAKE"Yes, every domain becomes a service." That is over-engineering.
    8. How do you handle distributed transactions?TRADE-OFF ▸ FOUR-LAYER ANSWER

      SIMPLE ANSWERWith sagas — compensating actions when things fail.
      STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWERI use several patterns depending on the flow. Saga pattern (orchestration or choreography) for critical flows. Event-driven processing for looser flows.     9. Should services share a database?DECISION ▸ FOUR-LAYER ANSWER

      SIMPLE ANSWERNo. Each service owns its database.
      STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWERNo, except in very specific temporary cases like migration. Shared databases create tight coupling, destroy independent deployment, and make technology choice impossible.       COMMON MISTAKE"It's easier to share." It is easier at first. It becomes impossible to untangle later.
    10. How would you migrate a monolith?DECISION ▸ FOUR-LAYER ANSWER
      WHAT INTERVIEWER CHECKSWhether you understand the safe path.
      SIMPLE ANSWERGradually. One service at a time.
      STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWERI use the Strangler Fig pattern. First, understand the current system and identify business areas.       COMMON MISTAKE"Rewrite from scratch in one year." That project ships late, over budget, and breaks in production.
    11. What happens when one service fails?FAILURE ▸ FOUR-LAYER ANSWER

      SIMPLE ANSWERThe other services should keep working.
      STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWERIt depends on the design. If services are tightly coupled, one failure can cascade and take down the whole system.       COMMON MISTAKE"The whole system goes down." That happens only in badly-designed microservices.
    12. How do you handle service-to-service communication?DESIGN ▸ FOUR-LAYER ANSWER

      COMMON MISTAKE"It's the modern way." That is not a business justification.

    17. What is eventual consistency and when is it acceptable?TRADE-OFF ▸ FOUR-LAYER ANSWER

      SIMPLE ANSWERData may be briefly old but will become consistent. Acceptable for most reads.

      COMMON MISTAKE"It's a bug." It is a feature — a deliberate design choice.
    18. What is the CAP theorem and how does it apply to microservices?THINKING ▸ FOUR-LAYER ANSWER

      SIMPLE ANSWERYou can't have consistency, availability, and partition tolerance at the same time.

The architect's job is to choose the right trade-off per service.
      COMMON MISTAKE"I can have all three." You cannot. Distributed systems force trade-offs.

Notification service is often a good first choice — it is async, independent, and if it fails, orders still work. The first extraction is about learning, not about solving the biggest problem.
      COMMON MISTAKEExtracting the most critical service first. That maximizes risk while the team is still learning.
    20. How would you explain microservices to a business stakeholder?COMMUNICATION ▸ FOUR-LAYER ANSWER

      SIMPLE ANSWERIt's like having separate teams for separate jobs.
      STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER"Think of a restaurant. In a monolith, one person is the chef, waiter, cashier, and cleaner. It works when the restaurant is small. We only split when the restaurant is big enough that one person cannot do everything well."
      COMMON MISTAKETechnical jargon: "distributed systems with bounded contexts." Stakeholders do not care about the mechanism — they care about the outcome.
    WHAT THE INTERVIEWER SCORES

    Not whether you know the terms. Whether you understand the trade-offs, can say no to microservices when appropriate, think about failure modes, and explain to non-technical people. A strong architect says "it depends" — and then explains what it depends on.

    3. Which should remain together? Customer, Product, Order, Inventory, Payment. These are tightly coupled — they change together. Splitting them prematurely creates a distributed monolith.

    4. Should we first create a modular monolith? Yes. This is the bridge. Clean the internal modules, establish clear APIs, make each module own its tables. This is 80% of the benefit at 20% of the cost.

    5. What should be the first service to extract? Notification. It is async, independent, low risk. If it fails, orders still work. It teaches the team about microservices without risking the core business.

    6. What risks exist? Limited DevOps maturity (biggest risk). Distributed transactions if Payment is split. Reporting migration complexity. Team coordination during migration. Data consistency across services.

    7. What additional infrastructure is required? For Notification service alone: message broker (RabbitMQ/Kafka), centralized logging, monitoring dashboard. For full microservices: API gateway, service mesh, distributed tracing, CI/CD per service, service discovery, configuration server.

    8. What would make you reject microservices? If DevOps does not improve. If the business logic is more tightly coupled than expected. If the team cannot handle the operational overhead. If the cost exceeds the benefit.
    THE KEY INSIGHT

    The company's request ("convert everything into microservices") is wrong. The right question is: "What specific problems are we trying to solve?" The answer: reporting slows down orders (fix with separate reporting service), teams want independent releases (fix with modular monolith first), traffic is increasing (fix with scaling the right parts). Microservices may be part of the solution, but they are not the solution itself.

    PART 17 · THE CLOSE

    Ten Rules for Microservices Decision-Making

    If you remember nothing else from today, remember these ten rules. They are simple, practical, and they will guide every microservices decision you make.

    Rule 1Start simple. Monolith first. Modular monolith second. Microservices only when the problem justifies them. Every step up is a response to a measured problem, not a guess about the future.
    Rule 2Microservices are not a goal. They are a means to a goal. The goal is faster releases, better scalability, team autonomy. If a modular monolith achieves those goals, use it.
    Rule 3Competitors are not a reason. "Competitor X uses microservices" is the weakest justification in architecture. Your system has different constraints, different team, different traffic, different business. Decide based on your reality.
    Rule 4DevOps maturity is non-negotiable. Without strong DevOps, microservices will fail. Invest in CI/CD, observability, and automation before you split the monolith.
    Rule 5One database per service. This is the rule most violated and the hardest to fix later. Shared databases create distributed monoliths. Each service owns its data.
    Rule 6Business capability, not entity. A service should represent a business capability, not a database table. Customer Address and Customer Contact belong with Customer. Order Header and Order Line belong with Order.
    Rule 7Things that change together belong together. The single most important test for service boundaries. If two modules always change together, merge them. If they change independently, split them.
    Rule 8Migrate gradually. The Strangler pattern. One service at a time. Measure each step. Continue only when the benefit is proven. Big-bang rewrites fail.
    Rule 9Design for failure. Microservices fail. Networks fail. Databases fail. Design with circuit breakers, retries, sagas, and graceful degradation from day one.
    Rule 10The best architecture is the simplest one that works. If a modular monolith solves the problem, use it. If microservices are needed, use them. The architect's job is to match the solution to the problem — not to chase trends.

    The one-minute interview answer

    "I avoid microservices when the operational cost exceeds the benefit: small teams, small systems, limited DevOps, early-stage products, or when business logic is tightly coupled. I use them only when specific problems — independent deployment, independent scaling, team autonomy — justify the complexity. Most systems start as monoliths or modular monoliths, and migrate to microservices only when the measured problems demand it."

    One powerful architect statement

    "Microservices are not a destination — they are a response. I choose them when the cost of not having them exceeds the cost of having them. Until then, I choose simplicity."

    Memorize that sentence. It captures the whole day. In an interview, say it — and you sound like an architect who has seen both the benefits and the costs.

    DAY 18 — CLOSE

    The Architect Who Chooses Wisely

    Five rules from today. If you remember nothing else, these carry you through any microservices conversation.

    01

    Simplicity is a feature.

    The simplest architecture that solves the problem is the best architecture. Complexity is a tax — pay it only when the problem justifies it. A well-designed monolith beats a poorly-designed microservices architecture every time.

    02

    Measure before you split.

    Do not split a monolith because "that's what modern systems do." Split it because releases take too long, teams block each other, or one part needs to scale independently. The numbers tell the story.

    03

    Modular monolith is not a compromise.

    It is a legitimate, professional, production-grade architecture. Shopify runs on one. Many successful companies run on one. It gives 80% of the benefits of microservices at 20% of the cost.

    04

    DevOps before microservices.

    You cannot operate 20 services without strong DevOps. CI/CD, centralized logging, distributed tracing, service mesh — build the infrastructure first, then migrate. Microservices without DevOps is a nightmare.

    05

    The answer is almost always "it depends".

    And the architect's value is knowing what it depends on: team size, traffic, budget, DevOps maturity, business coupling. The architect who says "microservices" without thinking is following fashion. The architect who says "it depends" and explains why is making a mature decision.

    A technical architect is not the person who knows the most patterns. A technical architect is the person who knows when to use which pattern — and can explain why to someone who has never written a line of code. Microservices are a powerful tool. The architect's job is to know when to reach for them — and when to leave them in the toolbox.
