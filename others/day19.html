PART 1 — THE MISSING STEP

Why Companies Jump Straight to Microservices

The most common architecture anti-pattern: a monolith starts showing problems, and the immediate response is to split it into 15–20 microservices. The step in between — the modular monolith — is almost always skipped.

Problem a monolith createsAs a monolith grows without discipline, it becomes a "big ball of mud" — everything is connected to everything, no clear boundaries, no clear ownership. One change breaks three other things. Developers are afraid to touch old code.
Problem a modular monolith solvesClear boundaries and clear ownership — inside the same application. The mud becomes organised sections. Developers know who owns what. Changes in one module do not break another. Deployment stays simple.
Problem microservices solveTrue independence — separate deployment, separate scaling, separate failure domains. This is only needed when the monolith (or modular monolith) cannot provide these at the scale the business requires.
Why architecture should follow business needsMicroservices are the right answer to a specific set of problems at a specific scale. Not having those problems, and choosing microservices anyway, means paying the cost without receiving the benefit.

A simple real-world example

A logistics company has a growing order management system. The monolith is becoming slow to release — teams interfere with each other's code. Management proposes splitting into microservices. But on investigation, the actual problem is: there are no module boundaries. The Order module directly changes the Inventory database table. The Notification module reads from the Payment database. Everything is entangled.

The solution is not 15 microservices. The solution is to add module boundaries to the existing application. Three months later, each team works independently in their module, releases are faster, and the system is more stable — without a single service split.

THE LESSON

The problem was bad architecture inside the monolith, not "not enough services". Microservices would have moved the problem from the monolith to the inter-service layer — and made it harder to fix. The modular monolith addressed the root cause.

PART 2 — THE THREE OPTIONS

Traditional Monolith, Modular Monolith, and Microservices

Option 1: Traditional Monolith

FIG. 19.1 — MONOLITH: SIMPLE TO START, HARDER TO MAINTAIN AS IT GROWS
  • Advantages: Fast to start, simple deployment, full ACID transactions, easy debugging, cheap to operate.
  • Disadvantages: As it grows without discipline, becomes hard to maintain. All teams release together. One bug can affect everything. Hard to scale one part independently.
  • Best use cases: New products, small teams, systems with strongly coupled business logic, internal tools.
  • Warning signs it is becoming a problem: Releases take hours because many teams must coordinate. A change in one area frequently breaks another. No one knows who owns which code.

Option 2: Modular Monolith

FIG. 19.2 — MODULAR MONOLITH: INTERNAL STRUCTURE MIMICS SERVICE BOUNDARIES WITHOUT DISTRIBUTION
  • What it is: One application, one deployment, but code deliberately organised into modules with clear ownership and defined interfaces.
  • How modules communicate: Via internal method calls or events — fast, typed, no network. One module calls another module's public API, not its internal database.
  • Data ownership: Each module owns its database schema. Other modules cannot directly access another module's tables.
  • Why it can be easier to operate: One deployment, one log, one monitoring setup, one rollback. Incidents are investigated in one place.
  • Why it still has good architecture: Clear boundaries, clear ownership, testable modules, and a well-defined migration path to services when genuinely needed.

Option 3: Microservices

  • What it is: Independent services, each deployed separately, each with its own database and codebase.
  • Independent deployment: Each team deploys their service without coordinating with others.
  • Independent scaling: Scale only the services that need it.
  • Separate ownership: One team owns one service end to end.
  • Communication: Over the network — HTTP, gRPC, or message queues. Slower and less reliable than a method call.
  • Operational complexity: Multiple deployments, multiple monitoring setups, distributed tracing, service discovery, API versioning, inter-service authentication.

The 5 most important differences

  1. Deployment independence: Only microservices offer truly independent deployment. This is the most often cited benefit — and the one that requires the most operational investment to realise.
  2. Data consistency: Monolith and modular monolith support full ACID transactions. Microservices require eventual consistency and distributed transaction patterns for cross-service operations.
  3. Cost: Infrastructure cost scales with service count in microservices. A modular monolith with 10 modules costs the same infrastructure as a monolith with 2 modules.
  4. Operational complexity: Microservices require observability infrastructure (distributed tracing, log aggregation) from day one. Without it, incidents become undebuggable.
  5. Migration path: A modular monolith with clean boundaries is easy to extract services from when genuinely needed. A traditional monolith is not.
PART 4 — THE EXAMPLE

Multi-Company Inventory and Order Management System

Same system — three designs. 10,000 companies, 1 million users, heavy dashboards and reporting, frequent order creation, limited DevOps team, small initial development teams.

Design A: Traditional Monolith

All 11 areas (Auth, Company, Customer, Product, Sales Order, Purchase, Inventory, Payment, Notification, Reporting, Audit) in one application, one database, no clear boundaries. Works well at start. Problems begin when: the Reporting module's complex queries slow down order creation, teams step on each other's database tables, and nobody can safely change the Inventory logic without checking 5 other modules.

The correct assessment: This design is appropriate for the first 12–18 months, with a small team (5–10 developers). The moment boundary problems start appearing, move to Design B — not Design C.

Design B: Modular Monolith

The 11 areas are grouped into coherent modules: Identity (Auth + Company), Catalog (Product + Customer), Commerce (Order + Purchase), Inventory, Payment, Notification, Reporting, Audit. Each module owns its database schema. Modules communicate via defined interfaces. One deployment, one database server (with schema separation).

The correct assessment: This is the right architecture for 10,000 companies with a limited DevOps team. It allows teams to work independently within their modules, prevents reporting from affecting transactions (reporting module uses read-only replicas internally), and keeps operational cost low. When reporting or notification genuinely need independent scaling — extract those first.

Design C: Microservices

Order Service, Inventory Service, Payment Service, Notification Service, Reporting Service, Identity Service — each independent. Each with its own database, its own deployment pipeline, its own monitoring. Communication via HTTP and message queues.

The correct assessment: This design is appropriate when the modular monolith can no longer meet the scaling or deployment independence requirements — when the Notification Service genuinely needs to scale to millions of messages per minute independent of orders, or when teams truly cannot coordinate releases even within a modular structure. For a limited DevOps team and a growing but not yet massive system, this introduces more operational risk than it solves.

THE RECOMMENDATION

Start with Design B (Modular Monolith). Measure real problems. Extract specific services (probably Reporting first, then Notification) only when a specific benefit is proven. Never extract all 11 areas simultaneously.

PART 5 — THE ARGUMENT

10 Reasons a Modular Monolith May Be Better Than Microservices

REASON 1 — SMALL OR MEDIUM TEAM

Situation: 5–20 developers building the system.

Why modular monolith helps: One codebase, clear module ownership, fast development, no service coordination overhead.

Why microservices may be excessive: Each service needs its own ops attention. A 10-person team managing 12 services spends more time on infrastructure than features.

When to revisit: When the team grows past 25–30 people and module teams genuinely cannot coordinate releases even within one application.

REASON 2 — LIMITED DEVOPS MATURITY

Situation: One or two DevOps engineers, basic CI/CD, no distributed tracing.

Why modular monolith helps: One deployment pipeline, one monitoring setup, one place to look when things break.

Why microservices may be excessive: Each service needs its own pipeline, monitoring, and on-call runbook. With two DevOps engineers, this is unmanageable.

When to revisit: When a dedicated platform team exists with distributed tracing, log aggregation, and automated deployment working end-to-end.

REASON 3 — SIMPLE DEPLOYMENT NEEDS

Situation: The business is happy with weekly or biweekly releases. There is no urgent need for independent deployment.

Why modular monolith helps: One artifact, one deployment, one rollback. Simpler release planning, simpler hotfixes.

Why microservices may be excessive: Coordinating releases across 12 services for a biweekly cycle creates unnecessary complexity. Microservices benefit is wasted.

When to revisit: When specific teams genuinely need to deploy multiple times per day, independently, and cannot do so within the current structure.

REASON 4 — STRONG NEED FOR TRANSACTIONS

Situation: Business operations require multiple data changes to succeed or fail together.

Why modular monolith helps: A database transaction spans all modules. Order creation, inventory reservation, and payment initiation can succeed or fail together in one database transaction.

Why microservices may be excessive: You replace one line of SQL with a saga pattern involving 6 network calls, compensating transactions, and retry logic. The business requirement is the same — the complexity is not.

When to revisit: When you can genuinely redesign business operations to be eventually consistent — which is a business decision, not just a technical one.

REASON 5 — CLOSELY CONNECTED BUSINESS OPERATIONS

Situation: Many business operations naturally span multiple modules.

Why modular monolith helps: Cross-module operations are method calls, not HTTP calls. Fast, reliable, transactional.

Why microservices may be excessive: If an order creation must call Order, Inventory, and Payment services simultaneously, you have introduced 3 failure points and 3 timeout scenarios into what was one reliable operation.

When to revisit: When operations can be redesigned with clear, event-driven decoupling that does not require synchronous cross-service calls.

REASON 6 — LOWER OPERATIONAL COST

Situation: Infrastructure budget is constrained.

Why modular monolith helps: One application server, one database server. Cost is predictable and low.

Why microservices may be excessive: 12 services × (compute + database + monitoring + pipeline) = 5–10× the infrastructure cost.

When to revisit: When the business case shows that independent scaling savings outweigh the infrastructure cost. This usually happens at significant scale.

REASON 7 — EASIER DEBUGGING

Situation: A production issue must be diagnosed quickly.

Why modular monolith helps: One log, one trace, one place to look. Stack traces span the whole operation in one view.

Why microservices may be excessive: Debugging a request that touches 6 services requires: distributed tracing infrastructure, correlation IDs, log aggregation, and cross-team communication to understand what each service did.

When to revisit: When distributed tracing infrastructure is in place and the team is experienced in reading cross-service traces.

REASON 8 — FASTER DEVELOPMENT

Situation: Feature velocity is the business priority.

Why modular monolith helps: Developers can add a feature that touches multiple modules in one pull request, one test run, one deployment.

Why microservices may be excessive: A feature spanning 3 services requires: 3 repositories, 3 pull requests, 3 deployments, and contract negotiation between teams. What takes hours in a modular monolith takes days in microservices.

When to revisit: When teams have stable enough service contracts that cross-service features are genuinely rare.

REASON 9 — EARLY-STAGE PRODUCT

Situation: The product is new and requirements are changing frequently.

Why modular monolith helps: Changing module boundaries is a refactoring session. Moving code between modules is cheap.

Why microservices may be excessive: Changing service boundaries requires updating APIs, databases, deployment pipelines, and possibly migrating data. Wrong boundaries are very expensive to fix in microservices.

When to revisit: When the product has found product-market fit, requirements are stable, and boundaries are well understood.

REASON 10 — LOWER INFRASTRUCTURE COMPLEXITY

Situation: The team has no service discovery, no API gateway, no distributed tracing in place.

Why modular monolith helps: None of those are needed. One application, one database, standard web infrastructure.

Why microservices may be excessive: You must build and operate service discovery, API gateway, distributed tracing, centralised log aggregation, and per-service authentication before the first microservice is useful. This is months of infrastructure work before a single business feature.

When to revisit: When that infrastructure is built, tested, and operated reliably.

PART 6 — BUILDING IT WELL

How to Build a Good Modular Monolith

A modular monolith is not an excuse for spaghetti code with folders. It requires deliberate design.

The golden rule

No module may directly access another module's database tables. All cross-module data access goes through the module's public interface (a service class or an event). This is the line that separates a "real" modular monolith from a regular monolith with nice folder names.

HOW A FAKE MODULAR MONOLITH LOOKS

The Order module reaches into the Inventory database table to check stock. The Reporting module reads directly from the Payment database. Every module shares the same database context with no ownership rules. This is a monolith with deceptive folder names — not a modular monolith.

ORDER MODULE

OWNS: orders table · order_lines table · order_status table

MUST NOT ACCESS: inventory tables · payment tables · notification tables

COMMUNICATES WITH: Inventory module (via IInventoryService interface) · Payment module (via IPaymentService interface) · raises OrderCreated event for Notification module

Responsibility: Order lifecycle management — creation, status updates, order history, order line management. Knows what was ordered and when. Does not know about stock levels or payment method details.

INVENTORY MODULE

OWNS: products_stock table · warehouse_locations table · stock_movements table · reservations table

MUST NOT ACCESS: orders tables · customer tables · payment tables

COMMUNICATES WITH: Order module (responds to reservation requests) · Purchase module (receives stock-in events). Publishes StockLevelChanged events.

Responsibility: Know what stock exists, where it is, and what is reserved. Does not know why an order was placed or how it was paid for.

PAYMENT MODULE

OWNS: payment_transactions table · payment_methods table · payment_status table

MUST NOT ACCESS: orders tables directly · inventory tables · customer profile beyond a customer ID reference

COMMUNICATES WITH: Order module (receives payment initiation requests) · external payment gateway. Publishes PaymentCompleted or PaymentFailed events.

Responsibility: Know what money was processed, when, and with what result. Does not know what was ordered or how much stock remains.

NOTIFICATION MODULE

OWNS: notification_log table · notification_templates table · delivery_preferences table

MUST NOT ACCESS: order tables · payment tables · inventory tables — it receives only what it needs via events

CONSUMES: OrderCreated event (gets order reference and customer contact) · PaymentCompleted event · ShipmentDispatched event. Calls email/SMS providers.

Responsibility: Send communications to customers. Does not own the business data — it only receives notifications about business events.

REPORTING MODULE

OWNS: reporting_snapshots table · aggregated_sales views (read-only, derived)

MUST NOT ACCESS: live transactional tables for complex queries (use read replicas or snapshots instead)

CONSUMES: Domain events from all modules to maintain reporting snapshots. Reads from a dedicated read replica or reporting schema.

Responsibility: Provide business intelligence without impacting transaction performance. Read-only. Updated asynchronously from domain events.

PART 7 — THE BOUNDARY QUESTIONS

How to Identify a Good Module Boundary

Eight questions. Ask them for every proposed module boundary. If you cannot answer yes to most of them, the boundary is wrong.

QUESTION GOOD ANSWER BAD ANSWER — RECONSIDER
1. Does this area have a clear, single responsibility? Yes — "this module manages all stock levels and movements" "It manages stock and also handles some order logic and customer preferences"
2. Does it have its own business rules? Yes — "this module decides reservation logic and stock validation" "Its rules are defined by what the Order module tells it to do"
3. Does it own specific data? Yes — specific database tables only this module writes to "Multiple modules write to the same table"
4. Does it change independently? "Changes to inventory logic rarely require changes to order logic" "Every inventory change requires corresponding order module changes"
5. Is it used by many other areas? If yes — it is a high-value module. Design its interface carefully. If every module calls it for everything — it may be doing too much (god module).
6. Does it require different scaling? "Notification has burst traffic. Order is steady." "All modules scale equally — no individual scaling need."
7. Does it have a different team owner? "The Inventory team owns this module fully" "Three teams touch this module regularly"
8. Can it be tested independently? "We can test Inventory module logic without running Order or Payment modules" "Our tests always need to start 4 modules to work"

What happens when boundaries are badly designed

  • Modules become tightly coupled: A change in one module silently breaks another. Teams blame each other. Code becomes hard to change.
  • The God Module problem: One module knows everything and is called by everything. It becomes a bottleneck and a single point of failure in logic.
  • Database coupling: Modules share tables, making it impossible to extract any module into a service later without splitting the database — the hardest migration task.
  • Test avalanche: Changing one module's logic requires running the entire test suite because boundaries are too interconnected.
PART 8 — THE DATABASE QUESTION

Shared Database: The Three Approaches

The database decision is the most consequential in any modular or distributed system design. Get it wrong and you will not be able to migrate later.

Approach A

SHARED, NO OWNERSHIP
  • One database, all modules share all tables
  • No enforced ownership
  • Fast to start
  • Becomes chaos as it grows
  • Schema changes break other modules
  • Cannot migrate to services later

Approach B

ONE DB, SCHEMA OWNERSHIP
  • One database, schemas per module
  • Only the owning module writes its schema
  • Transactions still work across schemas
  • Good balance of simplicity and clarity
  • Easier to migrate one schema to a separate DB later
  • Best starting point for modular monolith

Approach C

SEPARATE DB PER SERVICE
  • Each service has its own database
  • True independence
  • No shared transactions
  • Saga/event patterns required
  • Higher cost and operational complexity
  • Required for true microservices
THE RECOMMENDATION

Start with Approach B in a modular monolith. One database, clear schema ownership by module, enforced through code (no cross-module table access). This gives you architectural clarity without operational overhead. When you extract a module to a service, you promote its schema to a separate database — a well-defined operation rather than a painful split.

Do NOT claim every microservice must immediately have its own database — during migration, a service may share a database with the remaining monolith temporarily. But this must be a stepping stone with a clear plan, not a permanent design.

PART 9 — HOW MODULES TALK

Communication Between Modules

How modules communicate inside a modular monolith determines how well-separated they actually are.

METHOD HOW IT WORKS COUPLING LEVEL BEST FOR
Direct method calls (public service class) Order module calls IInventoryService.ReserveStock(orderId, items) — a defined interface, not an internal class. LOW — Interface can change implementation without breaking callers Synchronous operations where Order needs an immediate answer from Inventory
Internal application events Order module raises OrderCreated event. Notification module subscribes and sends an email. No direct knowledge of each other. LOWEST — Publisher does not know who is listening Side effects (notifications, audit, reporting updates) that do not need to be synchronous
Shared database access Order module reads from Inventory tables directly via SQL. VERY HIGH — Schema changes break callers immediately Should be avoided. If used at all, read-only from the same module's own replica

Order → Inventory example

Order creation needs to reserve stock. This is synchronous — the order cannot proceed without knowing if stock is available. The correct approach: Order module calls IInventoryService.TryReserveStock(). The interface defines the contract. The Inventory module implements it. Neither knows the other's internal database structure.

Order → Notification example

When an order is created, a confirmation email must be sent. This does not need to be synchronous — the customer can receive the email a few seconds later. The correct approach: Order module raises an OrderConfirmed event (internally, via an in-process event bus). Notification module subscribes to this event and sends the email. Order module never references Notification module's code.

THE RULE

Use interfaces for synchronous operations where the caller needs an answer. Use events for side effects where the caller does not need to wait. Never use direct database access across module boundaries.

PART 10 — THE SAFER PATH

The Migration Path

The most dangerous migration is "monolith to 20 microservices in one quarter". The safest migration is gradual and measurement-driven.

FIG. 19.3 — THE SAFE MIGRATION PATH: GRADUAL, MEASURED, ONE SERVICE AT A TIME

Why this gradual approach is safer than "monolith → 20 services":

  • Each step is reversible. An extracted service that creates problems can be re-incorporated.
  • The team builds operational capability (tracing, pipelines, monitoring) incrementally, not all at once.
  • Each extraction must prove its value before the next one begins.
  • The remaining modular monolith continues to function and deliver business value throughout.

Which module to extract first?

CANDIDATE WHY GOOD FIRST WHY NOT FIRST
Reporting Low risk — read-only. Solves a real problem (performance isolation). Can tolerate eventual consistency. —
Notification Genuinely async. Failure does not block orders. Clear boundary. Different scaling profile. —
Search Different technology (search index), different scaling, read-only business impact. —
Inventory Clear domain, well-bounded. Synchronous coupling to Order. Extraction requires saga/compensation design — significant complexity.
Payment Good security boundary. High business risk. Distributed transaction with Order is complex. Do this only after proving the pattern with lower-risk services.
Order — Almost certainly too tightly coupled to too many other modules to extract safely early.
PART 11 — EXTRACTION SIGNALS

10 Signals That a Module Should Become a Service

SIGNAL EXAMPLE WHY IT MATTERS CHECK BEFORE EXTRACTING
1. Independent scaling needed Notification sends 10M emails during a sale. Order processing stays steady at 500/min. Different scaling profiles require independent infrastructure. Can horizontal scaling of the monolith solve it cheaper? Usually yes at first.
2. Independent deployments needed The Notification team wants to ship template changes 5× per day. The rest of the system ships weekly. Deployment coupling slows the faster team. Is this actually happening or anticipated? Evidence of real friction?
3. Different release frequency Search index is updated every 15 minutes. Orders are deployed weekly. Different cadences in one application create risk for the slower team. How much does coordinated deployment actually cost?
4. Different team ownership A dedicated Analytics team wants to own Reporting end-to-end, including the technology stack. Team autonomy is one of the key microservices benefits. Is the team large enough to own and operate the service independently?
5. Failure isolation required If Reporting crashes, it must not affect Order creation. Business impact of failure in one area is disproportionate. Can you isolate Reporting within the modular monolith through async processing?
6. Technology needs are different Search requires Elasticsearch. The monolith uses SQL Server. A module with fundamentally different data infrastructure cannot stay in the same deployment. Is the technology difference genuinely necessary or just preferred?
7. Heavy processing File processing, PDF generation, or data transformation that would time out in the main application. Long-running work should not block application threads. Can background jobs within the monolith solve this first?
8. External integration boundary Payment gateway integration with PCI requirements that demand isolation. Regulatory or security requirements force a separate process. Does the regulation actually require process isolation, or just data isolation?
9. Security boundary Authentication service must be independently audited and protected. Separation limits the blast radius of a security incident. Can security isolation be achieved within the monolith with proper access controls?
10. Independently managed business area A newly acquired company brings its own Order module that must integrate but not merge. Business boundaries become technology boundaries. Is integration the long-term goal, or is this a stepping stone toward one unified system?
PART 12 — AVOID THESE

10 Common Architecture Mistakes

MISTAKE WHY IT IS BAD BETTER APPROACH
1. Creating services based on database tables You get 40 services with no business meaning. Every business operation calls 8 services. Define services by business capability — what the module does, not what data it stores.
2. Making every module a separate service on day one Operational cost and complexity immediately exceeds the team's capacity. Start with modular monolith. Extract only what has a proven case for extraction.
3. Sharing database tables between services Independent deployment becomes impossible. Schema changes require cross-service coordination. Each service owns its tables. Cross-service data is shared via APIs or events.
4. Splitting too early You lock in wrong boundaries before the domain is understood. Changing boundaries later is very expensive. Learn the domain in a monolith first. Extract after boundaries are proven.
5. Splitting too late A monolith with 100 developers and 5M users becomes an uncuttable knot. Add module boundaries early. Extract services before the monolith becomes unmaintainable.
6. Using microservices because competitors do Your problems are not your competitor's problems. Their solutions may not match your constraints. Define your specific problem first. Choose the architecture that solves it.
7. Ignoring operational cost Infrastructure and maintenance costs multiply with service count. Budget surprises derail the project. Model the full cost of each architecture before committing. Include operations, not just development.
8. Ignoring team maturity Microservices require distributed tracing, independent deployment, and on-call per service. Teams without this experience cannot operate them safely. Build operational capability before adopting microservices. Test with one service before committing to twelve.
9. Ignoring distributed transactions Business operations that span services require saga patterns. Most teams underestimate this complexity until they hit the first cross-service failure in production. Design the transaction strategy before designing the service. If it requires too many sagas — reconsider the boundaries.
10. Ignoring monitoring and troubleshooting Without distributed tracing and centralised logging, incidents in a microservices architecture take 10× longer to diagnose. Observability infrastructure must be in place before the first service is extracted. It is not optional.

Event schema changes between Order and Notification. Volume is actually manageable in the existing modular monolith.
8 Large reporting platform, complex queries, internal analysts EXTRACT AS SERVICE Reports should never run on the transactional database. Separate service or data warehouse protects transaction performance. Synchronisation delay makes reports stale. Real-time reporting is required — changes the design significantly.
9 Legacy monolith, 100 developers, slow deployments, many teams MODULAR MONOLITH FIRST The problem is internal coupling, not service count. Add boundaries first. Extract only after boundaries are clean. Management expects microservices. Architectural education required. After 12 months of modular monolith, if specific areas still need independence.
10 Product expected to grow 10× in 12 months MODULAR MONOLITH Speculative scale does not justify current complexity. Design with clean modules that can be extracted when growth arrives. Growth arrives before extraction is ready. Growth pattern is proven and specific areas are clearly the bottleneck.
11 Company with weak DevOps team, management wants microservices MODULAR MONOLITH + DEVOPS INVESTMENT Microservices without DevOps capability creates undebuggable incidents. Build capability first, then consider extraction. Management pressure to skip the learning phase. DevOps team grows and demonstrates readiness with successful isolated service deployment.
12 Company with many independent engineering teams, proven boundaries MICROSERVICES Teams, boundaries, and operational maturity all align. The conditions for microservices success are met. Distributed transaction complexity. Monitoring at scale. If teams frequently deploy together — boundaries are wrong, not the architecture.
PART 15 — THE INTERVIEW

20 Interview Questions: Modular Monolith vs Microservices

  1. Why would you choose a modular monolith over microservices?CORE▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Whether you can justify the simpler choice.
    SIMPLE: "When the team is small or operational maturity is limited."
    STRONG: "I choose a modular monolith when: the team size does not support one team per service, the business logic requires frequent cross-module transactions, operational maturity (distributed tracing, independent pipelines) is not yet in place, and the specific benefits of microservices (independent deployment, independent scaling) are not yet genuine business requirements. A modular monolith delivers most of the architectural clarity at a fraction of the operational cost."
    COMMON MISTAKE: "I always prefer microservices."
  2. Why not use microservices for every system?DECISION▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Cost awareness.
    SIMPLE: "They are expensive to operate."
    STRONG: "Microservices introduce distributed system complexity: network failures, distributed transactions, eventual consistency, per-service monitoring, service discovery, and API versioning. These costs are justified only when the specific benefits — deployment independence, scaling isolation, team autonomy — are genuine requirements at meaningful scale. For most systems, especially early-stage or small-team systems, these costs outweigh the benefits."
    COMMON MISTAKE: No concrete reasoning.
  3. How do you define a module boundary?DESIGN▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Domain thinking.
    SIMPLE: "By business responsibility."
    STRONG: "I define a module boundary by asking: does this area have a clear, single business responsibility? Does it own specific data? Does it have its own business rules? Can it change independently? Can it be tested independently? If yes to most of these, it is a good candidate for its own module. The boundary should be describable in one business sentence."
    COMMON MISTAKE: "By technical function — controllers, services, repositories."
  4. How do modules communicate in a modular monolith?DESIGN▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Communication patterns in context.
    SIMPLE: "Through defined interfaces."
    STRONG: "Modules communicate through two patterns. For synchronous operations (Order needs an inventory check) — through a defined interface (IInventoryService) that the module implements. For side effects (Order notifying Notification of a new order) — through internal domain events published and consumed within the application. Neither module accesses the other's database directly."
    COMMON MISTAKE: "They share the database — it is fine since it is one application."
  5. Should every module have a separate database?DESIGN▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Database ownership nuance.
    SIMPLE: "No — one database with schema ownership per module."
    STRONG: "In a modular monolith, one database with clear schema ownership per module is the right balance. Each module owns its tables — other modules cannot access them directly. This preserves transaction support and operational simplicity, while ensuring that when a module needs to become a service, its schema is already isolated. Moving from schema isolation to a separate database is straightforward. Moving from shared tables is not."
    COMMON MISTAKE: "Each module must have its own database or it is not a real module."
  6. How do you prevent modules from becoming tightly coupled?DESIGN▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Practical discipline.
    SIMPLE: "Strict rules about cross-module database access."
    STRONG: "The primary rule: no module may access another module's database tables. All cross-module access goes through defined interfaces or events. This rule must be enforced technically — linting rules, code review standards, architecture tests. Without enforcement, developers under time pressure will break boundaries 'just this once' and the modular structure degrades quickly."
    COMMON MISTAKE: "Rely on developers to follow the rules voluntarily."
  7. How do you migrate a modular monolith to microservices?STRATEGY▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Migration thinking.
    SIMPLE: "Extract one module at a time."
    STRONG: "The clean module boundaries make extraction straightforward. Choose the module with the strongest case for extraction (usually Reporting or Notification). Promote its schema to a separate database. Create an independent deployment pipeline. Shift traffic using the Strangler Fig pattern. Run the service alongside the monolith. Measure that the extraction solved the stated problem. Only then consider the next extraction."
    COMMON MISTAKE: "Extract all modules simultaneously."
  8. Which module would you extract first?DESIGN▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Risk and sequencing judgement.
    SIMPLE: "Reporting or Notification."
    STRONG: "I would start with Reporting — it is read-only (low risk if it fails), it solves a real problem (performance isolation from transactions), it can tolerate eventual consistency, and it has a clear, well-understood boundary. Notification is a close second — it is genuinely async and failure-safe. I would not start with Order or Payment — these are the highest-risk extractions and should come last after the team has operational experience."
    COMMON MISTAKE: "Start with the Order Service — it is the core of the business." It is precisely because it is the core that it should be last.
  9. When is a modular monolith not enough?DECISION▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Knowing the limits.
    SIMPLE: "When independent deployment or scaling is genuinely needed."
    STRONG: "A modular monolith is not enough when: teams genuinely cannot coordinate releases (not just prefer not to), when a specific area has dramatically different scaling requirements that cannot be met by horizontal scaling of the whole application, when a regulatory boundary requires process-level isolation, or when a technology boundary prevents the module from existing in the same process. These are the genuine signals."
    COMMON MISTAKE: "When the application becomes large." Size alone is not the trigger.
  10. How do you explain the modular monolith decision to management?BUSINESS▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Business communication.
    SIMPLE: "Lower cost and risk for the same architectural benefit."
    STRONG: "I frame it as delivering the organisational benefits of microservices — team ownership, clear responsibilities, independent development — without the infrastructure cost and operational overhead. I show the specific problems it solves (deployment coupling, reporting performance) and the problems it does not introduce (distributed transactions, complex monitoring). I explain the migration path: when microservices are genuinely needed, the clean module structure makes extraction safe and planned, not a crisis."
    COMMON MISTAKE: "We are not ready for microservices." Sounds defensive. Lead with what it does, not what it avoids.
  11. Can a modular monolith scale?CAPACITY▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Scaling understanding.
    SIMPLE: "Yes — horizontally, like any application."
    STRONG: "A modular monolith scales horizontally behind a load balancer, exactly like any stateless application. Multiple instances of the same application run in parallel. The database scales with read replicas, caching, and partitioning. This satisfies the majority of scaling requirements. The only scaling it cannot provide is independent scaling of one module (e.g., scaling Notification at 100× while Order stays at 1×). That specific need is the signal to extract Notification as a service."
    COMMON MISTAKE: "A monolith cannot scale." This is false and shows misunderstanding.
  12. What is a "fake" modular monolith?DESIGN▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Anti-pattern awareness.
    SIMPLE: "A monolith with folders but no real boundaries."
    STRONG: "A fake modular monolith is one that has the appearance of modules — separate folders, separate classes — but no enforced boundaries. Modules access each other's database tables directly. Any module can call any other module's internal methods. There is no real separation — just organisation. The test is simple: can any developer change one module without understanding what it does to another? If not, the boundaries are fake."
    COMMON MISTAKE: Thinking namespace organisation alone constitutes a modular monolith.
  13. What is Conway's Law, and how does it apply?CONTEXT▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Organisation-architecture connection.
    SIMPLE: "The architecture mirrors the team structure."
    STRONG: "Conway's Law states that a system's design reflects the communication structure of the organisation that built it. In practice: a single team tends to build a monolith. Module teams in one codebase tend to build a modular monolith. Separate, independent teams tend to build microservices. This means the right architecture also depends on the organisation design, not just the technical requirements. Forcing microservices on a team without the structure to support them will result in poorly designed service boundaries."
    COMMON MISTAKE: Treating Conway's Law as optional context rather than a design constraint.
  14. Should you re-architect from scratch or evolve?STRATEGY▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Risk preference.
    SIMPLE: "Evolve — always."
    STRONG: "Rebuilding from scratch ('the second system effect') is almost always more expensive and riskier than it appears. The existing system has knowledge encoded in it — business rules discovered through years of operation — that is invisible in the code and easily lost in a rewrite. I prefer evolution: add module boundaries to the existing system, gradually extract services when justified, and keep the system running and delivering value throughout."
    COMMON MISTAKE: "Rewrite as microservices — the codebase is not worth saving." It usually is.
  15. What metrics would you monitor to know if the modular monolith is working?MONITORING▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Measurement thinking.
    SIMPLE: "Deployment frequency, incident rates, and feature velocity."
    STRONG: "I track: (1) Deployment frequency — are teams deploying faster after adding module boundaries? (2) Deployment coordination cost — how often do releases require cross-module coordination? (3) Incident containment — do issues in one module still affect others? (4) Developer productivity — how long does it take to find and fix a bug? (5) Architecture drift — are teams violating module boundaries? These tell me whether the modular structure is delivering its intended benefits."
    COMMON MISTAKE: "We will know if something breaks."
  16. How do you test a modular monolith?TESTING▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Quality thinking.
    SIMPLE: "Unit tests per module, integration tests for the whole application."
    STRONG: "Each module has its own unit test suite that tests only that module's logic using stubs for other module interfaces. Cross-module integration tests test specific inter-module contracts. End-to-end tests validate the complete application. Because everything runs in one process, integration tests are much faster and simpler than in a microservices architecture — no service mocking or contract tests required."
    COMMON MISTAKE: "We test the whole application together always." That is testing a monolith, not a modular monolith.
  17. What is the strangler fig pattern?PATTERN▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Migration knowledge.
    SIMPLE: "Gradually replace monolith functionality with new services."
    STRONG: "The Strangler Fig pattern allows gradual migration without downtime. A new service is built alongside the monolith. An API gateway or proxy routes specific requests to the new service and others to the monolith. As the service grows, more traffic is shifted to it. Eventually, the monolith 'shrinks' as its functionality is taken over. This approach allows continuous delivery throughout the migration — no big bang cutover."
    COMMON MISTAKE: "We freeze features, rewrite everything, then switch over."
  18. How would you handle a reporting module that is slowing down order processing?SCENARIO▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Practical problem solving.
    SIMPLE: "Move reporting to a read replica."
    STRONG: "First, within the modular monolith: route all reporting queries to a read-only database replica. This is cheap, immediate, and often solves the problem. If the reporting module's queries are still complex enough to cause issues, extract it as a dedicated Reporting Service with its own database populated asynchronously from domain events. The key: solve it at the lowest complexity level first. A read replica is far simpler than a separate service."
    COMMON MISTAKE: "Extract all modules into microservices." — Solves a problem with a sledgehammer.
  19. How does module structure affect future migration?DESIGN▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Foresight.
    SIMPLE: "Clean modules make extraction easy."
    STRONG: "A modular monolith with clean schema ownership is future-ready by design. When a module needs to become a service, the work is: promote the module's schema to a separate database, create a deployment pipeline, expose the module's internal interface as an API, and shift traffic. Two to four weeks of structured work. A monolith without boundaries requires first creating those boundaries (months of refactoring), then doing the extraction. The modular monolith amortises the future cost into present discipline."
    COMMON MISTAKE: "We will refactor before we extract." — It is much harder than it sounds.
  20. What is the right size for a module?DESIGN▸ SHOW ANSWERSTESTING: Boundary intuition.
    SIMPLE: "Large enough to be a meaningful business capability."
    STRONG: "A module should be large enough to contain a meaningful business capability that one team can fully own — including its data, its business rules, and its interfaces. Too small and you have the same problem as nano-microservices — excessive inter-module calls for every operation. Too large and no team can own it effectively. The right size is where: one business sentence describes it, one team owns it, and it rarely needs to change because another module changed."
    COMMON MISTAKE: "One module per class" or "one module per table."
PART 16 — THE EXERCISE

Architecture Exercise

THE SCENARIO

A company has a .NET application with Customer, Product, Order, Inventory, Payment, Notification, and Reporting. Management is asking for microservices.

Current situation: 20 developers · 500,000 users · 50,000 orders/day · Reporting affects transaction performance · Teams want faster releases · DevOps team is small · Business expects 5× growth · Management is asking for microservices

Your task — answer all 10 questions before reading the solution:

  1. Challenge the requirement — what problem is management actually trying to solve?
  2. Identify the real problems in the current system.
  3. Decide: Monolith, Modular Monolith, or Microservices?
  4. Design the recommended architecture with module boundaries.
  5. Define data ownership for each module.
  6. Explain how modules communicate.
  7. Identify what should possibly become a service later.
  8. Explain risks of the recommended architecture.
  9. Explain risks of the rejected architecture (microservices).
  10. Describe the migration strategy.

1. Challenging the requirement: "Management wants microservices" is not a problem statement. Ask: are teams blocked from releasing because of each other? Is specific business area performance creating user impact? These are the real problems to solve — not the architecture style.

2. Real problems: (a) Reporting affecting transaction performance — a read isolation problem. (b) Teams wanting faster releases — a deployment coupling problem. Both are solvable without full microservices.

3. Recommendation: Modular Monolith. 20 developers and a small DevOps team cannot operate 7+ microservices safely. The specific problems do not require that solution.

4. Module design: Identity (Auth + Customer), Catalog (Product), Commerce (Order), Inventory, Payment, Notification, Reporting. 7 modules, one application, one deployment.

5. Data ownership: Each module owns its database schema. No cross-module direct table access. Reporting module uses read-only replica or read from snapshots built from domain events.

6. Communication: Commerce → Inventory via IInventoryService interface (synchronous reservation). Commerce raises OrderCreated event, consumed by Notification (async). Reporting consumes all domain events to build read models.

7. Future service candidates: Reporting (read isolation), Notification (async scaling). Not Order, Inventory, or Payment until the team has proven operational capability with the first two.

8. Risks of recommended: Developers skip boundary rules under pressure. Read isolation for Reporting must be actively enforced. Module teams must be defined and respected.

9. Risks of microservices (rejected): Small DevOps team cannot support 7 independent deployment pipelines. Distributed transactions required for Order+Inventory+Payment operations. Infrastructure cost multiplies. Debugging cross-service failures without distributed tracing is nearly impossible.

10. Migration strategy: Phase 1 (Month 1–3): Add module boundaries to existing codebase, route Reporting to read replica. Phase 2 (Month 4–6): Extract Reporting as a service, prove the pattern. Phase 3 (Month 7+): Evaluate based on what is measured — only extract if benefit is proven.

PART 17 — THE CHALLENGE

20-Minute Architect Challenge

THE CHALLENGE

"A successful monolithic application is becoming difficult to scale and maintain. Management wants to convert the complete system into microservices."

Business context: 300,000 users · 80,000 orders/day · 15 developers in 3 teams · 2 DevOps engineers · £15,000/month infrastructure budget · Weekly releases, often delayed due to coordination · Reporting slows the main database 4 hours daily · Application is 4 years old, relatively clean codebase · Domain is well-understood

Your task — decide and justify:

  1. Are microservices actually required?
  2. Is Modular Monolith better at this stage?
  3. Which problems must be solved first?
  4. Which modules should remain together?
  5. Which module could be extracted?
  6. What is the migration plan?
  7. How will success be measured?

THINK THROUGH YOUR ANSWER BEFORE READING THE MODEL RESPONSE

1. Are microservices required? Not yet. The real problems — reporting performance and release coordination — are solvable without full microservices. The team of 15 and 2 DevOps engineers is the limiting factor.

2. Is Modular Monolith better? Yes, at this stage. The codebase is clean and 4 years old — the domain is well-understood, making module boundaries clear and reliable. This is the ideal starting point for a modular monolith.

3. Problems to solve first: (a) Reporting slowing the database — immediate: route to a read replica. Medium term: extract as a service. (b) Release coordination — add module ownership boundaries so teams can release within their domain confidently.

4. Modules remaining together: Order, Inventory, Payment — tightly transactional. Customer, Catalog — closely related reads. These stay in the modular monolith.

5. Module to extract first: Reporting. Read-only, solves the immediate database performance problem, tolerates eventual consistency, well-bounded, lowest risk. Second candidate: Notification.

6. Migration plan: Month 1: Define module boundaries in code, assign team ownership. Month 2: Route all reports to a read replica. Month 3–4: Extract Reporting as an independent service. Month 5: Measure impact on release velocity and database performance. Month 6+: Decide on next extraction based on measurement.

7. Success metrics: Database CPU during reporting window (target: below 30%). Release frequency per team (target: teams release independently within their module without waiting for others). Incident rate (should not increase). Developer satisfaction with code ownership. Only if all metrics improve do we continue extractions.

DAY 19 — CLOSE

10 Rules for Choosing Between Monolith, Modular Monolith, and Microservices

01

Architecture solves problems, not trends.

Choose the architecture that solves the specific problem you have — not the one your competitor uses or the conference speaker recommended.

02

The modular monolith is the missing middle.

Most systems should be here, not in a traditional monolith and not in microservices. Learn it. Use it. Defend it.

03

Module boundaries are enforced, not documented.

A boundary that relies on developers remembering the rules will be broken. It must be enforced through code organisation, architectural tests, and code review standards.

04

No module accesses another module's database.

This is the single most important rule in a modular monolith. Violate it once and the modular structure starts collapsing.

05

Extract the service that solves a real problem.

The first extraction must fix something — a performance problem, a deployment independence problem. If it does not fix anything, it is over-engineering.

06

Start with Reporting or Notification, not Order or Payment.

Low-risk extractions first. Build operational capability. Then consider the high-risk, high-value services.

07

One extraction at a time, with measurement.

Every extraction must prove its value before the next begins. "Measure, then decide" is not slow — it is responsible.

08

Team size and DevOps maturity set the architecture ceiling.

The most important non-technical factors. A team of 6 cannot operate microservices sustainably. An organisation with no DevOps team cannot either.

09

Clean module boundaries now make extraction cheap later.

A well-designed modular monolith is future-ready. The same boundaries that make it easy to work with today make it easy to split tomorrow.

10

The score is a guide. The architect makes the call.

Frameworks and scorecards surface the question. Architectural judgement — shaped by context, experience, and honesty about constraints — answers it.

ONE-MINUTE INTERVIEW ANSWER — WHY MODULAR MONOLITH OVER MICROSERVICES?

"I choose a modular monolith when the team is not large enough to support one fully independent team per service, when operational maturity is not yet in place for distributed tracing and independent deployment pipelines, when the business logic requires frequent cross-module transactions, and when the specific benefits of microservices — deployment independence, independent scaling, failure isolation — are not yet genuine business requirements. A well-designed modular monolith delivers most of the architectural clarity that microservices offer, at a fraction of the operational cost and risk, and with the added benefit of being extractable into services when those requirements genuinely arrive."

ONE POWERFUL ARCHITECT STATEMENT

"Architecture is not about what looks sophisticated on a diagram. It is about what the business can operate, what the team can maintain, and what the system genuinely needs — today, not in a hypothetical future. A modular monolith that is actually working is worth more than a microservices architecture that is actually failing."

The best architects build the simplest system that works, not the most impressive system that could work. The difference between those two is usually a modular monolith.
