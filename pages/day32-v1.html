SECTION 01 — THE DIFFERENCE

What Is System-Level Judgement?

Technical knowledge is a catalogue. Technical judgement is a choice made when the catalogue does not tell you what to do. After twelve years with .NET, SQL Server, Redis, and APIs, the catalogue is not the scarce skill. The scarce skill is knowing what matters, what to ignore, what to trade, and what to reject.

KNOWLEDGE

"I know microservices. I can draw a gateway, a set of ASP.NET services, a bus, and a database per service."

JUDGEMENT

"I know microservices. I would not use them here. The team is eight people, orders must commit with stock in one transaction, and nobody is staffed to run a distributed system at 2 a.m."

Knowledge
    ↓
"I know microservices."

Judgement
    ↓
"I know microservices, but I would not use them here because
the team size, deployment complexity, transaction boundaries,
and operational cost do not justify them."

Experienced architects are not ranked by how many technologies they can name. A principal who has shipped one boring, operable, correct system will beat a candidate who has toured every conference architecture. Interviewers already assume you can build an API. They are listening for whether you can select, defend, live with the consequence, and change the decision when an assumption dies.

SKILL WHAT IT SOUNDS LIKE WHAT IT IS NOT
Knowing options SQL Server, Cosmos, Redis, RabbitMQ, Azure Service Bus, a modular monolith, microservices, RAG, fine-tuning. A decision. A list is the start of thinking, not the end.
Selecting an option "For this order ledger I will use SQL Server, not a document store." Preference. Selection names the constraint that made the other options worse.
Defending the option "Money movement needs transactions, constraints, and a story an auditor can read." Repeating the vendor's slogan. Defense survives "why not the other one?"
Understanding consequences "We will be slower to add a second write model. Reporting will need a replica, not the OLTP box." Hoping the downside will not show up. Every real choice creates a new problem.
Knowing when to change "If write contention on Orders passes what indexes can fix, we split the hot aggregate — not the whole estate." Loyalty to the diagram. A decision without a revisit trigger is a religion.
CONCEPT

System-level judgement is the ability to make a technical decision when requirements are incomplete, constraints conflict, and several reasonable options exist. The output is not a tool. The output is a choice, a sacrifice, a risk you accept, and a way to know you were wrong.

WHY IT MATTERS

A strong .NET lead can implement any of the options. An architect is paid to stop the organisation implementing the expensive wrong one. Interviews for architect roles fail candidates who jump to Kubernetes, Redis, or microservices before they have framed the problem.

EXAMPLE

A retailer with 40 developers wants "an event-driven microservice platform" for a new returns flow. Volume is 2,000 returns a day. The existing modular monolith already owns orders and stock in SQL Server. Judgement: build the returns module inside the monolith, publish an outbox event for email and refund status, and do not create a new network boundary for a workflow that must stay consistent with the order.

ARCHITECT THINKING

Who feels the failure? What is the real scale? What must be correct together? What can the team operate? What is hard to reverse? What would I refuse even if a stakeholder likes the diagram?

TRADE-OFFS

You give up independent deploy of returns and a fashionable architecture slide. You keep one transaction, one debugger, one on-call surface. You accept a later extraction cost if returns truly become a separate product with its own release train.

COMMON MISTAKE

Treating "I have used it in production" as a reason to use it again. Familiarity is a constraint, not a design. The opposite mistake is choosing the unfamiliar tool to prove seniority.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"You know microservices. Would you use them for this?"

STRONG ANSWER

"Not from the label. I would use them if a boundary needs its own deploy cycle, its own scale, or its own failure domain, and the team can pay for tracing, contracts, and partial failure. If this is one product, one team, and one SQL transaction, I would keep a modular monolith and draw the boundaries in code. I can extract a service later. I cannot easily glue a distributed transaction back together."

FOLLOW-UP

"The CTO already promised microservices to the board. Now what?" — You do not win by mocking the promise. You separate the public word from the first release: "We can keep service boundaries in the code and deploy independently when a boundary earns it. Shipping twelve network hops in quarter one makes the promise, and also makes the outage." Offer a staged path the CTO can still describe as a platform.

THE SENTENCE YOU SHOULD BE ABLE TO SAY IN 60 SECONDS

The problem I believe we are solving is X. The constraint that dominates is Y. I considered A and B. I choose A because it protects Y. I am trading away Z. I will know I was wrong if this metric moves.

SECTION 02 — THE LOOP

The Architect Decision Mindset

Judgement is not a mood. It is a repeatable loop. Use it when a director says "what would you choose?" and when you are alone at a whiteboard with a vague ticket. Skip a step and you will invent a requirement or fall in love with a tool.

  1. ProblemSay the business pain in one sentence a finance partner would recognise. "Reduce failed checkouts at peak" is a problem. "Introduce Redis" is not.SAY: "THE PAIN IS… NOT THE TOOL."
  2. ClarifyAsk what is known, what is guessed, and what is wished. Separate the user's words from the constraint. "Scalable" might mean a sale next Friday, or a five-year platform fantasy.ASK BEFORE YOU DRAW.
  3. Identify constraintsTime, money, team size, skills, on-call maturity, compliance, data residency, existing SQL Server estate, contracts already signed. Constraints remove options. That is their gift.A CONSTRAINT YOU HIDE WILL AMBUSH THE DESIGN.
  4. Identify risksWhat can hurt customers, money, data, or the release date? Name likelihood roughly and impact honestly. A rare total data loss outranks a common slow report.RISK ACCEPTANCE IS A DECISION.
  5. Define decision criteriaBefore you fall in love with an option, write the tests: correctness of money, p95 under 300ms, ship in 12 weeks, operable by five people, no data leaving the country. Criteria stop a loud preference winning.CRITERIA FIRST, OPTIONS SECOND.
  6. Generate realistic optionsTwo or three options the team could actually run. Include "do nothing" and "buy". Exclude the architecture you saw at a conference unless this team can operate it.IF EVERY OPTION IS FASHIONABLE, YOU HAVE NOT LOOKED.
  7. Compare trade-offsFor each option: what it improves, what it makes worse, the second-order effect, the operational cost. Hidden trade-offs live in failure, not in the happy-path slide.WHAT NEW PROBLEM DOES THIS CREATE?
  8. ChoosePick. Say the sentence. A meeting that ends in "it depends" without a choice has not done architecture. You may choose a temporary option with a review date.A DECISION HAS AN OWNER AND A DATE.
  9. Explain consequencesTell stakeholders what they are not getting. Speed without a second region. A monolith without independent scale. An LLM feature that can be wrong. Silence here becomes betrayal later.NAME THE SACRIFICE IN THE ROOM.
  10. Define validationWhat evidence, in what window, would confirm or kill the decision? Latency, error rate, cost per order, failed payment rate, hallucination rate on a labelled set, on-call pages.NO METRIC, NO DECISION.
  11. Revisit when assumptions changeWrite the trigger now. "If peak checkout exceeds 200 RPS and SQL CPU stays above 70% after index work, we revisit read replicas and stock contention." Changing your mind is competence, not weakness.LOYALTY TO A DEAD ASSUMPTION IS THE FAILURE.

How to avoid the eight classic traps

Premature decision

You pick RabbitMQ in minute two because the last project used it. Slow down until you know whether the user must wait for the result.

WAIT FOR THE CONSTRAINT.
Technology-first thinking

"We should be on Kubernetes" is not a problem statement. Ask what deploy, scale, or isolation pain exists today that App Service cannot carry.

NAME THE PAIN THE TOOL REMOVES.
Personal preference

You like Dapper, they like EF Core. That is a local choice unless it changes operability or a hot path. Do not spend the architecture meeting on taste.

IS THIS A ONE-WAY DOOR?
Overengineering

Event sourcing, CQRS, and six services for an internal tool with 40 users. Complexity is a loan. Interest is on-call.

WHO OPERATES THIS AT 2 A.M.?
Underengineering

A payment callback with no idempotency key because "the provider is reliable". Money paths do not get optimism.

WHAT IS THE COST OF BEING WRONG ONCE?
Copying famous architectures

Netflix's estate solved Netflix's org chart and Netflix's scale. Your eight-person .NET team does not have that problem or that platform team.

WHICH OF THEIR CONSTRAINTS DO WE SHARE?
Designing for imaginary scale

Sharding SQL Server on day one because a slide said 10 million users. You will pay distributed-data pain before you have a single index problem.

WHAT BREAKS AT 10× OF REAL TRAFFIC?
Solving non-problems

A multi-region active-active design for a system whose users are in one country and whose downtime budget is hours, not seconds.

WHAT HAPPENS IF WE DO NOTHING?
CONCEPT

The loop forces problem, constraints, and criteria to appear before technology. It also forces a revisit trigger, so the decision is allowed to die.

WHY IT MATTERS

Senior developers decide inside a service. Architects decide across time, money, failure, and people. The loop is how you stay in that wider frame when the room is impatient.

EXAMPLE

A product owner wants "AI search" on the employee handbook by Friday. Loop: the problem is finding policy, not "using an LLM". Constraint: data is internal and must not be sent to a public endpoint. Risk: a wrong answer about safety policy. Criteria: cited answer, permission-safe, ship a useful slice in two weeks. Options: keyword search now, RAG on Azure OpenAI in a private network next, fine-tune never for this corpus. Choose RAG behind a feature flag after a 50-question eval set. Revisit if citation accuracy is under 90%.

ARCHITECT THINKING

Am I solving the stated sentence or the real pain? Which step am I skipping because I am excited? What would a sceptical operations lead ask?

TRADE-OFFS

The loop is slower in the first meeting and faster over the quarter. You trade the feeling of progress for a decision you can defend. In a true incident, you compress the loop — you do not delete it.

COMMON MISTAKE

Running the loop silently and presenting only the tool. The interviewer cannot score thinking they cannot hear. Say the rejected option out loud.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"Walk me through how you would decide, not what you would build."

STRONG ANSWER

"I would restate the problem, list what I do not know, and name the constraint that will dominate — usually correctness, date, team, or cost. Then two or three options we could actually operate. I pick one, say what I am giving up, and name the metric that would make me reverse it. I would not open with a product name."

FOLLOW-UP

"You have ten minutes and the VP is in the room." Compress to: problem, dominant constraint, choice, sacrifice, next fact you need. Offer to go deeper on the one-way door only.

SECTION 03 — INCOMPLETE FACTS

Requirements Under Ambiguity

The brief will be thin. That is normal. Your job is not to freeze, and not to invent a company in your head and then design for it. State assumptions. Mark which ones are safe for a workshop and which ones must be validated before build.

THE RULE

Do not confuse missing information with permission to invent requirements. "They did not mention consistency, so I will use eventual consistency" is how orders get oversold. Silence is a question, not a default.

The brief: "We need a scalable order management platform."

That sentence contains a noun and a compliment. It does not contain a system. Here is what an architect asks, grouped so you do not forget a class of uncertainty under pressure.

Functional uncertaintyIs this capture, fulfilment, returns, or all three? Who creates an order — web, store, EDI, marketplace? What is the system of record today, and what must not break during migration?
Non-functional uncertaintyWhat latency can checkout tolerate? What does "scalable" mean in orders per minute at peak, not in a marketing number? What is the downtime budget in a month?
Business uncertaintyIs this a revenue path or an internal convenience? What is the cost of a wrong order versus a slow page? Is there a seasonal peak, a contractual go-live, a penalty?
Data uncertaintyHow long are orders kept? Are prices, tax, and addresses snapshotted? What is sensitive? Can a row be deleted, or only forgotten under a policy? Who owns the data model?
User uncertaintyCustomers, warehouse staff, finance, partner APIs — which journey is the one that loses money if it fails? Are users in one country? Do they retry angrily?
Scale uncertaintyOrders today, orders at the next campaign, read/write ratio, payload size, fan-out to other systems. Registered users are not concurrent users. Concurrent users are not RPS.
Compliance uncertaintyPCI scope, tax records, consumer privacy, industry retention, audit. "We are not a bank" does not mean "we can lose an order row".
Availability uncertaintyIs 99.5% acceptable (about 3.6 hours a month)? Who promises the number to customers? Which dependency — payment, tax, stock — is allowed to stop checkout?
Cost uncertaintyIs there a monthly ceiling? Is the cheaper design allowed to need more people? A design the team cannot staff is not cheap. It is unpaid operations.

SAFE TO ASSUME — IF YOU SAY SO

  • For a workshop, one primary region unless residency is mentioned.
  • Browser and API clients exist; you do not yet know the split.
  • The team can run ASP.NET Core and SQL Server if that is the estate you were told about.
  • You may assume a sale peak is higher than average, and you must ask for the multiple.
  • You may assume failure will happen. You may not assume which failure is acceptable.

MUST VALIDATE BEFORE BUILD

  • Peak orders per minute and the size of the order payload.
  • Whether stock decrement and payment must succeed or fail together.
  • Data residency, retention, and who may see a row.
  • The real downtime budget, and whether it is contractual.
  • Team size, on-call maturity, and the date that cannot move.
  • Whether an LLM or partner API is allowed to block the money path.
CONCEPT

Ambiguity is handled by questions, labelled assumptions, and a refusal to treat silence as a requirement. You design a thin slice against stated assumptions, and you park the rest.

WHY IT MATTERS

Invented requirements produce invented architecture. That is how a 500-user internal tool receives a multi-region active-active design, and how a real money path receives "eventual consistency" because nobody asked.

EXAMPLE

Interview prompt: "Design a scalable order platform." You say: "I am going to assume a mid-market retailer, one country, peak 50 orders per second at a campaign, SQL Server already in the estate, and a 99.9% target until you correct me. I am not assuming microservices, and I am not assuming stock can be stale. Which of those is wrong?"

ARCHITECT THINKING

Which unknown changes the shape of the system, and which unknown only changes a setting? Shape-changing unknowns — consistency, residency, scale order-of-magnitude, team size — get asked first.

TRADE-OFFS

Asking many questions can look slow. Asking none looks confident and is reckless. In an interview, three sharp questions beat fifteen nervous ones. Pick the questions that would delete an option.

COMMON MISTAKE

Filling every gap with the architecture you wanted to present anyway, then calling the fill "assumptions" at the end so you cannot be challenged.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"The product owner is vague. How do you proceed?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I separate facts from guesses. I ask the few questions that would change the design — scale, correctness, date, compliance, who operates it. For the rest I state an assumption and what I would do if it is wrong. I do not invent a 10-million-user requirement because the sentence said scalable."

FOLLOW-UP

"They cannot answer scale." Then design for the current known peak plus a stated headroom, put the bottleneck on a dashboard, and write the trigger that forces a redesign. Do not shard a database you have not measured.

SECTION 04 — THE CHECKLIST

The Most Important Decision Questions

Memorise the questions, not the answers. In a design interview or a steering meeting, this list keeps you from decorating a diagram before you know what the diagram is for. Tick the ones you actually asked. The ticks save in this browser.

QUESTION WHY IT CHANGES THE DESIGN ASKED
What problem are we solving? Stops technology-first work. If you cannot say the pain, you are not ready to draw boxes.
Who is affected? Customer, warehouse, finance, on-call, partner. The person who feels failure decides the priority.
What happens if we do nothing? Sometimes the honest architecture is "not this quarter". If nothing bad happens, you are solving a non-problem.
What is the scale? Peak RPS, payload, data size, fan-out. Without a number, "scalable" is a feeling.
What is the expected growth? 10× next campaign is a different design from 10× in five years. Headroom is not the same as a rewrite.
What must be available? Checkout may need 99.9%. A monthly report may not. Availability is per journey, not per company slogan.
What can fail? If you cannot name a failure you are willing to show the user, you will overbuild every path.
What must be strongly consistent? Money, stock decrement, permissions. These do not go in a cache "for performance" by default.
What can be eventually consistent? Search, recommendations, email, analytics, most AI answers. This is where you buy speed and decoupling.
What data is sensitive? Decides encryption, tenancy, logging, and whether an LLM is even allowed to see the text.
What is the cost of failure? A wrong price is not a slow page. Cost of failure sets how much complexity you are allowed to add.
What is the cost of complexity? Every new moving part is a future incident. A five-person team cannot own twelve deployables and a custom mesh.
What is the delivery deadline? A date cuts scope. It should not silently cut idempotency, auth, or backups.
What is the team's capability? The best design is the one this team can build, run, and debug. A genius diagram with no owners is theatre.
What is the operational maturity? If nobody can read a trace or restore a backup, do not add a system that requires both to stay safe.
What is reversible? Internal class shape, a log field, a private endpoint. Move. Do not hold the programme hostage to taste.
What is difficult to reverse? Public API contracts, tenancy model, encryption, the database that now holds five years of orders. Slow down.
CONCEPT

These questions are a pre-flight. They force scale, failure, sensitivity, people, and reversibility into the open before a box is drawn.

WHY IT MATTERS

Developers ask "how do I build this?" Architects ask "should this exist, for whom, under what failure, at what cost, and what must stay true?" That difference is the interview.

EXAMPLE

Two teams argue Redis versus "just scale SQL". The checklist ends the argument: the data is a product catalogue, it can be 30 seconds stale, the database CPU is the pain, the team already runs Redis for sessions. Cache the catalogue. Do not cache the wallet balance on the next screen — that question has a different answer.

ARCHITECT THINKING

Which three questions, if answered differently, would delete my favourite option? Ask those. The others can wait.

TRADE-OFFS

A full checklist in every meeting will exhaust people. Use the full list when the decision is a one-way door. Use five questions when it is reversible.

COMMON MISTAKE

Asking the questions, then ignoring the answers because the diagram is already in the slide. The list is useless if it does not change a choice.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"What do you need to know before you design?"

STRONG ANSWER

"The problem, who feels failure, the real peak, what must be correct, what may be stale, what is sensitive, the date, the team who will operate it, and which decision we cannot easily undo. I will state assumptions for the rest and keep going."

FOLLOW-UP

"Give me your first three questions for an AI assistant on internal documents." — Who may see which document? What happens if the answer is wrong? Is the data allowed to leave our network?

SECTION 05 — SPEED OF DECISION

Reversible vs Irreversible Decisions

Architects do not move at one speed. Two-way doors you walk through. One-way doors you study. Most of the delay in weak architecture programmes is spent arguing about class structure, while the public API and the tenancy model are waved through because the meeting ran out of time.

ONE-WAY DOORS — SLOW DOWN

  • Database technology migration. Moving five years of orders from SQL Server to another engine is a programme, not a sprint. Choose for the data you have, with an exit only if you truly need one.
  • Public API contract. Partners will bind to your JSON. A careless field name lives for years. Version, do not casually reshape.
  • Major data model. Order, ledger, tenant key, identity of a customer. Getting the grain wrong means a migration with downtime risk.
  • Cloud migration and vendor dependency. A proprietary queue feature in every service is a door that locks. Managed services are good. Unconditional coupling is not.
  • Encryption strategy. Keys, what is encrypted, who can decrypt. Retrofitting encryption onto a careless log pipeline is painful and sometimes impossible for data already leaked into logs.
  • Tenancy model. Shared database, schema per tenant, database per tenant. Changing this after customers exist is one of the most expensive rewrites in SaaS.

TWO-WAY DOORS — MOVE

  • Internal class structure. Refactor. Do not hold a release for a perfect folder layout.
  • Internal service boundaries that are not yet public. You can merge two ASP.NET services next month if you have not promised them as products.
  • Logging format. Standardise, but a field rename with a dual-write week is reversible.
  • Non-public implementation. Dapper on one hot path, EF Core elsewhere. Cache TTL. Retry counts. Feature flags.
  • An internal RAG prompt and chunk size. Change them when the eval set says so. Do not treat a prompt as a constitution. The choice to send data to a vendor is the one-way part, not the prompt text.

Realistic speed. A team spent six weeks debating repository pattern versus minimal APIs, then shipped a public webhook without idempotency and a tenant id only in the query string. The first decision was reversible and over-governed. The second was a one-way door and under-governed. Flip that energy.

CONCEPT

Reversibility decides the pace. If you can undo it in a sprint with no customer harm, decide and learn. If undoing it means a migration, a broken partner, or a compliance incident, slow down and write the decision.

WHY IT MATTERS

Architects who treat every choice as sacred become bottlenecks. Architects who treat every choice as reversible ship traps. The senior move is knowing which is which.

EXAMPLE

Choosing SQL Server for the order ledger is closer to a one-way door for this company — they have the skill, the backups, and the auditors. Choosing whether the email worker is a hosted service or a separate deployable is a two-way door until other teams bind to it. Choosing "database per tenant for every customer, including the 200 small ones" is a one-way operational cost. Do not make that casually because one enterprise prospect asked.

ARCHITECT THINKING

Who else will depend on this? Will data accumulate? Will a contract be published? Can I feature-flag the behaviour? If the answer is "partners will compile against this", it is not internal.

TRADE-OFFS

Moving fast on two-way doors creates some rework. That rework is cheaper than committee delay. Moving fast on one-way doors creates migrations that cost more than the original project.

COMMON MISTAKE

Calling a decision reversible because the code can be rewritten, while ignoring the data, the contract, and the habit already formed in other teams.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"Where would you slow down in this design?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I would slow down on the public contract, the tenancy model, and anything that becomes the system of record. I would move quickly on internal module shape, logging, and which private worker sends email. I want an ADR on the one-way doors and a feature flag on the rest."

FOLLOW-UP

"Is adopting a cloud LLM a one-way door?" — The prompt is not. The data path is. If prompts and documents leave your region, or if product behaviour now depends on a vendor you cannot replace, you have walked through a one-way door. Design the port, the retention setting, and the exit before you depend on it.

SECTION 06 — WHAT YOU GIVE UP

Trade-Off Thinking

Every architecture decision pushes on several dimensions at once. If you cannot name what got worse, you have not made a decision. You have announced a preference. The question to tattoo on the whiteboard: what problem does this solve, and what new problem does it create?

CostRun cost and people cost.
SpeedLatency and delivery date.
ScaleThe next 10×, not infinity.
TrustCorrectness, security, uptime.
LoadComplexity the team must carry.

A fuller list you should be able to scan: cost, performance, scalability, reliability, availability, security, complexity, delivery speed, maintainability, team capability, vendor dependency, operational effort. You will not optimise all of them. Say which ones you are deliberately leaving imperfect.

Direct, hidden, second-order

Direct: a cache reduces database reads. Hidden: invalidation, memory, a new failure domain, stale reads. Second-order: the team stops indexing because the cache hides the pain, until the cache dies and the database falls over. Long-term: a private Redis cluster becomes a platform you must patch, capacity-plan, and explain in every incident. Operational: someone must be paged when hit rate collapses.

THE CACHING EXAMPLE, TAKEN SERIOUSLY

"Adding caching improves performance" is a half-sentence. For a product catalogue in SQL Server, hit by an ASP.NET API at 2,000 RPS, a Redis cache with a 60-second TTL may be the right trade: lower CPU, higher stale-name risk, a stampede to design for, and a fallback to SQL if Redis is down. For checkout stock, the same sentence is dangerous. A stale "12 available" oversells the warehouse. The new problem is worse than the old one.

20 API gateway vs direct exposure Gateway: auth, limits, a front door. Direct: one less hop Gateway: a bottleneck and a team. Direct: every service reinvents auth How many external consumers, and who owns the edge?
CONCEPT

A trade-off is a deliberate worsening of one dimension to improve another, with the new problem named. If you cannot name the new problem, you do not understand the choice.

WHY IT MATTERS

Interviewers punish "Redis for performance" because it skips the new problem. They reward "Redis for catalogue reads, not for stock, and here is the failure plan."

EXAMPLE

Adding three retries around a SQL call looks like reliability. Under lock waits it triples the load, extends the request, and exhausts the ASP.NET thread pool. The new problem is a retry storm. Cap retries, back off, and fail the non-critical path instead.

ARCHITECT THINKING

Direct effect, hidden effect, second-order effect, who operates it, what happens when it fails, what I am willing to leave slow or stale.

TRADE-OFFS

Explicit trade-offs make some stakeholders unhappy in the room. Hidden trade-offs make customers unhappy in production. Take the room.

COMMON MISTAKE

Listing only gains. Or listing fake trade-offs ("the only downside is it is too good"). Serious people name a real loss.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"What would you sacrifice?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I would sacrifice independent deployability and theoretical infinite scale. I would not sacrifice idempotent payment handling or the ability to tell a customer the truth about stock. I am willing to accept a slower admin report and a single-region design until the downtime budget says otherwise."

FOLLOW-UP

"What new problem does your cache create?" — Stale data, invalidation, memory cost, stampede, and a hard dependency unless you have a fallback. Then say which of those you have designed for.

SECTION 07 — THE WHOLE SYSTEM

Local Optimization vs System Optimization

A local win can be a system loss. The architect's job is to see the next box the pain moves into. Optimising the component you own, without asking where the load goes next, is how good engineers create bad systems.

API made "faster" with more instances
        ↓
more concurrent checkouts
        ↓
SQL Server lock waits on Orders
        ↓
timeouts, retries, even more load
        ↓
the "optimised" API is now the outage

Database query made faster
        ↓
application fires 40 parallel calls per page
        ↓
connection pool and network become the bottleneck

One service scales independently
        ↓
it writes stock while another writes payment
        ↓
you no longer have a transaction, you have a saga
        ↓
compensation, duplicates, and a harder incident
ApplicationASP.NET Core can scale out only if it is stateless. More instances are not a fix for a chatty data access pattern or a blocking call to an LLM on the request thread.
DatabaseThe database is often the real constraint. Protect it. A fast API that multiplies calls is an attack on SQL Server, even if each call looks innocent.
NetworkEvery microservice hop adds latency, failure, and a timeout you must choose. Local method calls hid that cost. Do not split a boundary to feel modern.
InfrastructureAutoscaling the app tier while the database SKU is fixed just moves the queue of pain. Scale the constrained resource, or remove work from it.
OperationsA design that needs three senior people to deploy is not scalable if you have one platform engineer. Operability is a system property.
SecurityA shortcut "inside the cluster" becomes a breach path. Local developer convenience — shared admin connection strings — is a system risk.
UsersA page that is fast because it shows stale stock is not a win if the user then cannot complete checkout. Optimise the journey, not the endpoint in isolation.
Business processSpeeding order capture while the warehouse cannot pick faster only builds a bigger backlog and angrier customers. Ask where the real queue is.
CONCEPT

System optimisation asks where the constraint moved, who feels it, and whether the local improvement increased total harm. The weakest necessary part still sets the throughput.

WHY IT MATTERS

This is the difference between a tech lead who tunes a query and an architect who notices the query is being called 40 times because of a page design, a missing cache, or a service split that should not exist.

EXAMPLE

An API team celebrates p95 of 40ms after adding instances. SQL CPU hits 95% during the campaign. The system is worse. The architect stops the scale-out, finds the N+1 in the order history call, moves reporting off the primary, and only then adds instances — with a connection limit so the app cannot drown the database.

ARCHITECT THINKING

If I make this part twice as fast or twice as parallel, what saturates next? Is that next part harder to fix? Am I improving a metric the customer does not feel?

TRADE-OFFS

System thinking can slow a team that wanted a local hero win. You trade a sprint's applause for a campaign that stays up. Explain the moved bottleneck in business language: "more servers will make the database the outage."

COMMON MISTAKE

Adding capacity at the tier you control because you cannot get a database change approved. That is politics, and it still causes the incident. Escalate the constraint instead of hiding it.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"The team wants more application servers. The database is already expensive. What do you do?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I would not add servers first. I would look at which queries burn CPU, whether the growth is reads or writes, and whether the app is multiplying calls. If three report queries dominate, more ASP.NET instances make the database worse. I would fix or move those reads, then scale the app tier with a limit so it cannot overwhelm SQL Server."

FOLLOW-UP

"Traces show lock waits on Orders during checkout." — Stop thinking about read replicas. Look at transaction length, missing indexes, serializable isolation you do not need, and whether a chatty EF save holds locks across an HTTP call to the payment provider. Never hold a SQL transaction open across a network call.

SECTION 08 — DECIDE ANYWAY

Architecture Under Constraints

Constraints are not interruptions to architecture. They are the job. For each scene below: what moves, what the options are, the trade, the decision I would actually make, what I would stop optimising, and what I would watch. Context unless noted: an ASP.NET Core order platform on SQL Server, Redis for catalogue, RabbitMQ for email and outbox, one region, a team that can operate what it already runs.

Scenario A — Budget cut by 40%

Affected decisions. Multi-region, Kubernetes, a large Redis tier, a second LLM provider, premium SQL SKU, a separate search cluster, 24/7 follow-the-sun staffing you were quietly assuming.

Options. (1) Cut scope and keep the money path boring and safe. (2) Keep the diagram and cut redundancy — the dangerous option. (3) Freeze the programme.

Trade-offs. You lose active-active, spare environment luxury, and some speed on admin search. You keep backups, auth, idempotent payments, and a tested restore. Cutting redundancy to "save the features" trades a slide for an outage.

DECISION

Ship the checkout and order record on the existing SQL Server and App Service. Drop AKS, drop the second region, shrink Redis to catalogue only, defer the LLM feature. Do not cut backups, secret management, or payment idempotency. If the cut is so deep that you cannot test restores, say the programme should pause rather than pretend.

DELIBERATELY NOT OPTIMISING

Global latency, theoretical 10× headroom, developer preference for a service mesh, a second search technology.

MONITOR

Cost per order, SQL CPU and storage growth, backup success, failed payment rate, cache hit rate. A budget cut without a cost dashboard will be repeated blindly.

Scenario B — Deadline cut from 6 months to 3

Affected. Scope, number of deployables, migration completeness, nice-to-have channels, the temptation to skip tests on the money path.

Options. Cut scope. Add people (usually too late to help). Cut quality (usually how you miss the date and the quarter after).

Trade-offs. Partners do not get marketplace orders in v1. You do not get perfect reporting. You keep a correct, idempotent checkout.

DECISION

Modular monolith. Web checkout, payment, stock decrement, order status. Email via the outbox, even if the template is plain. Defer recommendations, advanced search, multi-region, and the rewrite of the old admin tool. Write the three failure tests: double submit, payment timeout, stock race.

NOT OPTIMISING

Elegance of module boundaries, a perfect design system, event sourcing, a second payment provider unless the first is a known single point of failure for launch week.

MONITOR

Scope burn, defect escape on checkout, cycle time, unpaid risks. A date cut that is "absorbed" with no scope cut is a lie. Make the cut visible.

Scenario C — Team cut from 10 developers to 5

Affected. Number of services, languages, on-call load, build-versus-buy, platform ambitions.

Options. Fewer moving parts. Buy identity, buy observability, stop the platform rewrite. Or keep the estate and accept that it will rot.

Trade-offs. You lose independent deploy of small teams you no longer have. You gain a system five people can reboot.

DECISION

Collapse planned microservices back into a modular monolith. Standardise on C#, SQL Server, one bus, one dashboard. Buy identity (Entra) and a managed log platform. One on-call rotation, not four. Freeze new technology for two quarters.

NOT OPTIMISING

Polyglot freedom, per-team database choice, a custom internal framework, "future" service extraction that has no owner.

MONITOR

Pages per week, time to restore, bus factor on SQL and the payment integration. If only one person can ship, that is the architecture risk.

Scenario D — Traffic expected to rise 10×

Affected. App tier, SQL, cache, queue consumers, connection pools, locks, external provider rate limits.

Options. Measure and remove waste. Scale the constrained tier. Shard or split only if measurement says the single primary cannot carry writes.

Trade-offs. A read replica and a catalogue cache buy time and add lag and staleness. Sharding buys write scale and years of pain. Do not buy the pain early.

DECISION

Profile first. If the 10× is mostly catalogue reads, cache them and add stateless API instances with a cap. If checkout writes dominate, shorten transactions, fix indexes, queue the non-critical side effects, and load-test the payment provider's limit. Revisit sharding only if write throughput still fails after that. 10× of a small number may still be a single SQL Server.

NOT OPTIMISING

Infinite scale, active-active, a new database engine "because 10×". Also do not optimise the admin screens.

MONITOR

p95 by endpoint, SQL CPU and lock waits, Redis hit rate and evictions, RabbitMQ depth, payment provider 429s, connection pool exhaustion.

Scenario E — Availability from 99.5% to 99.99%

99.5% is about 3.6 hours of downtime a month. 99.99% is about 4 minutes. That is not a configuration tweak. It is a different system, a different deploy policy, and a different bill.

Affected. Deploys, single-AZ resources, maintenance windows, dependency timeouts, the batch job that currently runs on the request path, staffing.

Options. Multi-AZ, health probes, automated rollback, remove single points, graceful degradation. Or tell the business the number is not buyable at this price, especially if a payment provider or LLM sits on the critical path.

Trade-offs. Cost rises sharply. Feature velocity falls because every change needs a rollback and a compatibility story. You still cannot promise 99.99% for a dependency you do not control.

DECISION

Accept the target only for the journeys you control, and write a dependency exception. Multi-AZ SQL and app tier, no big-bang deploy, timeouts and circuit breakers so a dead recommendation service cannot kill checkout, tested failover. Refuse to put the LLM on the checkout path. If the business wants 99.99% including payments, that is a commercial problem with the provider, not a Kubernetes problem.

NOT OPTIMISING

Feature throughput, the cheapest SKU, a design that needs a human to failover. Also do not pretend active-active is required if multi-AZ meets the 4-minute budget.

MONITOR

Error budget burn, failed deploys, time to detect, time to rollback, dependency error rates. Page on budget burn, not only on "site down".

Scenario F — Customer requires on-prem

Affected. Managed identities, Azure Service Bus, SaaS observability, the LLM endpoint, how you patch, who runs backups.

Options. A installable package on their Windows or Kubernetes, SQL Server they already run, RabbitMQ or even SQL-backed outbox if they will not run a broker. Or refuse the deal if you are a SaaS company with no install path and the revenue does not pay for a second product.

Trade-offs. You lose managed HA and easy scale. You gain data control for them and a support burden for you. One codebase with heavy ifdefs rots. A thin port with the same modular monolith is more honest.

DECISION

If the deal is strategic, package the modular monolith, use SQL Server as the source of truth, prefer an outbox over a broker they cannot operate, integrate with their AD, and do not require AKS. Charge for the operational responsibility you are about to inherit. If they also want your SaaS LLM features, those stay in your cloud with a clear data-flow agreement — or they do not ship on-prem.

NOT OPTIMISING

Elastic cloud scale, your favourite managed search, a design that assumes you can SSH in at will. Autonomy of their operators matters more than your diagram.

MONITOR

Install success, version skew in the field, backup proof they send you, support hours per customer. On-prem without telemetry is a black box you will be blamed for.

Scenario G — Data cannot leave the country

Affected. Region choice, SaaS logging, support access, vector database, LLM vendor, CDN, backups, analytics exports.

Options. Pin compute, database, backups, and retrieval index in-country. Use a model endpoint that contractually stays in-region. Or drop AI features that cannot meet the rule.

Trade-offs. Fewer vendor choices, higher cost, slower model updates. You do not get to "just use" a US-only vector SaaS because the demo was good.

DECISION

Region-pin SQL Server, blobs, Redis, and the search index. Private endpoint for the LLM, no training on prompts, prompt and completion logging kept in-country or disabled. Support access via customer-approved break-glass, not a global admin in another country. If the best model has no in-country deployment, ship keyword search rather than quietly violate the rule.

NOT OPTIMISING

The newest model, a global anycast shortcut that replicates data "just for latency", a convenient SaaS error tracker that stores payloads abroad.

MONITOR

Data-flow inventory, unexpected egress, vendor region attestations, backup location. Residency fails in a side tool more often than in the main database.

Scenario H — A critical vendor becomes unavailable

Affected. Payment, identity, SMS, LLM, a niche tax API. The architecture question is what the user can still do, and how you avoid double side effects when the vendor returns.

Options. Degrade, queue and retry, failover to a second provider, or stop the journey. Building a full replacement overnight is not an option.

Trade-offs. A second provider costs money and a second integration you must keep correct. A queue delays truth. Stopping the journey loses sales and keeps correctness.

DECISION

For payments: do not invent success. Show a clear pending or failed state, keep an idempotency key, retry with backoff, and fail over only if the second acquirer is already live and reconciliation is tested. For an LLM: fall back to cached answers and keyword search, and disable tools that write. For identity: this is closer to a full stop — do not bypass auth because the provider is down. Have a break-glass admin path that is audited and short-lived, designed before the outage.

NOT OPTIMISING

Feature richness during the outage, a heroic same-day replacement, perfect latency. Survive and stay correct.

MONITOR

Vendor error rate and latency, queue age, duplicate charge alerts, fallback hit rate, time since last successful reconciliation.

CONCEPT

Under a hard constraint, architecture is the art of choosing what degrades. You protect the thing whose failure is unacceptable, and you visibly abandon the rest.

WHY IT MATTERS

Candidates who say "we would still do microservices, just smaller" have not heard the constraint. The constraint is supposed to delete options.

EXAMPLE

Budget minus 40% deletes AKS and the second region. It does not delete the backup test. If your answer cuts the backup to save a feature, you have optimised the slide.

ARCHITECT THINKING

What does this constraint make impossible? What does it make foolish? What must remain true even if the programme shrinks?

TRADE-OFFS

Saying no to scope protects the date or the budget and disappoints a stakeholder this week. Saying yes to everything fails in production, which disappoints everyone later.

COMMON MISTAKE

Cutting the invisible safety (restore tests, idempotency, limits) because it has no demo, and keeping the visible feature that created the risk.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"The budget is cut in half. What do you change?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I cut scope and spare luxury before I cut correctness. One region, the platform we already know, no second cluster, no speculative AI. I keep backups, auth, and idempotent money movement. I would rather ship a smaller honest system than a cheap system that cannot be restored."

FOLLOW-UP

"They refuse to cut scope." Then the constraint is political, not technical. Show the risk in business terms — failed restore, double charge, missed date — and ask which risk they are explicitly accepting. Write it down. If they will not accept it either, the date or the budget must move. You do not silently accept it for them.

SECTION 09 — WHAT FIRST

Priority and Decision Ordering

The most technically interesting problem is often not the highest priority. Priority is a judgement about harm, dependency, and reversibility — not about how hard the puzzle is, and not about which ticket the loudest person brought.

HarmBusiness, customer, security, regulatory impact if we wait.
UrgencyIs the harm happening now, or only in a story?
DependencyWhat else is stuck until this is true?
ReversalOne-way doors move up the queue even if they are not exciting.
CostOf doing it, and of the complexity it adds.

Also weigh operational impact. A change that saves developers ten minutes and adds a pager for operations is not a free win. A change that unblocks five teams beats a clever optimisation inside one service.

SITUATION WHY THIS WINS HERE WHEN THE CONTEXT FLIPS
Security beats performance An unauthenticated internal admin API is a breach, not a latency win. Fix auth before you tune the query. If you are in a live incident and a security control is itself causing total outage, you may temporarily bypass with an audited break-glass, then restore the control. That is a timed exception, not a new design.
Reliability beats feature delivery A checkout that double-charges cannot ship the new coupon engine on top. Stop the line. If the feature is the contractual go-live and the reliability gap is a rare admin report, you may ship the feature and schedule the report fix. Say so.
Data correctness beats latency A stock figure 20ms faster and wrong loses more than a stock figure 80ms slower and true. A recommendation rail can be stale and fast. Do not apply the stock rule to the rail, or the rail rule to stock.
Operational simplicity beats theoretical scale Five people cannot run a cell-based global mesh for 200 RPS. The simple primary wins. When the simple primary has actually fallen over, and you have the people, theoretical scale has become today's constraint.
Customer impact beats developer convenience A shared database convention that prevents a class of tenant leak beats each team's favourite library. If the standard blocks a revenue-critical experiment and the risk is contained, allow a time-boxed exception with an owner.
NONE OF THESE ARE UNIVERSAL LAWS

They are defaults. Context flips them. Say the default, then say what would flip it. That is the architect answer. "Security always wins" is how you become the person the business routes around.

CONCEPT

Ordering is about harm over time, not difficulty. Do the thing that unlocks or protects the system of value, especially if it is hard to reverse. Defer the impressive work that nothing depends on.

WHY IT MATTERS

Teams love the hard technical problem. Customers feel the broken ordinary path. Architects who cannot say no to interesting work drown the programme.

EXAMPLE

Backlog: shard SQL, add OpenTelemetry, fix a tenant filter bug, build an AI summary. Order: tenant filter (security, customer harm, happening now), telemetry (you cannot see the rest), AI summary if it has a user, sharding last because you have not proved you need it.

ARCHITECT THINKING

If we only finish one thing this month, which unfinished thing still hurts customers? What is stuck behind this? Is this a one-way door we are casually delaying?

TRADE-OFFS

Priority makes someone wait. Hidden priority — pretending everything is P1 — makes everyone wait and hides the real sequence.

COMMON MISTAKE

Prioritising by architectural elegance or by who shouted. Also: treating a regulatory date as optional because the work is dull.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"What should we solve first?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I would not start with the hardest design. I would start with the failure that harms customers or breaks a promise we already made, especially if it is a security or correctness hole. Then the dependency that unblocks everyone else. The clever scale work waits until a number says we need it."

FOLLOW-UP

"Product says the AI feature is the board commitment." Then sequence a safe slice — retrieval with citations, no write tools — after the tenant bug if that bug leaks data. Explain that a board demo on a leaking platform is not a win. Offer a date for the demo that does not pretend the bug is cosmetic.

SECTION 10 — RISK IS A DESIGN CHOICE

Risk-Based Architecture

You cannot eliminate risk. You can see it, reduce it, move it, accept it, or watch it. Risk acceptance is also an architecture decision. Pretending you accepted nothing is how surprise becomes an incident review.

  1. IdentifyName the risk in plain language. "Redis might be down at peak" is better than "cache reliability".
  2. Estimate impactCustomer harm, money, data, time. Rough is fine. Hide nothing behind a medium/medium grid if the impact is "we charge twice".
  3. Estimate likelihoodHave we seen it? Does the design make it likely? A single node with no failover is not a rare risk. It is a planned outage.
  4. ReduceChange the design so the blast is smaller or the chance is lower. Timeouts, idempotency, a fallback, a second AZ, a limit.
  5. TransferInsurance, a vendor SLA, a contractual acceptance by the customer. Transfer does not work for reputation. An SLA cheque does not un-charge a card.
  6. AcceptSay it, name the owner, write the condition. "We accept single-region for v1. Owner: CTO. Revisit if the contract demands 99.99%."
  7. MonitorA risk you accepted and do not watch is a risk you forgot. Dashboard, alert, review date.
RISK EXAMPLE IN THIS STACK A GROWN-UP RESPONSE
Security Admin endpoint trusted because it is "internal". Reduce. Authenticate. Do not accept this to save a week.
Scalability SQL primary may not survive a 10× campaign. Measure, index, cache safe reads. Accept residual write risk with a load test date. Do not shard on a rumour.
Availability One region. A zone failure takes checkout down. Accept for 99.5% if the business agrees. Reduce with multi-AZ if the number is 99.99%.
Data loss Backups exist, restores never tested. Reduce. A restore test is the control. Do not accept untested backups on a system of record.
Vendor One payment provider, one LLM. Reduce on payments if revenue stops. Accept a single LLM if you can degrade to search. Write the degrade path.
Delivery Six services, three-month date, five people. Reduce by cutting services. Accept a smaller launch. Do not accept a date you already know is fiction.
Technical debt Modular boundaries are leaky, but the sale is Friday. Accept short-term if you contain it — no new cross-module table reads — and set a paydown. Dangerous if the debt is "no idempotency".
People One person understands the payment integration. Reduce. Pair, document the failure modes, remove hero culture. This risk pages at night.
Operational No trace across API → SQL → RabbitMQ. Reduce before you add more services. You cannot accept blindness and also add hop count.
Compliance Prompts stored in a US log tool; data must stay in-country. Reduce or stop the feature. Do not accept a quiet violation because the tool is convenient.
CONCEPT

Architecture is risk selection. Every design keeps some risks and spends money to shrink others. The mature act is to accept a risk in writing, with an owner and a trigger.

WHY IT MATTERS

Teams that "accept nothing" either never ship or accept risks silently. Silent acceptance has no owner. The incident review then sounds like surprise. It was not surprise. It was an unsigned decision.

EXAMPLE

You accept single-region for a domestic B2B portal at 99.5%, owned by the CTO, reviewed if a customer contract says otherwise. You refuse to accept untested backups. Same meeting, two different risk decisions. That is judgement.

ARCHITECT THINKING

Impact times a honest likelihood. Can I reduce it cheaply? If not, who is allowed to accept it? What will I watch so acceptance is not amnesia?

TRADE-OFFS

Reducing risk costs delivery speed and money. Accepting risk costs a future incident if you were wrong about likelihood. Transferring risk to a vendor does not transfer the customer relationship.

COMMON MISTAKE

A red-amber-green grid with everything amber, so nothing is decided. Or accepting security and data-loss risk because they are "unlikely" without a control.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"Which risk would you accept?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I would accept single-region and a stale catalogue cache, in writing, because the harm is bounded and the business has chosen the budget. I would not accept untested restores, missing idempotency on payments, or an LLM that can issue refunds without a human. Acceptance needs an owner. Some risks do not get an owner because we will not take them."

FOLLOW-UP

"The CEO accepts the refund-agent risk." You still do not design a silent auto-refund. You can accept a narrow, limited, audited, reversible action with a cap. You translate their acceptance into a control, not into the absence of one. If they want zero control, you escalate the consequence in writing and you do not pretend it is safe.

SECTION 11 — WRITE THE JUDGEMENT

Architecture Decision Records

An ADR is not bureaucracy. It is how a decision survives the meeting. Six months later nobody remembers why Redis was rejected for stock. The person who was in the room has left. The next team "fixes" the decision and recreates the outage. Documenting rejected options is the valuable part. The choice alone is a headline. The rejection is the reasoning.

ADR SHAPE — USE THIS

Title · Context · Problem · Constraints · Options considered · Decision · Reasoning · Trade-offs · Risks · Consequences · Rejected alternatives · Review date.

One to two pages. Plain language. A link to the metric that would reopen it. If you cannot fill "rejected alternatives", you did not decide. You selected your habit.

ADR 1 — SQL Server, not a document store, for orders

Context. New checkout for a retailer. Team knows SQL Server and EF Core. Orders relate to payments, stock lines, tax, and customers. Auditors ask for history.

Problem. Where is the system of record for an order?

Constraints. Money correctness, existing skill, reporting already in SQL, three-month date.

Options. SQL Server. A document database per order blob. Event store as the source of truth.

Decision. SQL Server. Snapshot price and tax on the order row. Outbox table in the same database for side effects.

Reasoning. An order is a contract with constraints and relations, not a shapeless document. Transactions and unique keys are the product.

Trade-offs. Schema changes need discipline. Flexible marketplace payloads will live in a JSON column, not drive the model.

Risks. Write hot spots at campaign peak. Review if lock waits remain after index and transaction work.

Rejected. Document store — weak constraints, reporting pain, a second skill. Event sourcing — powerful audit, far too much machinery for this team and date.

Review. After the first peak, or if a partner requires a different store as system of record (unlikely; we would still project into SQL).

ADR 2 — Modular monolith, not microservices, for v1

Context. Eight developers, one product, one on-call rotation. Orders, stock, and payment must agree.

Decision. One deployable ASP.NET Core app, modules with internal contracts, no cross-module table reads. Database remains shared but schema ownership is per module.

Reasoning. Independent deploy is not yet a real need. A network split would invent partial failure on a transaction we need.

Trade-offs. One release train. A defect in admin code can ship with checkout unless test boundaries are real.

Rejected. Microservices — operational cost, distributed stock/payment, no team topology to match. "Distributed monolith" of services sharing tables — the worst of both.

Review. If a module needs a different scale profile or a different team release train, extract that one service, with its own data.

ADR 3 — Redis for catalogue, not for checkout stock

Decision. Cache product name, image, and non-authoritative price display for 60 seconds. Stock decrement is a conditional UPDATE in SQL. The product page may show an approximate availability label fed by a short TTL, but the checkout command ignores it.

Reasoning. Catalogue reads dominate CPU and can be slightly stale. Stock is a decision. A cache must not authorise a sale.

Trade-offs. Checkout pays a database round trip. Redis down means catalogue latency rises; checkout still works.

Rejected. Cache-aside stock counts — oversell. No cache at all — primary melts on campaign reads. In-process only cache — inconsistent across instances, acceptable only as a short fallback if Redis is down.

Review. If checkout SQL CPU is the problem, revisit transaction shape before revisiting a stock cache.

ADR 4 — RabbitMQ for notifications and outbox, not for payment authorisation

Decision. Payment authorisation is a synchronous call with a timeout, idempotency key, and a recorded unknown state if we time out. Email, fraud review, and analytics consume from RabbitMQ via an outbox written in the same SQL transaction as the order.

Reasoning. The user must know whether they can be charged before we confirm the order. Email can be late. A queue in front of payment hides a failure the user must see, and complicates double-charge prevention.

Trade-offs. Checkout latency includes the provider. Provider slowness is visible. We gain a clear outcome.

Rejected. Fully async checkout — better spike absorption, worse UX and reconciliation. Direct HTTP for email — couples checkout to the mail vendor. SQL polling instead of RabbitMQ — acceptable fallback if the team will not operate a broker; not our default because we already run RabbitMQ.

Review. If payment latency dominates abandonment, add a well-defined pending state rather than pretending the queue is an authorisation.

ADR 5 — Cloud LLM with RAG, not a local model, for the internal assistant v1

Context. Employees ask policy questions. Documents live in SharePoint. Data may not be used to train a public model. No GPU estate. Team has Azure experience, not ML-platform experience.

Decision. RAG over Azure OpenAI in-region, private networking, no training on our data, citations required, retrieval filtered by the user's permissions. No fine-tune in v1. No agent tools that write.

Reasoning. The gap is private facts, not the model's manners. A local model means GPUs, patching, and a worse answer unless we have a security rule that forbids the cloud endpoint entirely.

Trade-offs. Vendor dependency, token cost, a region constraint on model choice. We gain speed and a better baseline quality.

Risks. Hallucination, ACL bugs in retrieval, cost spike, vendor outage. Mitigate with eval set, citations, spend caps, keyword fallback.

Rejected. Fine-tuning — does not keep facts fresh, costs more, still needs retrieval for policy updates. Local GPU model — skill and capacity we do not have, unless residency rules leave no cloud option. Ungrounded chat — confident and wrong.

Review. 60 days, on citation accuracy, cost per question, and any data-handling finding.

CONCEPT

An ADR freezes the context, the choice, the rejection, and the trigger to reopen. It is a memory for the organisation, not a trophy.

WHY IT MATTERS

Without rejected options, the next architect "improves" the system back into the failure you already paid to avoid. Interviews also ask "what did you reject?" The ADR is the practice rep for that question.

EXAMPLE

ADR 3 stops a well-meaning performance sprint from caching stock counts. The rejection paragraph is the control. The decision sentence alone would not have been enough.

ARCHITECT THINKING

Will a stranger understand the constraint in a year? Did I record the option we almost took? Is there a date or a metric that reopens this?

TRADE-OFFS

ADRs take an hour and save a quarter. Too many ADRs on two-way doors teach people to ignore them. Write them for one-way doors and for any decision that was contentious.

COMMON MISTAKE

An ADR that says "we chose Kafka because it is scalable" with no rejected alternative and no review date. That is a press release.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"How do you record a decision so it is not relitigated every month?"

STRONG ANSWER

"A short ADR: context, options, decision, what we rejected and why, the risk we accepted, and the metric or date that reopens it. I care more about the rejected paragraph than the title. I do not write one for a private refactor."

FOLLOW-UP

"When do you reopen?" When an assumption in the context is false — scale, team size, regulation, vendor terms — not when a new engineer prefers a different tool.

SECTION 12 — THE BORING GOOD CHOICE

When to Choose a Monolith

A monolith is not a failure of ambition. A modular monolith is often the highest-judgement choice for a product that is still finding its shape. Choose it when the constraints say the distributed version would add pain you do not yet have a reason to buy.

Team sizeOne team, or a handful of teams that release together. Eight people do not need eight deployables. Conway's law will create services that match a wish, not a department.
Product maturityThe boundaries are still moving. Splitting a boundary you will redraw next month means two migrations. Keep the cut in code until it stabilises.
Domain complexityHigh domain complexity is a reason for modules and a clear model, not automatically for network hops. A complex ledger wants a transaction more than it wants a service mesh.
Deployment frequencyIf the whole product ships weekly and that is fine, independent deploy is not paying rent. If one tiny change waits on a scary release, look at modularity and tests before microservices.
OrganisationNo separate ownership, no separate on-call. A service without an owner is a distributed orphan.
Operational maturityIf tracing, dashboards, and restore drills are weak, do not multiply processes. Get observability on one process first.
ScaleMost business systems are not Netflix. A single ASP.NET Core app behind a load balancer, plus SQL Server, carries a shocking amount of honest traffic.
TransactionsIf two facts must commit together — payment record and order, debit and credit — a monolith with one database is the simple correct design. A saga is what you do when you had no choice.

Scenes where microservices are a poor decision

  • The new returns module for a team that already has orders and stock in one SQL Server. A service split creates a saga for a workflow that used to be a transaction.
  • An internal tool for 200 users with a six-week life. Anything beyond a small app and a database is vanity.
  • "We might scale" with no measurement and no separate bottleneck. You are designing a future company.
  • A mandated platform rewrite that must ship the same features in six months with the same people. The rewrite into services will miss the date and lose the transactions.
  • A reporting concern dressed up as a service that still reads the OLTP tables. That is not a service. That is a distributed join with extra steps.
CONCEPT

Choose a monolith when one team, one release, one transaction, and one operational surface match the real problem. Make it modular so extraction remains possible.

WHY IT MATTERS

Recommending a monolith in an architect interview is a seniority signal if you can say why, and a junior signal if you say "microservices are overrated" without constraints.

EXAMPLE

A bank's new fee calculator is complex but must agree with the ledger in one commit. Keep it in the ledger process. Publish an event after commit for statements. Do not make the calculator a service that can succeed when the ledger fails.

ARCHITECT THINKING

Am I avoiding services out of fear, or because the costs are real here? Did I still draw module boundaries so this is not a ball of mud?

TRADE-OFFS

You give up independent scale and independent failure domains. You can still scale the process out if it is stateless. You accept a shared release and a shared database risk, contained by module discipline.

COMMON MISTAKE

A "monolith" that is actually an unstructured solution with no boundaries, defended as simplicity. That is not the choice above. That is debt.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"When would you not use microservices?"

STRONG ANSWER

"When one team owns one product, the boundaries are still moving, and two or more facts must commit together. I would build a modular monolith, keep an outbox for side effects, and extract a service only when scale, ownership, or failure isolation earns the split. I would not split because a diagram looks more senior."

FOLLOW-UP

"How do you stop the monolith becoming mud?" Module boundaries enforced in code — no reaching into another module's tables, internal interfaces, a test that fails on illegal references. Architecture tests are cheaper than a service mesh.

SECTION 13 — WHEN THE SPLIT EARNS ITS KEEP

When to Choose Microservices

Microservices become justified when a boundary is real in the business and the cost of not splitting exceeds the cost of the network. Not before. The hidden bill is larger than the conference talk admits.

Bounded contextsOrders and search ranking change for different reasons and do not need one transaction. That is a candidate boundary. Order and order-line are not.
Independent deploymentA team is blocked weekly by another team's release fear. Independent deploy is valuable when it is actually the constraint.
Independent scalingImage processing or a spike of notifications drowns the API. Split the workload whose scale profile is different, not every noun.
Team ownershipTwo teams, two on-call rotations, a contract between them. A service per developer is not ownership. It is fragmentation.
Failure isolationA recommendation bug must not take checkout down. A separate process with a timeout is justified. Isolation is the point, not the fashion.
Organisational boundariesA partner-facing API with a different lifecycle from the core. Sometimes a separate deployable is how you protect the core from partner-driven change.

The hidden bill — say it before you split

  • Distributed tracing or you will not debug a checkout that crosses four services.
  • Network failures that did not exist when this was a method call. Timeouts, retries, duplicates.
  • Service discovery and deployment complexity. Pipelines, config, secrets, version skew.
  • Data consistency. You gave up the transaction. You now own sagas, outbox, and reconciliation.
  • Debugging and observability. Logs in four places, correlation ids, a bus you must read.
  • Infrastructure. More than a copy of IIS or App Service. Even "simple" services need health, limits, and a dashboard.
JUSTIFIED SPLIT                         NOT A SPLIT
recommendation  | timeout | own team     OrderService + StockService
notification    | own scale | queue       sharing dbo.Orders
image render    | CPU heavy | isolate      "microservices" with
partner API     | other lifecycle          distributed transactions
CONCEPT

A microservice is a separately deployed boundary with its own data and an explicit contract, justified by ownership, scale, or failure isolation. Shared tables mean you do not have one.

WHY IT MATTERS

Saying yes to microservices without the bill is how architect interviews fail at the follow-up. The follow-up is always "what does that cost you?"

EXAMPLE

Checkout stays in the monolith. A recommendation service is separate because it is CPU-heavy, allowed to fail, and owned by another team. It is called with a short timeout. Checkout does not retry it three times. Empty recommendations are a valid degrade.

ARCHITECT THINKING

What is the boundary? Who owns it? What data does it own? What happens when it is down? How do we trace a request? Why is a module not enough?

TRADE-OFFS

You gain isolation and independent change. You lose simple transactions and simple debugging. If you cannot name a gain that is worth that loss, do not split.

COMMON MISTAKE

Splitting by entity — Customer service, Order service, Product service — then needing all three for every screen, with a distributed transaction hiding in the middle.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"When do microservices become worth it?"

STRONG ANSWER

"When a real boundary needs its own release train, its own scale, or must fail without taking the money path with it — and we can pay for tracing, contracts, and partial failure. I would extract that one service and leave the rest. I would not start by decomposing the noun list."

FOLLOW-UP

"How do the services share data?" They don't, not as a shared writable schema. They exchange contracts and events. If you need a join for a screen, build a read model. If you need a transaction, you probably should not have split.

SECTION 14 — RESTRAINT

Knowing When NOT to Architect

Simple requirement, small team, low scale, low risk — that does not justify a highly distributed architecture. The senior move is sometimes to refuse the design exercise as stated and design the thing in front of you, with a path if the facts change.

THE INTERVIEW TRAP

"Design this for 10 million users. The product has 500." You do not blindly draw sharding, multi-region, and Kafka. You say: "I will design for the real 500 and the next order of magnitude we can imagine with evidence, and I will show what I would change at 10 million so you can see I know the path. I will not make those 500 users pay for an imaginary estate."

Overengineering, named

  • Event sourcing for a CRUD internal form because "audit". An audit table would do. Event sourcing is a product decision, not a logging strategy.
  • A service mesh for three apps on one App Service plan.
  • CQRS everywhere when there is one writer and the read is the same shape as the write.
  • A custom orchestration platform for a nightly batch that a SQL job and an alert would finish.
  • Multi-region active-active for a company whose users and staff are in one city, sold as "enterprise grade".
  • An AI agent with write tools for a problem that is a search box.

Underengineering exists too. "We will just use a spreadsheet" for a regulated money movement is not restraint. It is avoidance. Restraint is the smallest design that survives the real risks, not the smallest design that fits on a slide.

CONCEPT

Not every request deserves an architecture programme. Some deserve a module, a bought service, or a no. Designing for a scale you do not have is a way of avoiding the harder question: what is the real problem this quarter?

WHY IT MATTERS

Architects who always add structure become expensive. The interview wants to see you protect the company from your own cleverness.

EXAMPLE

500 users, internal knowledge search. Ship a modular app, SQL full-text or a small index, and a careful RAG slice if the documents justify it. Do not design a global API platform. Mention, in one sentence, what you would split if usage became external and 100×.

ARCHITECT THINKING

What is the smallest design that meets the real constraint? What would I add at 10×, and what evidence would trigger it? Am I being asked to perform expertise instead of solve a problem?

TRADE-OFFS

A small design may need a rewrite of one boundary later. That is usually cheaper than operating a large design now. Say that cost so nobody thinks you are naive.

COMMON MISTAKE

Drawing the 10-million-user diagram because you are afraid the interviewer will think you cannot. They are often testing whether you can resist. If they insist, draw the path, not the day-one estate.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"Design for 10 million users. We have 500."

STRONG ANSWER

"I will not build the 10-million-user system for 500 people. I will design a stateless ASP.NET app and SQL Server, with clear module boundaries, and I will name the first bottleneck I expect — usually the database or a provider limit. At 10× I add caching and read replicas. At a true consumer scale I revisit sharding and regional deployment. Building that now would spend the team's only year on problems they do not have."

FOLLOW-UP

"Humour me. Draw 10 million." Then draw it as a staged path with triggers, and mark what you would not build in the first release. Still refuse to put twelve services in production next month.

SECTION 15 — WHO SHOULD OWN THIS

Build vs Buy vs Reuse

Building feels like control. Buying feels like speed. Both can be traps. The question is whether this capability is your product, whether you can operate it, and whether a vendor failure is survivable.

BUILD BUY / MANAGED OPEN SOURCE / REUSE
Cost High people cost, low licence Licence or usage bill, lower build time Low licence, hidden operate cost
Control Highest Limited by the vendor's model High if you can fork; forks become your product
Speed Slowest to first value Fastest if the fit is real Fast if the fit is close and the skill exists
Custom fit Exact You adapt to them Good until the edge case
Operations Yours Shared; you still own the outcome Yours, including patches
Lock-in Low vendor, high internal Real. Price the exit. Low if you stay on the public contract
Security Only as good as your team Their patch speed, your configuration Your patch speed
Skill Must already have it or hire it Need people who can integrate and govern Need people who can run it at 2 a.m.

Ten build vs buy scenarios

# NEED JUDGEMENT
1 Employee login Buy. Entra or the existing IdP. Building auth is how you create a breach and a password-reset team. Your product is not identity.
2 Order pricing rules that are the company's edge Build inside the monolith, with clear tests. A generic rules engine will fight your domain and still need your experts.
3 Email and SMS delivery Buy a provider. Build the outbox, the idempotency, and the preference rules. Do not build an MTA.
4 Search for a catalogue Reuse SQL full-text until relevance is the product problem. Buy or run a search engine when ranking and typo tolerance are the complaint, not before.
5 Workflow for a 6-step approval Build a small state machine in SQL. Buy a workflow suite only if you have dozens of long-running processes and analysts who will model them.
6 Observability Buy the platform, standardise OpenTelemetry in code. Building a metrics stack is a second product.
7 Feature flags Reuse a simple table or a small library until you need targeting, audit, and a UI for product managers. Then buy. Do not build a launch platform.
8 LLM answers over private docs Buy the model API if residency allows. Build the retrieval, ACL filter, eval set, and citations. Do not train a foundation model.
9 Payment acquiring Buy the provider. Build idempotency, ledger entries, and reconciliation. Never build card storage if you can stay out of PCI scope.
10 An internal design system or service framework Reuse the boring platform you have. Build a framework only after three teams have repeated the same pain. A framework with one consumer is a hobby.

Existing internal platform beats a new buy when it is good enough and someone still owns it. It loses when teams route around it because it is down, slow, or unstaffed. A platform nobody uses is not reuse. It is a museum.

CONCEPT

Build what differentiates and what you must control for correctness. Buy what is a commodity and painful to operate. Reuse what you already run well. Own the outcome either way.

WHY IT MATTERS

Architects who build everything create a private cloud of unfinished tools. Architects who buy everything cannot explain a vendor outage or a lock-in bill. Judgement is the line between them.

EXAMPLE

Do not build identity. Do not buy a black-box "order management suite" if order flow is the business and the suite cannot express your stock rules. Integrate the suite's commodity pieces, or do not buy it.

ARCHITECT THINKING

Is this our product? Can we operate the alternative at 2 a.m.? What does exit cost? What data does the vendor see? What happens on renewal when the price doubles?

TRADE-OFFS

Buy is fast and couples you. Build is slow and couples you to your own maintenance. Open source feels free until the patch and the skill are due.

COMMON MISTAKE

Building a worse version of a commodity to avoid a licence, or buying a platform and then spending two years forcing the domain to fit it.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"Would you build this or buy it?"

STRONG ANSWER

"If it is identity, email transport, or a model API, I buy and I wrap it with our rules. If it is the pricing or ledger behaviour customers pay us for, I build it where we can test it. I always price the exit and the 2 a.m. skill, not just the first invoice."

FOLLOW-UP

"The vendor just doubled the price." That was a foreseeable risk. Options: accept and cut elsewhere, degrade to a smaller SKU, or execute the exit you designed. If there is no exit, you are negotiating, not architecting. Say that honestly.

SECTION 16 — GUARDRAILS, NOT A CAGE

Standardization vs Flexibility

Standardise where consistency creates value. Allow flexibility where local optimisation is justified. Every team picking its own database, bus, language, and dashboard feels like autonomy. It is how a 40-person company ends up with four logging systems and an incident nobody can see end to end.

STANDARDISE

  • Primary OLTP engine, unless a workload is genuinely different.
  • Messaging backbone — one RabbitMQ or one Service Bus, not both plus Kafka "for later".
  • Logging, metrics, traces — one place an on-call engineer looks.
  • Identity and secret storage.
  • API error shape, correlation ids, idempotency header for public writes.
  • How tenants are identified in tokens and queries.

ALLOW FLEXIBILITY

  • Internal module structure and, within reason, EF versus Dapper on a measured hot path.
  • A specialised store for a specialised workload — search, blobs, a vector index — with a named owner.
  • UI experiments that do not leak into the system of record.
  • A different language for a true island (a small ML job) if the operational story is honest.
  • Cache TTL and retry policy per dependency. Those are local facts.

Excessive standardisation is also harm. A golden path that cannot express the one workload you actually have will be bypassed in secret. Standards need an exception path with an owner and an expiry. A standard with no exception process becomes either a bottleneck or a joke.

CONCEPT

Standards are how the organisation buys shared operability. Flexibility is how a local problem gets a local fix. The architect decides which inconsistencies are too expensive to allow.

WHY IT MATTERS

At architect level you are no longer only designing your service. You are deciding what twelve teams must not reinvent. That is a political and technical judgement.

EXAMPLE

One team wants MongoDB for a single flexible form. The standard is SQL Server, which can hold a JSON column. The form is not a new scale kingdom. Refuse the new engine. If they later have a search relevance problem, approve a search cluster as a standard exception with an owner — not a private database hobby.

ARCHITECT THINKING

Does this variation create a 2 a.m. skill gap? Does the standard block a real constraint? Is the exception time-boxed?

TRADE-OFFS

Standards slow local novelty and speed incidents. Too many standards slow delivery until teams cheat. Write few standards, enforce those, and review them.

COMMON MISTAKE

Standardising taste (folder layout, every library) and failing to standardise identity, tenancy, and observability. You governed the bikeshed and missed the blast radius.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"Should every team choose its own stack?"

STRONG ANSWER

"No. I would standardise the database family, the bus, identity, secrets, and the way we trace a request. I would let teams vary inside the service where the cost is local. A new datastore needs a reason the standard cannot meet, an owner, and an exit. Autonomy that the on-call cannot operate is not autonomy. It is a future incident."

FOLLOW-UP

"A team says the standard bus cannot do their ordering needs." Listen. If they need strict ordering per aggregate, RabbitMQ can often do that with a consistent routing key and a single active consumer — or they may need a different pattern, not a different platform. Approve a second bus only when the first cannot meet a proven constraint and someone will run both.

SECTION 17 — DEBT WITH A NAME

Technical Debt Judgement

Technical debt is not automatically bad. Sometimes it is the interest you pay to learn or to hit a date that matters. Sometimes it is a hole in the hull. The architect's job is to tell those apart and to decide: pay now, defer, contain, or replace.

Intentional debtYou knew the shortcut, you named it, you set a review. A hard-coded tax rule for one country to make a pilot, with a date to replace it.
Accidental debtNobody chose it. A shared helper grew tentacles. Fix the understanding, not just the code. Accidental debt hides in "temporary" integrations that became the architecture.
Strategic debtYou accept a messy boundary to learn whether the product should exist. Valid if the blast radius is small and the paydown is real if the product sticks.
Dangerous debtNo idempotency, no tenant filter, no backups, secrets in source, an LLM tool that can pay money. This is not debt. It is an untreated risk. Pay it now.
LOW BLAST / EASY TO CONTAIN HIGH BLAST OR HARD TO REVERSE
Low interest (rarely hurts) Defer. A clumsy admin screen. Revisit if you touch it. Contain and schedule. A one-way data model that is ugly but stable. Do not rewrite for taste.
High interest (slows every change or pages you) Pay soon. A module everyone is afraid to edit, if a few weeks of seams would stop the bleeding. Pay now or replace the path. Payment duplicates, tenant leaks, untested restores, a bus nobody can drain.

Pay now when the debt can lose money, leak data, or blocks the only roadmap that matters. Defer when interest is low and the area is quiet. Contain when a rewrite is fantasy: put a seam around it, stop new coupling, add a test that prevents the dangerous use. Replace when containing costs more than a new path, and you can run both until the old path is drained. A big-bang rewrite is rarely the answer. A strangler with a metric is.

CONCEPT

Debt is a trade with interest. Name the type, the blast, and the interest. Then pay, defer, contain, or replace. Unnamed debt is just mess.

WHY IT MATTERS

Architects who demand zero debt never ship. Architects who wave all debt through ship incidents. The distinction between a hard-coded pilot and a missing tenant filter is the job.

EXAMPLE

The team wants a quarter to rewrite the monolith. The pain is three modules with circular calls and a frightening checkout class. Judgement: do not rewrite. Draw seams around checkout, add characterisation tests, stop new dependencies inward, and extract only if a real scale or team boundary appears. That is containment, not denial.

ARCHITECT THINKING

What is the interest per month? What is the blast if it fails? Is this taste or risk? Can I contain it this sprint?

TRADE-OFFS

Paying debt delays features. Deferring debt taxes every future feature. Containment is the middle path and requires discipline people will call "not a real fix".

COMMON MISTAKE

Calling a rewrite the paydown without a costed plan. Or calling dangerous missing controls "debt" so they can sit in a backlog behind a banner redesign.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"How do you decide whether to pay technical debt now?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I ask what it costs us every month and what happens if it fails. Missing idempotency or a tenant filter gets paid now. An ugly screen in a quiet admin tool waits. If a rewrite is the only idea on the table, I look for a seam and a containment plan first. I want the interest named, not a vibe that the code is old."

FOLLOW-UP

"The team morale depends on a rewrite." Morale matters, and a doomed rewrite destroys it later. Offer a funded improvement slice they can feel — delete a hated path, add tests, improve the deploy — and be honest that a full rewrite needs a business case, not a mood.

SECTION 18 — STAGES, NOT FANTASY

Architecture Evolution

Do not design today's solution using tomorrow's imaginary problems. Design the stage you have evidence for, and leave a cheap path to the next stage. Evolution is a series of decisions with triggers, not a big diagram you suffer toward.

Stage 1   Simple app + SQL Server
          trigger: one process cannot carry the read load, or deploy fear
              ↓
Stage 2   Modular monolith + Redis for safe reads + outbox
          trigger: a real independent scale or team boundary
              ↓
Stage 3   Extract the one service that earned it
          trigger: side effects and spikes need decoupling you do not have
              ↓
Stage 4   Events for those flows, not for every table update
          trigger: measured multi-region or hard isolation need
              ↓
Stage 5   Distributed, on purpose, with the operational muscle
          not "stage 5 because the slide had five boxes"

Each arrow needs evidence: a metric, a team fact, an incident, a contract. "We might" is not a trigger. Skipping to stage 5 because a peer company is there ignores that they may have paid for a platform team you do not have.

CONCEPT

Architecture evolves when evidence says the current stage is the constraint. You design the seam, not the future estate.

WHY IT MATTERS

This is how you answer "what happens at 10×?" without building 10× today. You show the path and the trigger. That is judgement. Building stage 5 in month one is anxiety.

EXAMPLE

Today: one ASP.NET app, SQL Server, no Redis. Campaign reads hurt. Stage 2 is Redis for catalogue plus a read replica for reports. You do not introduce Kafka because "events are the destination". The outbox table is the seam. If a consumer appears later, a broker can sit on that seam.

ARCHITECT THINKING

What is the next constraint, not the final form? What seam makes the next stage cheaper? What would I regret hard-coding?

TRADE-OFFS

Stage-thinking leaves some rework. It avoids years of operating empty complexity. The rework should be a seam opening, not a rewrite.

COMMON MISTAKE

Drawing all five stages and then implementing stage 5 "so we don't redo it". You will redo it anyway, because you guessed the boundary wrong.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"How would this architecture evolve?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I would ship the simplest stage that meets today's constraints, with one seam — an outbox, a module boundary, a stateless app. I would name the metric that promotes us to the next stage. I would not deploy the final distributed picture now. If I am wrong about growth, the seam is the cost I pay, and it is cheaper than running a platform we do not need."

FOLLOW-UP

"What if growth comes overnight?" Then the trigger fires. You should already know the first bottleneck and have a load-test habit. Overnight growth is still not a reason to have sharded last year. It is a reason to have measured and to have a rehearsed scale step.

SECTION 19 — THE ROOM

Handling Competing Stakeholders

Architecture becomes an opinion battle when people argue tools. It becomes a decision when you translate each need into impact, risk, and an explicit trade. You are not the judge of taste. You are the person who makes the trade visible so the owner of the risk can choose. 

Stakeholder need
    ↓
Business impact  — revenue, date, trust, penalty
    ↓
Technical impact — what must change in the system
    ↓
Risk             — what we would be accepting
    ↓
Options          — including "not this quarter"
    ↓
Decision         — one owner, written
    ↓
Trade-offs accepted — said in the room, not later

Business wants speed. Security wants controls. Operations wants fewer technologies. Developers want a modern stack. Finance wants lower cost. The customer wants higher availability. None of them is wrong inside their frame. Your job is to stop the frames talking past each other.

Ten stakeholder conflicts

# CONFLICT DECISION I WOULD DRIVE TRADE ACCEPTED
1 Business wants the feature Friday. Security wants a threat model. Ship only if the change does not cross a trust boundary. If it does, a short threat model is part of Friday, not a rival to Friday. Cut a different feature. A smaller Friday. Not an unauthenticated endpoint.
2 Ops wants one database. A team wants Cosmos for one flexible form. SQL Server with a JSON column. New engine needs an owner and a proven gap. Slightly less elegant document model. Much less operational sprawl.
3 Finance wants to delete Redis. Latency SLO is burning. Show cost per order versus the SQL SKU you would need without cache. Keep Redis for catalogue, shrink it, set a cap. Do not "save" money by melting the primary. A cache bill remains. A larger SQL bill does not.
4 Customer wants 99.99%. The price assumes 99.9%. Price the multi-AZ change and the dependency exceptions. Sales does not get to promise the number for free. Either a higher price or a lower number. Not a quiet hero architecture.
5 Developers want a rewrite in a new language. No, unless the language is the constraint (it rarely is). Fund seams and tests. A second language is a staffing decision. Some disappointment. One stack the on-call can read.
6 Sales promised on-prem and SaaS from one codebase next quarter. Pick the channel that is the real revenue. A dual product in one quarter is two products. Say so before the contract, not after. One delivery promise kept. One slide corrected.
7 Data science wants all production text in the lake. Privacy says no. A consented, minimised, access-controlled extract. Not a replica of production "for convenience". Slower experiments. A design you can explain to a regulator.
8 Support wants synchronous email confirmation inside checkout. No. Checkout commits, outbox sends mail. Support gets a visible "email pending" state and a resend. The mail vendor must not fail the payment. A rare delayed email. A checkout that survives the mail provider.

Not a shadow RabbitMQ plus Kafka.
10 CEO wants an agent that can issue refunds. A read-only assistant first. Refunds only as a drafted action a human confirms, with a cap, an audit row, and idempotency. Full autonomy is a later risk decision, not a demo feature. A less magical demo. A refund process you can defend.

A difficult conversation

SALES
The customer will sign if we promise 99.99% and on-prem. I need architecture to make it true.
YOU

SALES

YOU

CONCEPT

Stakeholder judgement turns competing wants into a visible trade with an owner. You facilitate the decision. You do not smuggle your taste in as a technical necessity.

WHY IT MATTERS

Architects who "win" arguments lose trust. Architects who only facilitate never protect the system. The balance is: clear recommendation, explicit trade, written acceptance.

EXAMPLE

Finance versus Redis. You do not say "cache is best practice". You say "removing it adds this SQL cost and this outage risk at campaign. Here is a smaller cache. Which bill do you want?"

ARCHITECT THINKING

What does each person fear? What number would change their mind? Who is allowed to accept the residual risk?

TRADE-OFFS

A clear recommendation disappoints someone. A vague compromise disappoints the customer later. Prefer the first, said respectfully.

COMMON MISTAKE

Hiding behind "it depends" until the loudest stakeholder chooses the tool. Or declaring "security says no" when you never translated the risk into a business sentence.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"Security wants controls. The business wants speed. What do you do?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I separate controls that prevent a real harm from controls that are habit. The first ones stay, and we cut scope to keep the date. The second ones can be risk-accepted with an owner. I put the trade in a sentence the business can sign. I do not let the date silently delete authentication."

FOLLOW-UP

"They insist on both full scope and full controls and the date." Then the constraint set is inconsistent. Show the inconsistency. Ask which one moves. If nobody will move, you escalate. You do not privately drop the control to be helpful.

SECTION 20 — A USEFUL NO

Saying No as an Architect

A naked "no" creates a workaround. A useful no has a reason, evidence, a risk, an alternative, and an impact. You are rejecting a design, not a person. You are also not required to pretend every request is wise.

NoThe clear rejection.
ReasonThe constraint it breaks.
EvidenceA number, an incident, a skill gap.
RiskWhat happens if we do it anyway.
AlternativeWhat you can say yes to.

Add impact: what the alternative costs in time or scope. A no without an alternative feels like a veto. A no with a reckless alternative is not help. Offer the smallest yes that does not break the constraint.

Dialogues

CTO
We should start with twelve microservices so we do not redo it.
YOU
No — not as the first release. We are eight people, and order plus stock need one transaction. Twelve services would add network failure before we have a scaling problem. Evidence: today's peak fits one database; we have no tracing culture yet. Risk: a partial checkout and a missed date. Alternative: a modular monolith with an outbox, and one extraction when a boundary earns a team. Impact: we ship the transaction correctly and delay independent deploy, which we do not need yet.
DEV
The internal admin API can skip auth. It is on the private network.
YOU
No. Private networks get crossed by laptops, vendors, and future services. The evidence is every breach write-up that started with "internal". Risk: an unauthenticated refund or export. Alternative: the same Entra app registration, a simple role, one day of work. Impact: a day on the date, not a permanent hole.
PERF
Cache the stock count. The product page is slow.
YOU
No for anything that authorises a sale. A stale 12 becomes an oversell. Cache the name and image. Keep the conditional update in SQL for checkout. If the page label must be fast, mark it approximate. Risk of caching the decision is customer and warehouse harm. Alternative is a read-only label plus a true checkout. Impact: checkout stays a database hit, which is the point.
INT.
Design it for 10 million users.
YOU
No, not as the system we operate next month. We have 500 users. Building shard and multi-region now spends the team on an imaginary load. I will show the evolution path and the trigger. Risk of the big design is a missed problem and an estate we cannot run. Alternative: stateless app, SQL, seams. Impact: a later project if the evidence appears, instead of a current outage caused by complexity.
CEO
Let the agent issue refunds. The demo is Thursday.
YOU
No to autonomous refunds by Thursday. A wrong tool call is a payment event, not a bad sentence. Alternative: the agent drafts the refund, a human confirms, we cap the amount, we store the prompt and the tool call. Impact: the demo still shows the agent, and we do not discover a duplicate refund in the review. We can talk about autonomy after the eval set and the audit exist.
CONCEPT

A professional no is a decision with a safer yes attached. It rejects unrealistic requirements, dangerous designs, unnecessary technology, insecure shortcuts, and unsupported scale stories.

WHY IT MATTERS

Architects who cannot say no become diagram clerks. Architects who only say no get bypassed. The formula keeps you useful and firm.

EXAMPLE

The refund-agent dialogue. The no is real. The alternative still respects the CEO's goal — a visible agent — without accepting an uncompensated money action.

ARCHITECT THINKING

Am I saying no to protect a constraint, or to protect my preference? If it is preference, I should be flexible. If it is a one-way harm, I should be stubborn and polite.

TRADE-OFFS

Every useful no spends political capital. Spend it on harm, not on style. A no on folder structure is how you lose the no on auth.

COMMON MISTAKE

"No, that's not best practice." That is not a reason. Or a yes that hides a no in a document nobody reads.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"Tell me about a design you rejected."

STRONG ANSWER

"I rejected caching stock counts. The reason was oversell, the evidence was a race we could already reproduce, the risk was warehouse and customer harm, and the alternative was caching catalogue display data only. We kept a slower checkout write and a faster product page. I would rather tell that story than a story about a tool I like."

FOLLOW-UP

"What if they do it anyway?" Then you write the risk acceptance, limit the blast if you can, and do not sabotage. You also do not pretend you agreed. A written dissent is part of the job. Silent compliance that hides your name from the risk is not loyalty. It is how the postmortem becomes fiction.

SECTION 21 — TEN PRODUCTION DECISIONS

Production Incident Judgement

An incident is a decision under worse information and more pressure than a design review. The order of operations is the judgement. Root cause is not step one. Stabilize, shrink the blast, gather evidence, tell the truth, mitigate, recover, then learn.

  1. StabilizeStop the bleeding. If a deploy correlates, roll back before you understand the bug. If a dependency is killing you, shed that call.
  2. Reduce blast radiusDisable the feature, drain one tenant, stop one consumer, close the gate. A smaller outage is a better outage.
  3. Gather evidenceError rate, latency, deploy marker, dependency health, queue depth, SQL CPU. One hypothesis at a time. Do not restart everything and destroy the evidence unless restart is the stabilization.
  4. CommunicateWhat customers cannot do, what you are doing, when you will update. No architecture essay. No false certainty.
  5. MitigateThe safest action that restores the journey, even if it is degraded. Scale only if the evidence says capacity.
  6. RecoverConfirm the customer path, not just a green dashboard. Watch for the retry storm you created.
  7. Investigate root causeAfter the bleed stops. Five whys, not five tools.
  8. Prevent recurrenceA control, not a slide. A test, a limit, an alert, a rollback habit.
PRESSURE FROM MANAGEMENT

"Have you restarted it?" is not a plan. You may restart a stateless API if it is a plausible mitigation and you have captured a dump or a trace window. You do not restart SQL Server because someone is anxious. Say what you know, what you are trying next, and what you will not do because it widens the blast.

1 · Redis is down

Catalogue pages slow down; checkout should not. Confirm the fallback to SQL is happening. If there is no fallback and the API throws on cache failure, that is the bug to mitigate: feature-flag the cache off, accept higher SQL load, protect checkout with a connection limit. Do not "fix" Redis by blocking requests until it returns. Watch SQL CPU. Recurrence: circuit-break Redis, serve catalogue degraded, never make cache mandatory for the money path.

2 · Database overload

SQL CPU or lock waits at the ceiling. Do not add API instances. Find the query or the blocking session. If a report is the culprit, kill it or move it. If a deploy added a scan, roll back. If checkout transactions hold locks across the payment HTTP call, that is the design bug — mitigate by stopping the worst caller, recover, then remove the network call from the transaction. Watch lock wait time, not just CPU.

3 · RabbitMQ backlog

Depth rising, consumers healthy or not? If consumers are crashing, fix or roll back before you add consumers — more consumers will poison faster. If they are slow, scale consumers only if the work is idempotent. Watch for duplicate emails. Protect the broker: a backlog is better than a broker out of disk. Do not purge a payment-related queue to "go green". Purge only a queue you can regenerate, and say so.

4 · API latency spike

Is it all endpoints or one? One endpoint points at a dependency or a query. All endpoints point at thread pool starvation, a bad node, or a shared gateway. Check a recent deploy first — it is the most reversible cause. Mitigate by rollback or by shedding the slow dependency. Do not spend the first fifteen minutes in a profiler on a hunch while a rollback sits unused.

5 · Memory leak

Instances cycling or one node climbing. Stabilize by recycling the worst node or rolling back the deploy that correlates. Capture one dump if you can do it without prolonging harm. Adding instances hides a leak and multiplies cost. Recurrence needs a load test and a cap, not a bigger SKU as the permanent fix.

6 · Thread pool starvation

ASP.NET requests queue while CPU is low. Classic cause: blocking on sync-over-async, or many threads stuck on a slow SQL or HTTP call. Mitigate by reducing concurrency, timing out the stuck dependency, rolling back. Do not raise thread pool limits as the first move — you will drown the dependency. The system fix is timeouts and not blocking the request thread on an LLM or a payment call with no cap.

7 · Authentication failure

If users cannot log in, this is a full stop for protected journeys. Check the IdP status, certificate expiry, a bad config deploy. Roll back config before you invent a bypass. A bypass is a security incident stacked on an outage. Break-glass only if it was designed, audited, and time-boxed. Communicate that login is down, not that "some APIs are degraded".

8 · Bad deployment

Error rate moved with the deploy marker. Roll back first if the rollback is compatible with the database. If a migration is irreversible, roll forward with a fix or disable the feature flag. This is why expand-contract migrations exist. Do not debug for forty minutes to avoid a rollback that would take four. Ego is not an incident strategy.

9 · Cloud service outage

Confirm it is the cloud, not your config, using a status page and a probe from outside your code. If a region is impaired and you are single-region, your job is honest communication and a degrade, not a fantasy failover you never tested. If you have multi-AZ, fail over only along the rehearsed path. An unrehearsed failover is a second incident.

10 · LLM provider outage

The assistant fails. Checkout must not. Disable the assistant entry point, serve keyword search or a "temporarily unavailable" message, stop agent tools so retries do not fire writes later. Watch the queue of half-finished tool calls. Do not let Polly retry a non-idempotent tool for five minutes. Recurrence: a spend and error circuit, a fallback, and no LLM on the money path.

CONCEPT

Incident judgement is sequence and blast control. The first decision is what to stabilize, not what the elegant root cause might be.

WHY IT MATTERS

Design interviews ask you to be clever. Production asks you to be orderly. Architects who redesign during an outage extend it.

EXAMPLE

Bad deploy plus a manager asking for a root cause on the bridge. You roll back, you say customers can pay again, then you talk about the bug. The order is the seniority.

ARCHITECT THINKING

What is the safest reversible mitigation? What evidence will I destroy if I restart? Who is harmed right now? What must I not do?

TRADE-OFFS

Rollback loses the feature and restores the service. Staying forward keeps the feature and may extend the harm. Prefer the customer unless rollback is unsafe because of data.

COMMON MISTAKE

Adding servers, restarting the database, purging queues, or disabling auth because those actions feel like action. They are often a wider blast.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"What would you do first in a production incident?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I would stabilize and shrink the blast before I hunt the clever cause. If a deploy lines up with the error rate, I roll back. I tell people what customers cannot do. I do not add app servers if the database is the thing on fire. After recovery I want one control that would have made this smaller."

FOLLOW-UP

"Rollback is impossible because the migration rewrote the table." Then you mitigate forward: feature flag off, a corrective script you have reviewed, or a read-only mode. This is why one-way data changes do not ship on a Friday without a reverse path. Say that as the prevention, not as a lecture during minute five.

SECTION 22 — WHEN THE MODEL MISBEHAVES

Decision-Making During AI Incidents

AI incidents are system incidents with extra ways to be wrong. The model can be up and still harm you — cost, hallucination, a bad tool call, a leaked document. Decide what to stop first. Curiosity about the prompt comes after the bleed.

SCENE DECIDE FIRST DO NOT DO FIRST
LLM cost doubles overnight Check spend cap, a loop, a retry storm, a model-default change, a prompt that exploded context. Shed traffic or drop to a smaller model for the cheap path. Protect the budget the way you protect a database from a scan. Do not "wait for the invoice" and do not raise the cap as the fix.
Hallucination rate jumps Compare the eval set. Did retrieval change, did the model version change, did a corpus update poison chunks? Disable uncited answers. Fall back to search for the worst intents. Do not tweak the prompt live in production as the only control.
Vector search quality drops Check embedding model version, index build, a bad ingest, ACL filter accidentally emptied or widened. Freeze ingest. Route to keyword search. Do not re-embed the world before you know which change shipped.
Embedding model changes Treat it as a one-way data event. Old vectors and new vectors are not the same space. Dual-index or rebuild before you cut over. If someone cut over already, roll queries back to the old index. Do not mix vectors from two models in one index and hope ranking saves you.
AI vendor unavailable Circuit-break, hide the assistant, keep keyword search, stop write-tools. Checkout stays up. Do not let retries pile up a queue of side effects.
Sensitive information returned Disable the assistant or the offending corpus now. Treat it as a data incident: what was shown, to whom, from which document, was it authorized? Preserve logs. Narrow retrieval. This outranks answer quality. Do not quietly patch the prompt and move on without knowing the blast.
Agent executed the wrong tool Disable write tools immediately. Identify the action, compensate if you can (void the refund, restore the record), tell the customer if money or data moved. Then look at allow-lists and confirmation. Do not keep the agent online "to reproduce" against production tools.
GPU capacity exhausted If you self-host: shed non-critical jobs, queue with a visible delay, fail fast rather than timeout-storm. If this is a vendor quota, same behaviour at your edge. Do not unbounded-retry. You will remain exhausted and look down.
CONCEPT

An AI feature is a dependency with a correctness problem, a cost problem, and sometimes a write problem. Incident order does not change: stop harm, shrink blast, then analyse the model.

WHY IT MATTERS

Teams treat model bugs as research. Customers experience them as product bugs. An architect puts a kill switch on the feature before the launch, not during the review.

EXAMPLE

A permission bug in retrieval shows another department's HR doc. First decision: turn the assistant off or drop that index. Second: identify who saw it. Third: fix the filter and add a test that a user cannot retrieve a document they cannot open in SharePoint. The prompt is irrelevant until the filter is true.

ARCHITECT THINKING

Can this feature write? Can it leak? Can it spend? Those three decide the urgency. A bad summary of a public FAQ is not the same incident as a wrong refund tool.

TRADE-OFFS

Disabling the assistant loses a feature and stops the harm. Leaving it on preserves the demo and continues the leak or the spend. Disable.

COMMON MISTAKE

Prompt-tuning during the incident, raising quotas, or assuming the model "knows" permissions. Permissions are your retrieval filter, not the model's mood.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"The assistant returned another tenant's document. What do you do first?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I turn the retrieval path off. I treat it as a data incident, not a model-quality issue. I find who saw what, I keep the logs, and I do not reopen the feature until a test proves a user cannot retrieve a document they are not allowed to open. I would rather have no assistant this afternoon than a second leak."

FOLLOW-UP

"Was it the model hallucinating the text?" Maybe. You still disable first. Then you check whether the chunk was actually in the context. If it was, retrieval and ACL failed. If it was not, the model invented it — still unacceptable for a tenant document, and still a reason to require citations and to refuse uncited answers.

SECTION 23 — DECIDE THE FAILURE

Architecture Under Failure

Assume something will fail. Decide what should happen next. A design that only describes the happy path is a brochure. For every important box, the architect should be able to answer a short failure questionnaire before the diagram is "done".

What happens if it fails?
Does the system stop?
Can we degrade?
Can we retry?     — only if idempotent, only with a cap
Can we cache?     — only if stale is allowed
Can we queue?     — only if later is allowed
Can we fall back? — to a simpler answer, not to a lie
Can we recover automatically?
Can users continue partially?

Failure decision matrix

Redis Catalogue cache miss storm No Read SQL, maybe shed anonymous traffic, keep checkout No tight retry. Circuit-break
RabbitMQ Side effects stall No for checkout if outbox is in SQL User sees pending email. Publish later Publisher confirms, replay outbox

Recommendation service Empty or slow No Hide the rail. Timeout short Do not retry on the user path

CONCEPT

Failure design is a set of explicit next steps: stop, degrade, retry, queue, fall back, or recover. "We have retries" is not a failure design.

WHY IT MATTERS

This is where system judgement shows up in an interview without a new tool. You are deciding the user experience of being wrong, late, or down.

EXAMPLE

Payment timeout: you do not know if the charge happened. You do not retry blindly. You record unknown, query the provider with the same idempotency key, and you do not show "order confirmed" until you know. That single decision prevents a class of double charges.

ARCHITECT THINKING

Is this dependency allowed to stop the journey? If yes, say so. If no, what degraded truth are we willing to show?

TRADE-OFFS

Degrading preserves availability and may show a weaker answer. Stopping preserves correctness and loses the journey. Pick per dependency, not once for the whole system.

COMMON MISTAKE

Retrying everything, queueing everything, or caching everything. Each of those is a specific trade, not a universal resilience spice.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"What happens when this dependency fails?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I decide whether the user journey may continue. Recommendations fail open — we hide them. Payments fail closed — we do not confirm an order we cannot reconcile. Redis fails open onto SQL for catalogue reads, with a limit so we do not kill the database. I want that sentence for every box, not a generic retry policy."

FOLLOW-UP

"How do you avoid a stampede when Redis returns?" One request rebuilds the key, others wait briefly or serve SQL. A lock or single-flight in the app, plus a jittered TTL so keys do not expire together. And a circuit so a dead Redis is not called by every request.

SECTION 24 — HOW MUCH MAY FAIL TOGETHER

Blast Radius Thinking

Blast radius is how much of the system, the customer base, or the data set is allowed to fail together. Architects decide that size on purpose. Accidental blast radius is just coupling you have not looked at.

Blast radiusOne tenant, one region, one journey, one shard, or everyone. Name it. "The site" is a lazy answer when only search is down.
Failure isolationA process boundary with a timeout. Recommendations in-process with checkout means a bad loop can starve checkout threads. Separate the things that may die.
BulkheadsSeparate connection pools so a slow report cannot take every SQL connection from checkout. In .NET, do not share one unlimited HttpClient usage pattern across critical and casual dependencies.
Circuit breakersStop calling a dependency that is hurting you. Fail fast, then try again later. A circuit is a blast-radius tool. It spends correctness or completeness to save the rest.
Rate limitingPer tenant, per API key, per route. Limits are how one client does not become everyone's outage. Return a clear 429. Do not let them retry in a tight loop — tell them when.
Queue isolationA poison notification must not share a single consumer prefetch with payment events if those payments matter more. Separate queues for separate criticality. Do not one-queue the world.
Tenant isolationShared database is a shared blast for noisy queries and for a missing WHERE TenantId. Isolation can be logical (limits, RLS) or physical (database per tenant) depending on the harm.
Cell-based architectureA slice of the system that serves a slice of customers, so one cell can die. Powerful and expensive. Justified when one global failure is unacceptable and you can operate the cells. Not a default for a single-region B2B app.
WITHOUT A BULKHEAD                    WITH A BULKHEAD
checkout pool  ──┐                    checkout pool ── SQL (protected)
reports pool   ──┼── one SQL pool     reports pool  ── SQL (capped)
admin export   ──┘                    admin export  ── replica or shed

one bad export takes checkout         export waits; checkout lives
CONCEPT

You decide the maximum set of users and functions that may fail together, then you place bulkheads, limits, and timeouts so reality matches that decision.

WHY IT MATTERS

A beautiful service split that shares a database pool, a broker, and a single tenant filter still fails together. Isolation is not a box on a slide. It is a resource limit.

EXAMPLE

One enterprise tenant runs a catalogue export every hour and saturates SQL. Logical isolation: rate-limit that job, move it to a replica, cap its pool. Physical isolation: their own database, only if their contract and your ops team justify the cost. Do not jump to cells because one export hurt.

ARCHITECT THINKING

If this fails, who else fails? Is that acceptable? What shared resource couples them — pool, cache, queue, schema, region, identity?

TRADE-OFFS

Smaller blast radius costs money and operational surface. Larger blast radius is simpler until the day it is not. Choose the size the business can stomach, not the size that looks tidy.

COMMON MISTAKE

Microservices as the only isolation idea, while a shared database and a shared thread pool couple everything. Or cells for a product that has one region and one on-call engineer.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"How do you limit blast radius?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I name what is allowed to fail together. Then I separate the resources: a timeout so recommendations cannot hold checkout, a connection bulkhead so reports cannot steal checkout's SQL connections, a per-tenant limit so one customer cannot saturate the API, and a queue per criticality so email poison does not block something worse. I would not start with a cell architecture unless a single global failure is truly unacceptable and staffed."

FOLLOW-UP

"One tenant is 40% of traffic and hurting others." Shed or throttle that tenant, move their heavy read to a replica, and talk to them. If this is permanent, price a silo. Do not silently degrade every other customer to be fair to an unbounded export.

SECTION 25 — THREE DIALS

Performance vs Reliability vs Cost

These three dials do not move independently. In an interview, say which dial you turned, which dial got worse, and why that was acceptable for this journey. A candidate who only turns the performance dial is still thinking like a tuner.

MOVE WHAT IMPROVES WHAT GETS WORSE HOW YOU SAY IT
More replicas Availability, read scale Cost, lag, operational surface "I buy failover and read scale. I accept lag and a bigger bill. I do not send the checkout write to a replica."
More caching Latency, database relief Staleness, invalidation, a new failure "I cache what may be stale. I accept a wrong-for-a-minute catalogue, not a wrong charge."
More retries Success on a blip Load on a sick dependency, tail latency, duplicates "I retry idempotent reads with backoff. I do not retry a payment without a key. I cap them so I do not DDoS myself."
More consumers Throughput, shorter backlog Duplicate processing, provider rate limits, harder ordering "I scale consumers only if the handler is idempotent. Ordering, if required, is per key, not global."
Larger model Harder questions answered Cost, latency, capacity "I use the large model where the eval set proves a gap. Cheap intents stay on a small model or on search."
Stronger consistency Correct stock and money Latency, availability under partition "Checkout pays the consistency cost. The catalogue does not."
Multi-AZ Survives a zone loss Cost, deploy complexity "I pay for multi-AZ when the error budget is minutes. I do not pretend it is free reliability."
CONCEPT

Performance, reliability, and cost are a triangle. State the turn of the dial and the price on the other two. That is the whole interview answer in miniature.

WHY IT MATTERS

Executives hear "make it faster and cheaper and always up". Your value is translating that into a choice they can fund.

EXAMPLE

More consumers on the email queue during a backlog: throughput up, duplicate-mail risk up, provider throttle risk up. You scale only after the handler keys off an idempotency id stored in SQL. Cost of consumers is cheap. Cost of a thousand duplicate "you have been charged" emails is not.

ARCHITECT THINKING

Which dial is the actual pain? Which dial am I quietly worsening? Would I accept that worsening on the money path?

TRADE-OFFS

Saying the worsening out loud makes the decision fundable and reversible. Hiding it makes the incident look like bad luck.

COMMON MISTAKE

"We'll add retries, replicas, cache, and consumers." That is all three dials turned at once with no owner for the downside.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"How do you balance performance, reliability, and cost?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I do not balance them in the abstract. For checkout I pay cost and some latency to keep correctness and a clear failure. For catalogue reads I pay staleness to save database cost and latency. For the assistant I cap spend even if a larger model would impress more. I name the dial I refused to turn."

FOLLOW-UP

"Cost must fall 30% and the SLO stays." Then something else gives: scope, a region, a model, a retention window, a support promise. Bring three concrete cuts with their SLO impact. Do not promise the triangle can ignore physics.

SECTION 26 — HOW TRUE, HOW FAST

Consistency Judgement

Different parts of the same system deserve different consistency. Applying one model everywhere is how you either melt the database or oversell the warehouse. Say which fact must be true now, and which fact can catch up.

FLOW MODEL WHY WHAT YOU REFUSE
Banking ledger Strong, in one database transaction. Idempotent posting key. Debit and credit must agree. Eventual money is a defect. A cache that authorises a spend. A cross-service hope.
E-commerce inventory Strong at the decrement. Conditional UPDATE, row version. Read model for the page can be slightly stale if labelled. The sale decision must not oversell. The badge on the page can lag a few seconds. Distributed transaction across payment and stock if you can reserve stock and confirm payment with compensation instead — but the reservation itself is strong.
Product catalogue Eventual. Redis, 30–60 seconds, or a replica. A stale subtitle is annoying. It is not a wrong ledger. Using the same cache policy as stock.
Notifications Eventual, at-least-once, idempotent consumer. Late email is acceptable. Lost or duplicated "charged" email needs a key so duplicates collapse. Sending mail inside the checkout transaction.
Analytics Eventual, minutes of lag fine. Decisions are not operational. Moving this off the primary is a reliability feature. Live dashboards on the OLTP primary because someone said "real time".
AI conversation history Read-after-write for the user's own next turn. Eventual for analytics and for other channels. The user must see what they just said. A recommendation model can lag. Treating the transcript as a system of record for money. Tool side effects need their own idempotent transaction, not "the model remembers".

Read-after-write matters when the user saves and immediately reads — a profile, a chat turn, an order confirmation page. Send that read to the primary, or use a session-consistent path. Do not "fix" a replica lag by caching the stale read.

Idempotency is the companion of retries and queues. A payment, a refund tool, an outbox consumer — each needs a key so doing it twice is safe. Without that key, consistency talk is theatre.

Distributed transactions (two-phase commit across services) are usually the wrong repair for a split you should not have made. Prefer one database for the facts that must commit together. If the split is real, use a saga you can explain: reserve, charge, confirm, compensate. Compensation is a business process, not a magic rollback.

CONCEPT

Consistency is per fact, not per platform. Strong where a wrong answer costs money or trust. Eventual where lag is a nuisance. Idempotent wherever a retry exists.

WHY IT MATTERS

This is a favourite architect cross-examination. One consistency slogan for the whole system is a fail, even if the slogan is sophisticated.

EXAMPLE

Checkout: reserve stock with a conditional update, call payment with an idempotency key, commit the order, publish outbox. The confirmation page reads the primary. The catalogue badge stays cached. Analytics lags five minutes. Four models, one system, no apology needed.

ARCHITECT THINKING

If this fact is wrong for ten seconds, who is harmed? If this message is delivered twice, what breaks? If the user writes and reads, which node must answer?

TRADE-OFFS

Strong consistency costs latency and availability under failure. Eventual consistency costs a reconciliation story. Pick the cost the fact deserves.

COMMON MISTAKE

"We're event-driven, so everything is eventual." Then the ledger drifts and nobody knows which event is true. Events after a commit are fine. Events instead of a commit, for money, are not.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"Where do you need strong consistency?"

STRONG ANSWER

"Where two facts must be true together and a wrong answer costs money or stock: the ledger posting, the stock decrement, the payment idempotency record. Catalogue, email, and analytics can lag. The user's own order confirmation should read the primary so they see what they just did. I would not put one consistency label on the whole platform."

FOLLOW-UP

"Payment succeeded, stock update failed." You do not have a distributed transaction, you have a compensation decision. Retry the stock decrement if the reservation can still be taken. If not, refund or void using the same idempotency discipline, and show the user a clear failure. Do not leave them charged and unfulfilled in silence. The prevention is to reserve stock before charging, inside a design you can compensate.

SECTION 27 — WHAT YOU WILL NOT TRADE

Security Judgement

Security is a design constraint that competes with usability, performance, cost, and speed. Some of that competition is healthy. Some risks should not be traded away for a date. Knowing the difference is the judgement. "Security is important" is not an answer.

UsabilityStep-up auth on money, not on every click.
PerformanceAuthz cache carefully. Do not skip the check.
CostManaged identity is cheaper than a breach.
SpeedCut scope, not the control, when the date slips.
Never casualAuthn, tenant isolation, secrets, backups of trust.
AuthenticationBuy the IdP. Do not build passwords. Decide where step-up is required — refunds, exports, admin — and where a session is enough. A bypass "for the mobile team" is a second product with no security.
Authorization granularityStart with roles that match real jobs. Add fine-grained permissions when a role is too wide for a real harm, not because a model diagram looks complete. Every check must include the tenant.
EncryptionTLS everywhere, encryption at rest on SQL and blobs, keys in a managed store. Field-level encryption only for the fields that require it — it breaks search and support. Do not log the plaintext you just encrypted.
API throttlingA security control and a reliability control. Per key and per tenant. Unlimited internal service accounts are how a bug becomes a breach-sized scrape.
Network isolationPrivate data stores, no public SQL. Useful, and not a substitute for authentication. "It's in the VNet" is not an auth scheme.
SecretsA vault or managed identity. Not appsettings in git, not a connection string in a wiki. Rotation must be possible without a hero.
Tenant isolationA missing predicate is a data breach. Enforce in a single place — EF filter plus RLS if the data is sensitive — and test it. Do not rely on every developer remembering WHERE TenantId.
AI data accessThe model does not have permissions. The retrieval layer does. Filter before the prompt. Do not send secrets in a prompt. Do not let a tool call become a confused deputy that uses a privileged key.
DO NOT TRADE THESE AWAY FOR A DATE

Authentication on anything that changes state or reads private data. Tenant isolation. Secrets out of source control. A tested restore for the system of record. An idempotency story for money. An AI tool that can move money or data without a bound, an audit, and a confirmation commensurate with the harm. You may trade a perfect threat-model document, a second factor on a low-risk read, or a fancy encryption scheme on non-sensitive catalogue text. Say which is which.

CONCEPT

Security judgement is choosing controls that match harm, refusing casual trades on the non-negotiables, and not gold-plating the rest into a programme that never ships.

WHY IT MATTERS

A candidate who says "we'll add OAuth" has not decided anything. A candidate who can say what they will not skip, and what they will defer, sounds like an architect.

EXAMPLE

The mobile team wants a long-lived token in local storage to avoid login friction. You refuse long-lived bearer tokens in an easy-to-steal place. You offer refresh tokens in a safer store and a slightly longer session. Usability moves. The non-negotiable does not.

ARCHITECT THINKING

What is the harm if this control is absent for a week? Who can reach this? Does the AI path bypass a check the UI enforces?

TRADE-OFFS

More security friction reduces conversion and speed. Less friction increases breach probability. Put the friction on the dangerous actions, not on every read of a public catalogue.

COMMON MISTAKE

Trusting the network, trusting the model, or trusting every service account. Also: blocking a release for a theoretical control while a known tenant bug sits unfixed. Fix the real hole first.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"How do you balance security and delivery speed?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I cut scope before I cut authentication, tenant filters, or secret handling. I will defer a perfect permission matrix if roles cover the harm and a review date exists. I will not defer a control because the network is private or because the model seems polite. Speed is a scope decision. Security of the trust boundary is not optional scope."

FOLLOW-UP

"The AI agent uses a service account that can read all tenants, and the model picks the tenant from the question." That is a confused deputy. The tool must run as the user, or receive an allow-list computed in your code from the token. The model never chooses its own privilege. If that cannot be done by the demo, the demo does not include cross-tenant tools.

SECTION 28 — REMOVE

Architecture Simplification

Senior architects add structure. Principal-level judgement also removes it. Complexity is not a trophy. The question that cleans a system: if I had to rebuild this today, what would I remove?

Unnecessary complexity hides in: a service that is only called by one other service and shares its database; a cache of a cache; an abstraction with one implementation; a "temporary" sync job that also sends events; an AI agent wrapping a workflow that is three if-statements. If removing it does not harm a real constraint, it is a candidate.

CONCEPT

Simplification is an architecture change with a risk profile. You remove moving parts that do not earn their operational cost, while protecting contracts and data.

WHY IT MATTERS

Systems get judged on incident rate and change rate, not on box count. An architect who can only add will drown the team that has to run the drawing.

EXAMPLE

Twelve services, four of which are "customer", "customer-preferences", "customer-address", "customer-query", all owned by one team, all reading one database. Merge them. You lose nothing but deploy theatre. You gain a transaction and a debugger.

ARCHITECT THINKING

What would I not rebuild? What contract is real? What failure domain is real? What is only a folder that escaped into a repository?

TRADE-OFFS

Merging reduces isolation you might have wanted later. If the isolation was fictional, you lose nothing. If it was real, do not merge those two. Simplification is not collapse-everything.

COMMON MISTAKE

A rewrite labelled simplification. Or deleting a bus that actually decouples a spike, because the diagram looks cleaner without it.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"How would you simplify this estate?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I would ask what I would not rebuild. Services that share a team, a database, and a transaction get merged. Extra caches and extra buses get frozen and drained. I would keep a split that has a real failure or scale reason. I would not announce a rewrite. I would delete one unjustified moving part at a time and watch the incident and change metrics."

FOLLOW-UP

"People's identities are tied to the services they own." Then the problem is organisational. Merge the on-call and the deployable, and give people a boundary that matters — a module they own inside a system that stays up. Do not preserve a production incident surface as a job title.

SECTION 29 — WAS IT ANY GOOD?

Measuring Decision Quality

A decision is not good because the diagram was praised or the deploy went green. A technically elegant design that fails the business objective is not a successful architecture. Measure the outcome you claimed, in the window you named.

Business outcomeDid failed checkouts fall? Did the assistant deflect calls? Did the date that mattered get met with the scope you promised?
ReliabilityError budget, incident count, time to recover. A design that needs a hero every Friday is unreliable even if the SLA slide is green.
Performancep95 of the journey you cared about, not a benchmark of an empty endpoint.
CostRun cost and people cost against the forecast. A surprise bill means the decision was incomplete.
Incident frequencyDid the new boundary add pages? If Redis was meant to reduce SQL incidents and instead added cache incidents, count both.
Operational effortHours on call, time to diagnose, number of people who can ship. This is the cost the licence sheet hides.
Delivery speedLead time after the change. A platform that made every feature slower needs a reckoning, even if it is "correct".
User impactComplaints, oversells, double charges, abandoned checkout, wrong AI answers shown to customers.
MaintainabilityCan a new engineer change the module without a guide from the author? If not, the decision did not land.
CONCEPT

Decision quality is the gap between the claimed outcome and the measured outcome, including the new problems you accepted. Review it on the date you wrote in the ADR.

WHY IT MATTERS

Without this, architecture becomes taste and politics. With it, you can change your mind without losing face — the evidence changed, as you said it might.

EXAMPLE

You introduced Redis to cut SQL CPU below 60% at peak and keep p95 under 300ms. Two months later CPU is 40%, p95 is 180ms, and you had one cache-stampede incident. The decision was mostly right. The follow-up is single-flight, not a victory lap that ignores the incident.

ARCHITECT THINKING

What did I claim? What did I measure? What new cost appeared? Would I make the same decision with today's facts?

TRADE-OFFS

Measuring takes instrumentation you should have wanted anyway. A decision with no measure cannot be defended or retired. That is the more expensive option.

COMMON MISTAKE

Judging success by "we used the modern tool" or by a launch party. Also judging failure too early, before the metric had a fair window.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"How do you know an architecture decision was good?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I compare it to the outcome I wrote down: business effect, reliability, cost, and whether the team can operate it. A green deploy is not enough. An elegant design that missed the revenue or the error budget is a failed decision, even if the code is clean. I want a review date so I can say that out loud and change course."

FOLLOW-UP

"The design is elegant and the business missed the quarter." Then the decision was not successful. Understand whether the architecture slowed delivery, or the scope was fantasy. If architecture slowed delivery, simplify. Do not defend the diagram against the outcome.

Executive-Level Technical Judgement

Executives do not need your vocabulary. They need the decision, the risk, the cost, and what you are asking them to accept. Translate. Do not dumb down the risk. Dumb down the jargon.

# TECHNICAL SENTENCE BUSINESS SENTENCE

Executive communication is the same decision, retold as risk, cost, customer impact, and the choice you need from them. Jargon is a failure of translation, not a sign of depth.

WHY IT MATTERS

A decision the business does not understand will be overturned in the corridor. If you cannot translate it, you do not yet own it.

EXAMPLE

Row 5. "Multi-AZ" does not help a CFO. "Four minutes versus three hours, here is the bill, here is what we still cannot promise" does.

ARCHITECT THINKING

What decision am I asking for? What happens to customers if we choose badly? What does it cost? What am I explicitly not promising?

TRADE-OFFS

Simple language can be attacked as incomplete. Carry the technical depth underneath, ready for the follow-up. Do not lead with it.

COMMON MISTAKE

Either drowning them in tools, or hiding a real risk to get a yes. Both destroy trust. The second one destroys it later and harder.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"Explain this to a non-technical executive."

STRONG ANSWER

"I would say what the customer feels, what it costs, what risk we are accepting, and what I need them to decide. For a cache: we protect the database that takes orders by allowing product text to be a minute out of date. I would not mention Redis until they asked how."

FOLLOW-UP

"They ask how." Then one layer down: a small fast store in front of product reads, with a plan for when it is down. Still no lecture on eviction policies unless the room is technical. Match the depth to the question.

SECTION 31 — TEN DECISIONS, NOT TEN DIAGRAMS

System-Level Architecture Case Studies

The point of each case is the decision, not the boxes. Read the constraints. Cover the recommendation. Decide. Then reveal. Five are written long enough to rehearse aloud. Five are tighter, same skeleton. All ten are complete decisions.

CASE 1 · DEEP · E-COMMERCE CHECKOUT

A campaign retailer that must not oversell

Business context. Mid-market retailer, .NET team of nine, SQL Server already the estate. 8,000 orders on a normal day, 80 orders/sec for twenty minutes during three campaigns a year. Payment via a single acquirer. Warehouse hates oversells more than it hates a slow badge on the product page.

Requirements. Place an order, take payment, decrement stock, send email, show status. p95 checkout under 800ms excluding the acquirer. No double charge. No confirmed order without a stock decision.

Constraints. Three months to the next campaign. One on-call rotation. No appetite for a new database. Acquirer timeout is common enough to design for.

Unknowns. Exact campaign multiple until marketing commits. Whether a second acquirer is commercially possible this year.

Possible architectures. Microservices for order, stock, payment. Modular monolith with outbox. Fully async checkout.

Decision criteria. Correct money and stock, operability by this team, ready before the campaign, a measured path if write locks appear.

DECIDE BEFORE YOU REVEAL What do you refuse? Where is the transaction? What is eventual?
RECOMMENDED REASONING

Modular monolith. Reserve stock with a conditional UPDATE in SQL Server before calling the acquirer. Payment call uses an idempotency key and records unknown on timeout — never a blind retry that can double-charge. Order commit and outbox insert share one transaction. RabbitMQ sends email. Redis caches catalogue display only. Confirmation page reads the primary. Reports go to a replica.

REJECTED

Microservices for v1 — a saga on the money path with no team topology. Caching stock as the sales decision. Fully queued payment — hides the outcome the user must see. A new document database for orders.

RISKS

Acquirer latency dominates the SLO. Single acquirer is an accepted availability risk, written down. Lock waits if reservations are held across a slow payment — so the reservation is short and the HTTP call is outside the SQL transaction. Campaign 10× beyond the brief reopens read replicas and lock strategy, not sharding, unless tests say otherwise.

EVOLUTION

If email volume or a new channel needs isolation, the outbox already exists. If a second team owns recommendations, that is the first service, with a timeout, off the money path.

CASE 2 · DEEP · BANKING LEDGER POSTING

Internal transfer that must balance

Business context. A retail bank's internal transfer between two accounts. Auditors read the ledger. Customers forgive a slow statement. They do not forgive a missing debit. Volume is steady, not campaign-shaped. The team runs SQL Server and a strict change process.

Requirements. Post a balanced pair, idempotent if the client retries, immutable history, statement reads that can lag by seconds, not by an accounting period.

Constraints. Strong consistency. No "eventual money". Existing core is painful to change. Regulatory retention. On-prem or a private cloud region with residency rules.

Unknowns. Whether mobile clients double-submit. Whether statement "real time" means seconds or "the next screen must match".

Criteria. Balance invariant, auditability, operable change, no new engine without a regulatory reason.

DECIDE BEFORE YOU REVEAL Would you introduce events as the source of truth?
RECOMMENDED REASONING

SQL Server transaction: insert two posting lines and a unique idempotency key, or insert nothing. The ledger is the source of truth. An outbox publishes a "posted" event after commit for statements, notifications, and fraud scoring. Statement UI reads a projection that is allowed to lag seconds, but the posting API reads the primary. No cache on the authorisation balance. Corrections are new postings, not updates.

REJECTED

Event sourcing as the only ledger — the team and the auditors would need a new notion of truth for no volume reason. Microservice per account — distributed balance. Redis balance — a stale authorisation. Two-phase commit with an external fraud service inside the posting transaction — holds locks across the network. Fraud is after-commit or a fast pre-check that cannot be the system of record.

RISKS

Hot accounts (a payroll account) create lock contention. Mitigate with a clear posting order and short transactions, not with dropping isolation. Replay bugs if consumers are not idempotent. Accepted risk: statement lag of a few seconds, told to the product owner in plain language.

EVOLUTION

If read volume of statements grows, scale the projection, not the ledger write path. A second region is a business-continuity programme with a rehearsed RPO, not a toggle.

CASE 3 · DEEP · MANUFACTURING ERP SLICE

Shop-floor orders, not a consumer spike

Business context. A plant wants a cleaner order-to-build slice on top of an ageing ERP. Users are planners and the shop floor, a few hundred people, mostly on-site. Downtime of an hour hurts production. A wrong bill of materials hurts more. The network is plant-local. The IT team is small and can run SQL Server and Windows services. They do not want a cloud dependency for the line to run.

Requirements. Create a works order, reserve components, record completions, sync to the ERP of record without losing a transaction if the sync is late.

Constraints. On-prem. Low request rate, high correctness. Limited skills. ERP vendor has a slow API and a nightly batch they do not fully trust.

Unknowns. Whether the vendor API can accept idempotent replays. How long the plant can run if the ERP link is down.

DECIDE BEFORE YOU REVEAL Is this a microservices case? Is the cloud a virtue here?
RECOMMENDED REASONING

A modular on-prem application, SQL Server as the local source of truth for the shift, a sync worker with an outbox to the ERP. The line must keep recording completions if the ERP is down, and reconcile when it returns. No microservices — the team cannot operate them, and the transactions are local. No Redis required at this scale; a careful query is enough. No LLM on the critical path. If they want a document helper for procedures, it is a separate, optional tool that fails open.

REJECTED

Replatforming the whole ERP. Cloud-only hosting that stops the line when the WAN drops. Event-driven decomposition of BOM, inventory, and labour into services. A custom workflow engine — the states are few and belong in tables.

RISKS

ERP sync duplicates if the vendor is not idempotent. Contain with our own key and a reconciliation report a planner can see. Accepted risk: reports that join ERP and local data can lag. Not accepted: losing a completion recorded on the floor.

EVOLUTION

If several plants need central visibility, ship a read-only export upward. Do not invert the design so the plant cannot work without the centre.

CASE 4 · DEEP · DOCUMENT PROCESSING

Invoices in, structured data out

Business context. Accounts payable receives PDFs and scans. Clerks retype them. Volume is 20,000 documents a day, spiky at month end. A wrong amount is a finance incident. A slow document is a queue, not an outage. The company is on Azure, .NET, SQL Server. They want "AI" because a vendor demo extracted fields magically.

Requirements. Ingest, virus-scan, store, extract supplier, date, total, lines, route low-confidence items to a human, post approved results to the ERP.

Constraints. Documents contain bank details. Residency applies. Humans remain accountable for posting. Budget does not include a GPU farm.

DECIDE BEFORE YOU REVEAL Where is the model allowed to be wrong? What is the system of record?
RECOMMENDED REASONING

Blob storage plus a SQL status row. A queue (RabbitMQ or Service Bus — pick the one they will operate) drives scan, then extraction. Extraction is a bought model API in-region, or a specialist OCR vendor if the accuracy on their invoices is better. Confidence below a threshold, or any unseen supplier, goes to a human. The human decision is the system of record for posting. The model never posts to the ERP. Idempotent workers keyed by document id. Version the extractor so a bad model can be rolled back without reprocessing blindly.

REJECTED

Synchronous extraction on upload — month-end spikes will time out the browser. Fine-tuning as the first step — you do not have a labelled set yet; the human queue creates one. An agent that posts payments. A microservice per field.

RISKS

Silent low-quality extraction if nobody watches the human-correction rate. That rate is the product metric. Vendor outage queues work; it must not lose files. Accepted: latency of minutes. Not accepted: a posted amount nobody confirmed when confidence was low.

EVOLUTION

When the correction set is large, consider a specialised model for the top suppliers. Still keep the human threshold. Do not "automate fully" because the correction rate looked good on an average.

CASE 5 · DEEP · INTERNAL KNOWLEDGE ASSISTANT

Policy answers for employees, with permissions

Business context. 4,000 employees ask HR and IT the same questions. The knowledge is in SharePoint, badly structured. A wrong answer about safety or dismissal policy is worse than no answer. Data must not train a public model. Some sites are in-country only. The team knows ASP.NET and Azure. They do not have a GPU cluster or an ML platform team.

Requirements. Answer with citations, respect permissions, fail visibly, give IT a way to see what was asked without storing secrets forever.

Constraints. No write tools in v1. A security review before go-live. Cost cap. Eval set of at least 50 real questions before launch.

DECIDE BEFORE YOU REVEAL RAG or fine-tune? Cloud or local? What do you turn off first if it leaks?
RECOMMENDED REASONING

RAG, not fine-tune. The gap is private, changing facts. Azure OpenAI in-region, private endpoint, no training on content. Retrieve only documents the user can open — enforce that in code, not in the prompt. Require citations; if retrieval is empty, say you do not know. Log question ids and document ids, not full secrets. Keyword search remains the fallback and the kill-switch path. No agent tools. Spend cap and a feature flag.

REJECTED

Fine-tuning — stale the week policy changes, no citation, cost, still needs retrieval. A local model — no GPU skill, worse quality, only justified if cloud residency is impossible. Ungrounded chat. An agent that files HR tickets in v1 — a write path before the read path is trusted.

RISKS

ACL bugs are data incidents. Hallucinated policy is a trust incident. Cost loops. Vendor outage. All three have a switch. Accepted risk: the assistant sometimes says it does not know. That is success, not failure.

EVOLUTION

If citations stay above the bar and users want actions, add a single idempotent tool with confirmation — for example draft a ticket, do not submit it. Revisit local models only if policy forbids the cloud endpoint.

CASE 6 · MULTI-TENANT SAAS

One product, many customers, one noisy neighbour

Context. B2B SaaS on ASP.NET and SQL Server. 300 tenants, one of them 40% of traffic. Compliance is mostly similar; two prospects want "their own database" in the contract. Team of twelve.

Decision. Shared database, tenant id in the token, EF global filter plus row-level security on sensitive tables, per-tenant rate limits, heavy reports on a replica. Silo only the tenants whose contract or threat model pays for a database and the ops cost. Do not silo everyone because two prospects asked.

Rejected. Database-per-tenant as the default — connection counts, migrations, a team that cannot babysit 300 instances. A shared database with isolation "by convention" only.

Risk accepted. A noisy tenant can still hurt others until limits and the replica exist. That is a this-sprint control, not a next-year platform.

Evolution. If a regulated tenant requires physical isolation, give them a cell you can operate. Do not let that cell's design become the default for the long tail.

CASE 7 · HIGH-VOLUME REPORTING

The dashboard that is killing checkout

Context. Finance wants live dashboards. The queries run on the OLTP primary. Checkout lock-waits during month end. "Real time" turns out to mean "this morning is fine, not last month's batch".

Decision. Get reporting off the primary this week — replica or a warehouse feed via the outbox. Pre-aggregate the heavy tiles. Cache tiles in Redis for a minute if the dashboard is popular. Tell finance the lag is one to five minutes and what that buys them: a checkout that works.

Rejected. A live query path on the primary with a bigger SKU as the only plan. A full warehouse programme before the replica. CQRS everywhere.

Not optimising. Sub-second freshness. Monitor: replica lag, primary CPU after the move, dashboard error rate.

CASE 8 · NOTIFICATION PLATFORM

Email, SMS, and a provider that throttles

Context. Orders, marketing, and password reset share one SMTP vendor and one queue. A marketing blast delays password resets. Consumers are not idempotent. Duplicates have happened.

Decision. Separate queues by criticality. Password reset and payment receipts are not on the marketing queue. Idempotency key per send. Rate-limit marketing. Provider failover only for transactional mail, and only if the second provider is already wired. Checkout never waits for SMTP.

Rejected. One topic for all messages because "simpler". Infinite consumer scale without a provider limit. Building our own mail transfer agent.

Risk accepted. Marketing can be late. Password reset cannot wait behind a blast. That is a business trade, made explicit.

CASE 9 · AI DOCUMENT CLASSIFICATION

Route incoming mail to the right department

Context. A shared inbox. Misroutes annoy staff. A misroute of a legal hold is serious. Volume justifies automation. A rules engine already catches the easy cases.

Decision. Rules first, model second. The model classifies only what rules do not claim. Confidence below threshold goes to a human queue. Store the model version on the decision. Eval set from last month's mail before any cutover. The model does not delete or forward outside the company. Cost cap. Fallback is the existing rules plus the human queue — the system already worked, slowly.

Rejected. Replace the rules engine on day one. An agent that replies to customers. Fine-tune before you have labels from the human queue.

Measure. Correction rate, not demo accuracy. If correction rate is worse than rules-only, turn the model off.

CASE 10 · GLOBAL API PLATFORM

Partners in two regions, one contract

Context. External partners call your order API. You are about to open a second geography with a residency rule. Today everything sits in one region, one SQL Server, a mix of ad hoc auth.

Decision. Freeze the public contract, version it, require idempotency keys on writes, put a gateway in front for auth, quotas, and correlation ids. Do not share one writable database across regions. Second region gets its own primary and a clear ownership of whose orders live there. Cross-region is asynchronous and minimal. Partner auth becomes the standard, not a per-team scheme. A global "active-active write anywhere" design is rejected until conflict rules exist, which they do not.

Rejected. One worldwide primary with a hopeful replica. A new API style per partner. Kubernetes as the answer to residency. Letting partners retry without keys.

Evolution. If a partner truly needs a global read of their own orders, build a read model replicated on purpose, with lag in the contract. Do not sneak writes across the residency boundary.

CONCEPT

Each case is the same loop: problem, constraint that dominates, options, a rejection, a risk you accept, a metric that reopens the decision.

WHY IT MATTERS

Interview system design is a case study with a clock. Practise the decision sentence, not the icon set.

EXAMPLE

Case 1 and case 2 both use SQL transactions. Case 1 still has a compensate path because a third party sits in the middle. Case 2 does not let that third party inside the posting transaction. Same tool, different judgement.

ARCHITECT THINKING

What is the system of record? What may lag? Who operates this? What did I refuse even though it is fashionable?

TRADE-OFFS

Every recommendation above leaves something slower, staler, or less magical. That is the proof it is a decision.

COMMON MISTAKE

Drawing the same microservice picture for the plant, the bank, and the assistant. The constraints are not the same. The diagram should not be either.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"Why this architecture?"

STRONG ANSWER

"Because the constraint that dominates is correctness of stock and payment, the team is one on-call rotation, and the date is the campaign. A modular monolith with a real transaction and an outbox meets that. I rejected services and a stock cache. I will revisit if lock waits survive a load test, not if someone prefers a different diagram."

FOLLOW-UP

"What if your scale assumption is wrong?" Name the trigger and the first change — usually cache the safe reads, cap the app tier, move reports — and the change you still will not make. Then stop talking. Let them push.

SECTION 32 — DECIDE, THEN LOOK

Thirty Architecture Judgement Exercises

Do not open the reasoning until you have said an answer aloud. A weak answer that you notice is worth more than a strong answer you only read. Each exercise is ambiguous on purpose.

EX 01 · SCALE

More servers, expensive database

Traffic is growing. SQL Server is already the expensive line item. The team proposes more application servers. What do you investigate first?

CANDIDATE THINKING SPACE Is the app tier saturated, or is it amplifying a database problem? Reads or writes? What would more instances do to connection count and lock waits?
STRONG

Investigate the bottleneck before adding the tier you control. Look at SQL CPU, lock waits, top queries, and whether the app is doing N+1 calls. If reports dominate, move them. If the app is genuinely CPU-bound and SQL is calm, then add instances — with a connection cap.

WEAK

"Scale out the API. Stateless apps scale horizontally."

FOLLOW-UP

The traces show lock waits on Orders during checkout. Now what? Shorten transactions, get HTTP calls out of the transaction, fix indexes. Do not add replicas for a write-lock problem.

EX 02 · DATA

Cache the stock count?

Product page p95 is 900ms. A developer wants Redis on the stock count so the page can render under 100ms. Checkout uses that same number to decide if it can sell.

THINK Is the page label the same decision as the sale? What new problem does a fast stale count create?
STRONG

Split the two uses. Cache display data. Let checkout run a conditional update against SQL. If the badge must be fast, mark it approximate. Do not let a cache authorise a sale.

WEAK

"Cache it with a short TTL. Five seconds is fine."

FOLLOW-UP

Marketing says oversell by one unit is acceptable. Then a bounded oversell policy can be a business rule, implemented deliberately — not an accidental TTL. Get that rule in writing.

EX 03 · ORG

The board was promised microservices

You have seven developers and a six-month rewrite of a transactional monolith. The CTO's slide already says microservices. What do you recommend in the room?

THINK How do you protect the transaction without humiliating the sponsor? What can still be called a platform?
STRONG

Recommend a modular monolith with explicit boundaries and an outbox, and one extraction only if a boundary earns it. Offer the CTO language that is still true: "service boundaries in the design, independent deployment when ownership or scale requires it." Do not implement twelve services to match a slide.

WEAK

"The CTO wants it, so we start splitting."

FOLLOW-UP

They insist on at least one service in production this quarter. Pick a side path — notifications or recommendations — not the ledger. Make the timeout and the failure mode part of the story.

EX 04 · INCIDENT

SECTION 33 — STAY IN THE CHAIN

Twenty Decision-Defense Conversations

Interviewers do not stop at your first tool. They walk down a chain: technology → problem → evidence → constraint → trade-off → decision. If you skip a step, they will ask the step. Practise moving down without getting defensive. The goal is not to win. The goal is to show that the choice is conditional and the condition is real.

HOW TO MOVE

When they say "why?", do not repeat the tool. Name the problem. When they say "how do you know?", give evidence or an assumption you will test. When they remove a constraint, change the decision. Changing your mind in the room is a strong signal. Clinging to Redis after they told you the data must be correct is a weak one.

01
Redis. Candidate: "I would use Redis." Interviewer: "Why?" — "For performance." — "What problem exactly?" — "Reduce database calls on the catalogue." — "What happens when Redis is down?" Strong close: "Catalogue falls back to SQL and may be slower. Checkout does not depend on it. If I cannot build that fallback, I do not make Redis mandatory. The trade is staleness of display data, which I accept for 60 seconds."
02
Microservices. "I'd split into services." — "Which boundary, and what transaction did you just break?" Strong close: "I would not split order and stock. I would split recommendations because they may fail open and another team owns them. The evidence I need is a separate scale or release pain, which I do not have yet. So v1 stays a modular monolith."
03
Kafka. "We need Kafka." — "What volume, and who operates the cluster?" Strong close: "The problem is a few thousand emails and an outbox. RabbitMQ, which we already run, is enough. Kafka becomes rational at a log-scale feed with replay consumers and a platform owner. I will not adopt it to feel ready."
04
NoSQL. "Orders should be in a document store. The schema changes." Strong close: "The schema of a line item changes. The invariants — paid amount, customer, idempotency key — do not. SQL Server with a JSON column for the flexible part keeps the constraints. I would revisit if the write shape truly does not fit rows and we have the operate skill."
05
Kubernetes. "Put it on AKS so it scales." — "What does not scale today?" Strong close: "I don't have evidence the host is the constraint. I have evidence we lack cluster on-call. App Service or the current host, stateless, with autoscale if traffic is spiky. AKS when a platform team owns the cluster and we need the shape. The trade of staying is less control. I accept that."
06
Retries. "Polly retry three times on everything." — "Including payment?" Strong close: "No. Retry idempotent reads with backoff. Payment uses a key and an unknown state. Immediate retries make a sick dependency sicker. The constraint is duplicate side effects. The decision is a cap plus idempotency, not more hope."
07
CQRS. "We'll CQRS the whole system." — "Which read is so different that a second model earns its inconsistency?" Strong close: "The dashboard. Not the checkout command. One write model in SQL, a projection for reports. Full CQRS everywhere adds a sync problem we do not have. I reject it as a default."
08
Event sourcing. "Event source the ledger so we have audit." Strong close: "Audit needs an immutable history, which posting rows already give if we never update them. Event sourcing is a new source of truth. I would take that cost only if we must reconstruct complex state from a true event log and the team has done it. Not for audit alone."
09
Multi-region. "Active-active for resilience." — "What is the downtime budget, and how do you resolve two writers?" Strong close: "I don't have a conflict rule, and the budget is hours not seconds. Multi-AZ plus tested backups. Active-active is a later decision if the contract and the skill exist. I am trading regional survival for a system we can reason about."
10
GraphQL. "Partners want GraphQL." — "Do they, or do they want fewer round trips?" Strong close: "If the pain is a chatty mobile client we own, a tailored endpoint is the smaller fix. If external partners truly need a query language, we version it, limit depth, and still keep a write model that is not 'the client composes transactions'. I will not adopt it because it is flexible."
11
Fine-tuning. "Fine-tune so it knows our policies." — "What happens when policy changes on Tuesday?" Strong close: "RAG, citations, permission filter. Fine-tune if the gap is format or tone after retrieval works. The constraint is freshness and source. A confident model with last quarter's policy is a worse product."
12
Local LLM. "We should self-host for security." — "Who patches the model, and where is the GPU capacity?" Strong close: "If residency forbids the cloud endpoint, local is the constraint and we staff it. If the concern is training on our data, a contractual no-train endpoint in-region solves that with less operational risk. I will not self-host to feel safer while nobody owns the box."
13
Shared database. "Services can share the database for speed of delivery." Strong close: "Then they are not services. I will allow a shared database inside a modular monolith with schema owners. I will not allow two deployables to write the same table and call it loose coupling. The short-term speed becomes a migration. I'd rather be honest about the monolith."
14
Cache balance. "Cache the wallet so the app feels instant." — "Can the user spend what they see?" Strong close: "Then the cache is an authorisation bug. Display a labelled figure if product insists, and authorise against SQL. The trade is a slower spend check. I accept it because a wrong spend is more expensive than a spinner."
15
Sync email. "Send the email in the request so support knows it went." Strong close: "Support can see an outbox state. The mail vendor must not fail the order. Constraint: checkout availability. Decision: commit, then send, retry idempotently. The user may see a delay. I will say that in the release note."
16
More replicas as the fix for locks. "Add three replicas, CPU is high." — "The waits are locks on the write." Strong close: "Replicas do not take the write. I need shorter transactions and better indexes. A replica still helps reports, so I may add one for that reason, not as the lock fix. If I cannot explain that distinction I should not be adding capacity."
17
Build identity. "We'll build login. The IdP is slow to approve." Strong close: "No. The delay is organisational. A homegrown password store is a long-term incident. Escalate the IdP approval, or use the existing directory. I will build the application's roles. I will not build authentication."
18
Agent autonomy. "Let the agent update the CRM without confirmation. Otherwise it isn't AI." Strong close: "Then it isn't safe yet. Draft the update, human confirms, audit the tool call, cap the fields it may touch. The constraint is a wrong write. When the eval set shows the tool call is boringly correct, we can widen it. Not before the demo."
19
Purge the queue. "Just purge it, the demo is in an hour." Strong close: "Only if those messages are regenerable and not money-related, and a person accepts the loss. Otherwise we dead-letter poison and scale idempotent consumers. A green graph that drops receipts is a worse demo. I am trading the demo's comfort for correctness."
20
Sharding. "Shard SQL now so we never have to." — "What is the write rate, and what is the shard key?" Strong close: "I don't have a key that matches every query, and I don't have a write ceiling. Sharding now freezes a key we will get wrong. Trigger: sustained write saturation after index and transaction work. Until then one primary, replicas for reads, cache for safe reads. I would rather revisit a seam than live with a bad shard key."
CONCEPT

Defense is the willingness to walk from the tool back to the problem, and to change the tool when the constraint changes. It is not stubbornness.

WHY IT MATTERS

This is the difference between a candidate who memorised a design and a candidate who can be in the room. Interviewers push because production will push.

EXAMPLE

Conversation 1 is the whole day in miniature. Redis is not the answer. Catalogue relief with a fallback is the answer. Stock is the place you withdraw the tool.

ARCHITECT THINKING

What fact, if they told me the opposite, would make me pick the other option? Say that fact before they ask. It makes you look calm.

TRADE-OFFS

Changing your mind can look inconsistent if you do not say "given that new constraint". Always tie the change to the new fact.

COMMON MISTAKE

Repeating "because it scales" at every step. Or treating a challenge as an attack and adding more technology to look confident.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"Why this, and what would change your mind?"

STRONG ANSWER

"I chose it for this constraint. Here is the evidence I have, here is what I am trading, and here is the fact that would make me choose the other option. If you have that fact, I will change the decision in this room."

FOLLOW-UP

They will give you the fact. Change the decision. Do not negotiate with the new fact to save your first diagram.

SECTION 34 — SIXTY QUESTIONS

System-Level Judgement Questions

Open one. Answer aloud in under two minutes. Then read the strong answer and notice what you skipped — usually the trade, the rejection, or the trigger to change your mind. Filter by level. These are not trivia. They are decisions.

Evaluates. Whether you can separate a catalogue from a choice.

Strong answer. Knowing Redis means I can operate it. A decision is choosing it for catalogue reads that may be a minute stale, and rejecting it for stock, because the new problem — oversell — is worse than the slow page.

Reasoning. Seniority is judged on the rejection and the consequence, not the feature list.

Trade-offs. You sound less certain than a candidate who lists tools. You sound more hireable to a careful panel.

Follow-up. "Give me a technology you know and would not use here."

Weak answer. "I have ten years of microservices, so I would use them."

Better structure. Tool I know → problem it does not fit → what I would use instead → what I give up.

Evaluates. Data-first thinking versus fashion.

Strong answer. What are the relationships and invariants? What must be transactionally true? What is the read shape, the retention, the size, and who already knows how to restore it? For orders I start at SQL Server if that is the skill we have, and I ask what would make it the wrong choice.

Reasoning. Pretending inconsistent constraints are a technical puzzle leads to a dishonest system.

Trade-offs. You may stall a deal. You avoid signing a sentence the system cannot keep.

Follow-up. "Pick the least-bad compromise yourself."

Weak answer. "We'll do active-active on the existing budget."

Better structure. Show the contradiction → offer a menu → do not choose their risk in silence.

Evaluates. Cost as an incident.

Strong answer. Cap or shed first if the curve is still rising. Then look for a retry loop, a model-default change, and token inflation before I look at "AI is just expensive". Route easy intents to search or a smaller model. Do not raise the cap as the investigation. If tools are writing in that loop, disable them.

Reasoning. A doubled bill is often a bug. Treating it as a finance fact hides the bug.

Trade-offs. Users lose a fancy answer for a day. The company does not discover the invoice later.

Follow-up. "Quality must stay identical."

Weak answer. "Ask for a larger budget."

Better structure. Stop the bleed → find the multiplier → redesign the cheap path → then talk price.

Evaluates. Governance judgement.

Strong answer. I govern one-way doors and the few standards that affect 2 a.m. — identity, tenancy, tracing, the record of truth. Two-way doors stay with teams. Exceptions have an owner and an expiry. I write ADRs so people do not need me in the room to know why. If every pull request needs me, I have governed taste, not risk.

Reasoning. Bottlenecks govern too much. Ungoverned estates fail on the things that mattered.

Trade-offs. Some local mistakes survive. The expensive mistakes get a review.

Follow-up. "Teams bypass you."

Weak answer. "An architecture review board for every service."

Better structure. Govern irreversibility → leave the rest → exception path → measure bypass.

Evaluates. Decision quality versus craft pride.

Strong answer. It means the decision was not successful, even if the code is clean. I want to know whether we solved the wrong problem, overbuilt, or ignored a constraint. I do not defend the diagram. I simplify or retarget, and I change what we measure next time so elegance cannot outvote the outcome.

Reasoning. Architecture is a means. A means that misses the end is a miss.

Trade-offs. Pride. Clarity. A team that learns the metric is the point.

Follow-up. "The team needs the win of the design."

Weak answer. "The business moved the goalposts. The architecture was correct."

Better structure. Outcome first → why it missed → what you change → what you stop defending.

Evaluates. Portfolio risk, not ticket risk.

Strong answer. I accept bounded, watched, owned risks: single-region for a domestic product, catalogue staleness, a delayed rewrite of an ugly module. I do not accept silent data exposure, untested restores, or an unbounded AI write tool. Acceptance without an owner is amnesia. Refusal without a smaller yes is a veto. I try to do neither.

Reasoning. Principal judgement is which risks the company is actually in, not which risks a slide deleted.

Trade-offs. You will be wrong about likelihood sometimes. The written acceptance makes that a decision, not a scandal of surprise.

Follow-up. "Name one you accepted and later regretted."

Weak answer. "I drive risk to zero."

Better structure. Class of risk → owner → watch → what class you will not own.

Evaluates. Incident leadership on an AI path.

Strong answer. Disable the retrieval path. Treat it as a data incident: who saw what, from which document, was it authorised. Preserve logs. Do not reopen on the same filter. A prompt change is not a reopening. I would rather have no assistant than a second leak while we study the model.

Reasoning. The model being "up" is irrelevant. The trust boundary failed.

Trade-offs. A visible feature outage. A contained breach window.

Follow-up. "Legal wants it left on for other tenants."

Weak answer. "Tune the prompt and monitor."

Better structure. Kill switch → scope the exposure → fix the control → prove it with a test.

Evaluates. Subtraction.

Strong answer. I ask what I would not rebuild. Services that share a team, a database, and a transaction merge. I freeze extra buses and extra caches and drain them as we touch them. I keep a split that has a real failure domain. I do not announce a greenfield. I delete one unjustified moving part and watch incidents and lead time.

Reasoning. Rewrite fantasies preserve the complexity for another year while people argue.

Trade-offs. Some isolation you might have wanted later. If it was fictional, you lose nothing.

Follow-up. "Owners identify with their service."

Weak answer. "Let's rewrite the platform properly this time."

Better structure. What would I remove → protect real contracts → strangler → measure.

Evaluates. Changing course as competence.

Strong answer. I point at the trigger I wrote down. "I assumed peak under 50 orders a second. We are past that and lock waits remain after index work. I am changing the design in the way I said I would." Trust is lost when you pretend the old decision is still right, or when you had no trigger and look erratic. I explain the new trade as clearly as the first one.

Reasoning. A revisit is the last step of the decision loop, not an apology for having decided.

Trade-offs. A public change of mind. A system that matches the facts.

Follow-up. "People say you flip-flop."

Weak answer. "We'll stick with the original architecture to stay consistent."

Better structure. Old assumption → new evidence → pre-committed trigger → new decision and new sacrifice.

SECTION 35 — FIFTY SNAP JUDGEMENTS

Rapid-Fire Judgement Round

There is no universal winner. Say it depends on the variable that actually decides, then pick under a stated fact. Print shows every card. On screen, step through them.

1 / 50
Redis or the database?
It depends on whether the value may be stale and whether the cache may fail open. Catalogue display: Redis. Stock or balance authorisation: the database. No freshness contract, no cache.
Queue or synchronous API?
It depends on whether the user needs the outcome now. Payment authorisation: sync, with timeout and unknown state. Email: queue. A queue in front of a decision the user must see hides failure.
SQL or NoSQL?
It depends on invariants, relations, and who can restore it. Orders and ledgers: SQL Server. A search or vector index can sit beside it. Schemaless is not a reason to drop constraints on money.
Monolith or microservices?
It depends on team size, transaction boundaries, and a real independent-deploy pain. One team, one transaction: modular monolith. A failing side path with its own owner: maybe one service. Not a service per noun.
RAG or fine-tuning?
It depends on whether the gap is fresh private facts or style. Changing policy: RAG with citations. Tone, after retrieval works: maybe fine-tune. A weekly policy change kills a fine-tune from last quarter.
Cache or database optimisation?
It depends on whether the query is already honest. Fix N+1 and scans first. Cache hot reads that may be stale. A cache in front of a bad query fails closed onto the bad query.
Retry or fail fast?
It depends on idempotency and load. A blip on an idempotent read: limited retry with jitter. A sick dependency or a payment: fail fast or record unknown. Tight retries are a self-inflicted load test.
Strong or eventual consistency?
It depends on the harm of being wrong for ten seconds. Ledger and stock decrement: strong. Catalogue, email, analytics: eventual. One label for the whole platform is a slogan, not a design.
Build or buy?
It depends on whether it is your product and who runs it at 2 a.m. Buy identity, mail transport, and usually the model API. Build pricing and ledger behaviour. Price the exit before you feel locked in.
App Service or AKS?
It depends on whether the host is the constraint and who is on call. One API, no cluster skill: stay on the host you can operate. A paved platform with an owner: AKS can be rational. Future-proof is not a requirement.
RabbitMQ or Service Bus?
It depends on what you already operate, residency, and features you will actually use. Do not run both plus Kafka. The outbox and the idempotent consumer matter more than the logo. Pick the broker the on-call can drain.
Shared database or database per service?
It depends on whether the split is real. Inside a monolith, shared SQL with schema owners is honest. Across deployables, shared writable tables are coupling. Give the service its data or stop calling it a service.
Read replica or a bigger primary?
It depends on reads versus write locks. Reports move to a replica. Lock waits on checkout writes need shorter transactions, not more replicas. Do not send read-after-write to a lagging replica.
Multi-AZ or multi-region?
It depends on the minute budget and whether users and data are global. Domestic 99.9%: multi-AZ may be enough. Region-loss promises need a price and a rehearsed failover. Active-active writes need a conflict rule.
Feature flag or ship it?
It depends on blast radius. A checkout pricing rule: flag. A quiet copy change: ship. Flags make risk reversible. Flags you never remove become debt. Remove them when the risk is gone.
Blue-green or rolling?
It depends on spare capacity and whether two versions can share the schema tonight. Blue-green rolls back faster and costs more during release. If the migration is incompatible, neither saves you. Expand-contract first.
EF Core or Dapper?
It depends on a measured hot path, not taste. Default to the team standard. Drop to SQL where a trace proves the abstraction hurts. Do not spend the architecture meeting on a micro-ORM preference.
In-process cache or Redis?
It depends on whether instances must agree. A short fallback when Redis is down: in-process is fine. A shared catalogue across nodes: Redis. An in-process cache is not a consistency strategy.
Outbox or publish directly?
It depends on whether losing the message after commit is acceptable. For mail about a paid order, no. Write the outbox in the same SQL transaction. Direct publish is simpler and can drop the message in the gap.
Saga or one transaction?
It depends on whether the facts can live in one database. If they can, use one transaction. A saga is for a justified split you can compensate. It is not a sign of maturity.
Gateway or direct exposure?
It depends on how many external callers exist. One internal app: a gateway can be ceremony. Many partners: auth, quotas, and correlation at the door are blast-radius tools. Do not put the business transaction in the gateway.
REST or events for partners?
It depends on whether they must confirm now. "Can I buy this" is a sync API. "Order shipped" can be a webhook or a queue. Do not force a partner onto a bus they did not ask to operate.
Version the API or evolve in place?
It depends on who compiles against it. External partners: version. Clients you deploy together: evolve carefully. A public JSON field is a one-way door. Treat it that way.
Idempotency key or a disabled button?
It depends on whether retries happen outside the UI. Timeouts, mobile replays, and queues duplicate. For money, the key is mandatory. The button is a courtesy. Clients lie. Networks duplicate.
RLS or an application filter only?
It depends on the harm of one missed predicate. Multi-tenant customer data: global filter plus RLS, and a test. A low-sensitivity internal tool with one query path may live with a tested filter. Convention alone is how leaks happen.
Column encryption or disk encryption?
It depends on who you are defending against and whether you must query the field. Disk encryption stops a stolen drive. Column encryption stops a curious reader and breaks search. Do not column-encrypt the catalogue. Do protect secrets with a real key design.
Search engine or SQL full-text?
It depends on whether relevance is the complaint. Until typos and ranking are the pain, SQL full-text is the smaller system. A search cluster is another restore. Add it when users feel relevance, not when the diagram wants a box.
Warehouse now or a replica first?
It depends on how fast reports must leave the primary. A replica is this week. A warehouse is the programme when history and heavy transforms are real. Do not start a warehouse while checkout is lock-waiting today.
Delete data or keep it forever?
It depends on legal hold, audit, and the blast of keeping secrets. Orders may live for years. Prompts and traces should die. "Keep everything" turns logs into an undocumented database.
Cells or one region?
It depends on whether one global failure is unacceptable and staffed. A single-region B2B app needs limits, bulkheads, and multi-AZ first. Cells are a later, expensive isolation choice, not a default.
Larger model or smaller model?
It depends on your eval set, not a leaderboard. Small model or search for easy intents. Large model where it measurably wins and the bill is acceptable. Double spend for the same correction rate is a failed decision.
Agent or a button?
It depends on whether a wrong action is compensable. A button on a tested API beats an agent that might call the wrong tool. Agents start read-only. Writes need confirmation matched to harm.
Citations or a free-form answer?
It depends on the cost of a confident lie. Policy, legal, tenant data: citations or "I do not know". Marketing draft copy can be free-form. No source, no pretence of fact.
Keyword search or vector search?
It depends on the query and the text quality. Codes and exact names: keyword. Paraphrase over decent text: hybrid. Bad scans: fix documents before a bigger embedding model. Vector without an ACL filter leaks.
Human in the loop or full automation?
It depends on the cost of being wrong and the correction rate. Invoice totals and refunds: human below a high bar. Catalogue tags: automate and sample. Full automation is a metric you earn, not a launch mood.
Sync the ERP or tolerate lag?
It depends on whether the floor can work while the ERP is down. Often the local record must survive and reconcile. Forced sync makes the vendor's outage your line stoppage. Lag needs a screen a human trusts.
One queue or many?
It depends on criticality. Password resets must not wait behind a marketing blast. Split by harm, not by every event type. Too many queues are debt. One queue for everything is a blast-radius bug.
Scale consumers or fix the handler?
It depends on why depth grows. Poison: do not scale. Slow idempotent work and a healthy downstream: scale. A throttled provider: scaling makes it worse. Look before you add.
Roll back or fix forward?
It depends on whether the data change is compatible. Compatible deploy: roll back. Irreversible migration: flag off, fix forward. Ego is not a strategy. Expand-contract exists so this question is rare.
Page or ticket?
It depends on harm now. Checkout down or a data leak: page. A stale tile: ticket. Paging everything trains people to ignore pages. Paging nothing trains customers to call the CEO.
Rewrite or put in a seam?
It depends on whether the pain is dislike or inability to change safely. Seam, tests, stop new coupling. Rewrite when containment costs more than a strangler. Old code is not a business case.
Standard or an exception?
It depends on the 2 a.m. skill gap. A new database for a form: no. A specialised search with an owner and a measured gap: maybe, with an expiry. Exceptions without expiry become the unofficial standard.
Log the payload or the id?
It depends on sensitivity. Payments, documents, prompts with secrets: ids. Forever-logs of PII are a second system of record. Sampled debug access can exist. It must expire.
Short timeout or wait longer?
It depends on whether the call is on the user path. Recommendations: short, fail open. A payment you must know: a bounded wait, then unknown. Long timeouts feel reliable and exhaust the thread pool.
Circuit breaker or keep calling?
It depends on whether more calls make it worse and whether you can degrade. Dead Redis: open the circuit, read SQL. A ledger write has no casual fallback. You can still stop a retry storm.
CDN or origin only?
It depends on whether the response is public. Public images: yes. Authenticated order JSON: no. A CDN that caches a private response is a breach. Edges are also data-residency flows. Check them.
One tenant filter or a filter in every query?
It depends on how many ways a developer can forget. One global filter is the design. "Remember the WHERE" is the incident. Add a test. Add RLS when raw SQL bypass matters.
Ship the AI demo or delay?
It depends on whether the demo can write or leak. Cited, filtered, read-only: ship if the eval set is honest. An agent that refunds: delay. Offer the safe slice. Thursday is not a control.
Accept the contract clause or refuse?
It depends on whether the system can make the sentence true at that price. 99.99%, on-prem, and a flat budget often cannot coexist. Offer a menu. A yes that hides an exception destroys trust later.
Measure the decision or move on?
It depends on whether you claimed an outcome. One-way doors need a review date. Two-way doors with no claim can move on. The metric is how you change your mind without looking erratic. No metric, no revisit trigger, no trust.

CLOCK

Round 1 — ten rapid judgements · 8 minutes

Saying "it depends" and not landing. Or landing the same tool every time.

Round 2 — five architecture decisions · 10 minutes

THINKING FRAMEWORK One-way doors first. Record of truth, then freshness, then failure, then what you leave imperfect.
STRONG SHAPE

SQL Server for orders. Redis for catalogue only. Payment sync, mail via outbox. If multi-tenant: shared DB, enforced tenant filter, silo only who pays. Not optimising: multi-region and a second search stack. Rejected: microservices and a stock cache.

"Why not Cosmos?" Invariants and skill. JSON column if a payload is flexible. Revisit only with a measured gap and an operator.

Rejections appear without being begged for.

COMMON MISTAKE

Five tools, zero sacrifices.

Round 3 — two ambiguous problems · 8 minutes

Prompt. "We need a scalable order platform." Then: "We need an AI assistant for internal policy, and the board wants it this month." Ask, assume, decide.

THINKING FRAMEWORK Three questions that delete options. Label assumptions. Do not invent scale or permissions.
STRONG SHAPE

Orders: ask peak, what must be correct together, date, who is on call. Assume one region and SQL skill if told. AI: ask who may see which document, what a wrong answer costs, whether data may leave the network. Ship cited RAG, no write tools, eval set, kill switch. Delay anything that writes.

"They cannot answer scale." Design for known peak plus headroom and a trigger. Do not shard on a shrug.

Questions before boxes. Assumptions spoken.

Drawing the full platform because the sentence was vague, which you treat as freedom.

Round 4 — two production incidents · 8 minutes

THINKING FRAMEWORK Stabilize, shrink blast, communicate, do not widen the blast with cleverness.
STRONG SHAPE

Scene one: roll back or flag off if the migration allows. Tell the bridge customers cannot pay, update in ten minutes. Scene two: shed catalogue load, circuit-break Redis, protect checkout, do not add API servers. Root cause after the bleed stops.

FOLLOW-UP

"Rollback will lose orders from the last five minutes because the migration rewrote a column." Then flag off, keep rows, fix forward. Say why Friday migrations without a reverse path are the prevention.

Customer truth before architecture essay. A refusal to restart SQL or disable auth.

COMMON MISTAKE

Adding capacity. Debugging for pride. Purging queues. Opportunistic redesign on the bridge.

STRONG SHAPE

Assume a campaign spike, state it, ask them to correct it. Modular monolith. Conditional stock update, payment outside the SQL transaction with an idempotency key, outbox for mail, Redis for catalogue display, confirmation read on the primary, reports on a replica. Reject services and a stock cache. Trigger to revisit: lock waits after a load test. Monitor: failed payments, oversells, SQL CPU, p95.

"Make it microservices." Walk the transaction you would break. Offer recommendations as the only split, with a timeout, fail open.

A decision sentence a CFO could hear, and a diagram that matches it. Numbers or explicit assumptions.

A box for every noun. No failure path. No rejected alternative. Kafka because scale was mentioned.

Round 6 — architecture defense · 6 minutes

STRONG SHAPE

"Redis is for catalogue text that can be a minute stale, with a SQL fallback. It is not in the sale decision. I would drop it if the primary is calm. They say the write ceiling is already here. You change: shorter transactions first if not done, then a specific split, not a cosmetic shard. You do not defend the old diagram out of pride.

EXPECTED BEHAVIOUR

Calm. Conditional. Willing to move.

COMMON MISTAKE

"Because it scales." Repeating it. Or abandoning every choice at the first push, which shows the choices were costume.

STRONG SHAPE

"The competitor said yes." They accepted a cost or a risk you have not seen. Ask which. Do not match a sentence you cannot keep.

Respectful, specific, no jargon lead. A decision requested from them.

COMMON MISTAKE

Folding. Or a technical monologue about Kubernetes that the VP cannot use.

CConstraints. Date, money, skill, law, team.
SScale. Real peak, not the slogan.
RRisks. Harm, likelihood, what you might accept.

CONCEPT

The framework is a compression of the whole day. If you can fill the ten letters under pressure, you can defend a decision. If you cannot, you are still listing tools.

WHY IT MATTERS

Interviewers remember a sentence with a sacrifice and a trigger. They forget a diagram that had every box.

EXAMPLE

Framework 5 is the emotional test. Changing course with a pre-written trigger is competence. Changing course with no memory of why you chose is chaos. Staying with a dead assumption is pride.

ARCHITECT THINKING

Can I say this in sixty seconds to a VP and in six minutes to a principal engineer without contradicting myself?

TRADE-OFFS

A framework can become a script. Use it to force the missing letter — usually T, M, or the second R — not to sound mechanical.

COMMON MISTAKE

Filling O and D only. Options and a choice, no risk, no measure, no revisit. That is a preference with stationery.

INTERVIEW QUESTION

"Walk me through how you decide."

STRONG ANSWER

"I name the problem, the constraint that dominates, and the real scale. I name the risk I will not take. I put two operable options on the table, including something boring. I choose, I say what gets worse, I say who feels it, and I write the metric and the fact that would make me reopen it. If I cannot fill those, I do not have a decision yet."

FOLLOW-UP

They ask you to do it on their system, live. Use the worksheet. Speak the letters. Do not skip T.

REVISE IN 15–20 MINUTES

System-Level Judgement Interview Cheat Sheet

Read this the morning of the interview. Do not cram tools. Cram the sentence, the doors, the incident order, and the things you will not trade.

THE SENTENCE

The problem is X. The constraint that dominates is Y. I considered A and B. I choose A, trading Z. I will know I was wrong if M. I will reopen if R.

IF THEY ASK YOU ANSWER WITH
Why this architecture? The dominant constraint, not the tool's reputation.
What else did you consider? Two real options plus the one you rejected and the reason.
What are the risks? One you will reduce, one you will accept with an owner, one you will not take.
What will you trade? A real sacrifice: region, feature, freshness, or speed — not "nothing".
What if the assumption is wrong? The trigger you would have written in the ADR. Then change.
What if the constraint changes? Show which letter of the framework moved, and the new decision.
Why should the business accept it? Customer impact, cost, and the risk of the alternative. No jargon first.

SLOW DOWN — ONE-WAY

  • Public API contract
  • Tenancy model
  • System of record and major data model
  • Encryption and data residency path
  • Vendor you cannot exit, including an LLM data path
  • Anything that authorises money or stock

MOVE — TWO-WAY

  • Internal module shape
  • Log fields, retry counts, TTLs
  • Prompt text, once the data path is safe
  • Which private worker sends mail
  • EF versus Dapper on a measured path
  • A feature flag you can remove

INCIDENT ORDER

  • Stabilize. Rollback if the deploy lines up.
  • Shrink blast. Flag, shed, throttle.
  • Evidence. Do not restart the evidence away.
  • Say what customers cannot do.
  • Mitigate. Then recover the journey, not the graph.
  • Cause and one prevention after.

DO NOT TRADE FOR A DATE

  • Authentication on state-changing or private reads
  • Tenant isolation
  • Secrets out of source
  • Tested restore of the system of record
  • Idempotency on money
  • An AI write without a bound, an audit, and a confirmation

Did I start with the problem? Did I reject something? Did I name what gets worse? Did I say what I will not trade? Did I say what would change my mind? If yes, you are thinking like an architect. If no, you have a diagram. Leave the diagram.

DAY 32 — CLOSE

Twenty Rules of Architect Thinking

Say these without notes. Each one is a decision habit, not a slogan. The full expansion lives in the lesson; this close is the set you should be able to defend.

01

Start with the problem, not the technology.

A team asked for Kafka. The problem was a checkout email that sometimes arrived twice. A transactional outbox on SQL Server solved it. Kafka would have added a platform nobody on call knew how to repair.

02

Ask before you assume.

"Scalable order platform" is not a requirement. Peak orders per minute, what must be correct, and who is on call at 2 a.m. are requirements. The first five minutes are questions.

03

Separate facts from assumptions.

Say "I am assuming one region and a campaign under 80 orders a second." Silence is not a fact. Inventing 10 million users because nobody corrected you is how you build the wrong system with confidence.

04

Make constraints visible.

A three-month date, five people, and a residency rule should be on the diagram, not in someone's head. Hidden constraints return as incidents and as "why didn't architecture know?"

05

Understand the cost of complexity.

Twelve services are not twelve features. They are tracing, deploys, partial failure, and a 2 a.m. skill gap. If the team cannot pay that bill, the diagram is unaffordable.

06

Design for failure.

Decide what happens when Redis, SQL, the acquirer, or the LLM is down before you call the design done. Retry-everything is not a failure design. Fail open or fail closed, per dependency.

07

Know what must be optimised, and what need not be.

Checkout correctness is the optimisation. Admin search can be slow. A catalogue badge can be stale. If everything is priority one, you have not decided.

08

Understand operational consequences.

A cluster, a second bus, or a local GPU model is a job, not a library. If nobody on the rota can drain the queue or restore the backup, you have not finished the design.

09

Prefer evidence over preference.

You like Dapper. They like a document store. Neither is a decision until a trace, a constraint, or a restore drill says so. Taste is allowed inside a two-way door.

10

Make trade-offs explicit.

"We will cache catalogue text and accept a minute of staleness. We will not cache stock." That sentence is the architecture. The gain without the loss is a sales line.

11

Know which decisions are reversible.

Move on a log field. Slow down on a public contract, a tenancy model, and the path customer data takes to a model vendor. Speed on the wrong door is how a quarter is lost later.

12

Do not solve imaginary scale problems.

Five hundred users do not need a shard key. They need a seam and a trigger. Draw the path to 10 million if asked. Do not operate it on Tuesday.

13

Do not confuse modern technology with good architecture.

Kubernetes, Kafka, and an agent are not arguments. A conditional UPDATE in SQL Server that prevents an oversell is architecture. Modern is allowed when it earns the operate cost.

14

Know when to simplify.

If you would not rebuild the extra cache, the extra bus, or the service that shares a table, remove it. Subtraction is a design. A rewrite fantasy is not simplification.

15

Know when to say no.

No, the reason, the evidence, the risk, the alternative, the impact. A naked no creates a workaround. A yes to an unauthenticated admin path creates an incident. Offer the smaller yes.

16

Know what you are willing to trade.

Region, feature, freshness, or a slower checkout write. Say it in the room. If you cannot name a sacrifice, you have not chosen. You have hoped.

17

Understand the blast radius.

Who fails together, through which shared pool, queue, or missing tenant filter? A timeout on recommendations and a cap on one tenant are architecture. A box labelled isolated is not.

18

Make security a design concern.

Not a phase. Tenant filters, secrets, and the authority an AI tool runs as are design decisions. "Internal network" and "the model seems polite" are not controls.

19

Measure whether the decision achieved its goal.

A green deploy is not success. Did checkout failures fall? Did cost stay inside the cap? Did the assistant leak? Review the claim you wrote down. Elegance that missed the outcome failed.

20

Change the decision when the assumption changes.

Point at the trigger. "We passed the write rate I named, and lock waits remain. Here is the next step I already described." Loyalty to a dead assumption is the failure. Changing your mind with evidence is the job.

Good architecture is not the design that sounds modern. It is the decision you can explain, the trade you can name, and the change you will make when the assumption breaks.
