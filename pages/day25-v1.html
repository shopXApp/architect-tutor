PART 01 — UNDERSTAND THE BUSINESS FIRST

You Cannot Architect What You Cannot Explain

Enterprise systems are not won by technology choices. They are won by understanding what the business actually does, where it loses money, and which mistakes are unforgivable. Choose technology first and you will build something impressive that solves the wrong problem beautifully.

WHY BUSINESS BEFORE TECHNOLOGY?

In this system, "the stock number is wrong" costs real money — a company sells goods it does not have, disappoints a customer, and loses trust. "The dashboard is 30 seconds late" costs nothing. Only the business can tell you which is which, and that single distinction decides your database strategy, your caching, your event design and your consistency model. An architect who starts with "we'll use microservices and Kafka" has already skipped the only conversation that matters.

The business in plain language

We are building one application that many separate companies use at the same time. Think of an apartment building: one structure, one set of pipes and lifts, but every apartment is locked and private. A wholesale distributor in one city, a fashion retailer in another, and an electronics importer somewhere else all log into the same platform — and each of them sees only their own world.

WHAT EACH COMPANY OWNSIts own users, customers, suppliers, products, orders, purchases, stock, payments, reports and settings. None of it is ever visible to another company.
WHAT THEY ALL SHAREThe same application code, the same servers, the same deployment, the same upgrades. One fix helps everyone; one bug can hurt everyone.
THE DAILY WORKSell goods (sales orders), buy goods (purchases), move goods (stock in, out, transfer, adjust), collect money (payments), and understand the business (dashboards and reports).
THE PAINFUL MOMENTSTwo salespeople selling the last unit; stock that does not match the shelf; a payment taken with no order recorded; a report that freezes the whole system at month-end.

The business problem, stated simply

"Thousands of independent companies need to run their buying, selling and stock on one shared platform — without ever seeing each other's data, without the busiest company slowing down everyone else, without stock numbers going wrong when many people work at once, and without heavy reports damaging day-to-day trading."

Read that sentence again: it contains the four architectural pillars of the entire day — isolation, fair sharing, transactional correctness, and read/write separation. Everything in Parts 6–28 exists to deliver one of those four.

What the business cares about (and what it does not)

THE BUSINESS SAYS… THE ARCHITECT HEARS…
"We can never show one client's data to another." Tenant isolation is a hard requirement enforced in multiple layers, not a WHERE clause someone might forget.
"We must never oversell stock." Inventory needs atomic, transactional updates with reservations — the one place where strong consistency is non-negotiable.
"Order entry must feel instant." The write path stays short and synchronous; everything optional moves to background work.
"Managers live in dashboards." Read traffic will dwarf write traffic — separate read models and caching, or reports will strangle orders.
"We have two people running operations." Operational simplicity is a first-class requirement. A design nobody can debug at 3 a.m. is a bad design regardless of elegance.
"We're onboarding bigger clients next year." Plan for 5× growth and one very large tenant without rewriting the platform.
THE INTERVIEW MOVE

Open with: "Let me restate the business in one sentence to make sure I'm solving the right problem…" Then say the sentence above. Interviewers rarely hear it, and it instantly separates an architect from a senior developer.

PART 02 — FUNCTIONAL REQUIREMENTS

What the Platform Must Actually Do

Functional requirements are the things users can do. Group them by business area — that grouping becomes your module map in Part 5, so doing this carefully saves you twice.

AREA WHAT USERS CAN DO ARCHITECTURAL SIGNAL
Company management Create a company (tenant), configure settings (currency, tax rules, numbering, fiscal year, branding), company-specific configuration per module, and complete isolation from other companies. Every table, cache key, file path, queue message and log line needs a company id. Settings are read constantly → prime cache candidate.
User management Login, roles (admin, sales, inventory, purchase, reports), fine-grained permissions, user invitation and deactivation, per-company user lists. One person may belong to more than one company → identity is global, membership is per company.
Product management Products, categories, variants (size, colour), units of measure, barcodes, price lists, per-company product configuration. Read-heavy master data, rarely changed → cache aggressively, invalidate on edit.
Customer management Customer records, multiple addresses, credit limit and outstanding balance, order history, contact people. Credit checks sit on the order write path → must be accurate, not cached blindly.
Supplier management Supplier records, purchase history, supplier balances, lead times, payment terms. Mirror of customers; feeds the purchase flow and stock receipts.
Sales orders Create, update, confirm, cancel an order; order lines with prices and discounts; order status history; document numbering per company. The core transaction. Needs speed, correctness, idempotency and a clean state machine.
Purchases Create purchase orders, receive stock (full or partial), record supplier invoices, handle returns. Increases stock — same inventory engine as sales, opposite direction.
Inventory Stock in, stock out, adjustments (damage, count corrections), transfers between warehouses, current stock per item/location, full movement history. The concurrency battleground (Part 11). Current stock must be exact; history is append-only.
Payments Record customer payments, refunds, payment status, allocation to invoices, provider integration. Money → idempotency keys, reconciliation, never trust a single external response.
Reporting Sales, inventory and purchase reports, dashboards, period comparisons, exports, company-level analytics. Heavy reads that must never touch the transactional hot path (Parts 12 & 17).
Notifications Email (order confirmations, low stock), SMS, push to future mobile apps, per-user preferences. External, slow, failure-prone → always background work.
Audit Who did what and when: user activity, business events, important changes (price edits, stock adjustments, permission changes). Append-only, high volume, read rarely — separate storage and retention.
PRIORITIZE OUT LOUD

Not every requirement deserves equal architecture. Say: "The critical path is order → stock → payment. Everything else — reports, notifications, audit, exports — supports that path and must never block it." That one sentence orders your whole design.

PART 03 — NON-FUNCTIONAL REQUIREMENTS

The Qualities That Actually Shape the Design

Non-functional requirements describe how well the system must behave. Each line below silently decides part of the architecture — so each row states the decision it forces.

REQUIREMENT WHAT IT MEANS WHY IT CHANGES THE ARCHITECTURE
10,000 companies Ten thousand separate tenants on one platform. Rules out "one database per company" as the default — 10,000 databases means 10,000 migrations, backups and connection pools. Drives shared-schema tenancy with a hybrid escape hatch (Part 8).
1 million users Total registered accounts across all tenants. Identity and permission lookups happen on every request → cache permissions, keep tokens compact.
100,000 concurrent users People actively using the system at peak. Stateless API servers behind a load balancer so any node can serve anyone; session state never lives in server memory.
5,000 requests/second peak The busiest second of the busiest day. Sizing anchor for API nodes, connection pools, cache and replicas. Most of it is reads → read replicas + cache absorb the bulk.
24/7 availability, 99.9% target ~43 minutes of downtime per month is the budget. Redundant nodes, zero-downtime deploys, health checks, automated failover, and graceful degradation when a dependency dies.
Fast order creation Order save feels instant (target p95 well under a second). Short synchronous write path: validate → reserve stock → save → return. Email, reporting, integrations all move to background.
Heavy dashboards & reporting Managers refresh dashboards constantly; month-end is brutal. Separate read model / reporting store fed by events, plus caching. Without it, one report can lock the tables orders need.
5× expected growth 50,000 companies or 5× traffic within a few years. Design must scale by adding (nodes, replicas, shards, tenant groups), never by rewriting. Put the tenant key in every layer from day one.
Secure tenant isolation No company can ever read another's data — contractually and legally. Defence in depth: token claims, enforced query filters, per-tenant storage paths, audit, and automated tests that try to break it (Part 21).
Disaster recovery Survive losing a database, a zone, or a region. Backups with tested restores, replication, documented RPO/RTO, and a runbook people have actually rehearsed (Part 25).
Future mobile apps Field sales and warehouse scanning apps are coming. API-first design, token auth that works off-browser, small payloads, offline-tolerant endpoints, versioned contracts.
Future AI features Demand forecasting, smart reordering, anomaly detection. Keep clean event history and an analytics store — AI needs data that was captured properly long before the feature is requested.
Small DevOps team A handful of people run everything. The strongest argument against premature microservices. Every moving part must justify its operational cost.
THE NUMBER THAT MATTERS MOST

5,000 RPS at peak with ~50,000 orders/day means writes are modest (roughly 1–2 order writes per second on average, maybe 20–30/sec at peak) while reads are enormous. This is a read-heavy system with a small, precious write core. Say that early — it justifies nearly every later decision.

PART 04 — ASK CLARIFYING QUESTIONS

24 Questions to Ask Before You Draw a Single Box

The first ten minutes of an architect interview should be questions. Each one below comes with why it matters — because asking without knowing why you asked is just theatre.

Business questions

  1. What kind of companies use this — retail, wholesale, manufacturing, or all three?Why: manufacturing needs bill-of-materials and work orders; wholesale needs price tiers and credit. This changes the domain model, not just screens.BUSINESS
  2. What is the average and largest company size — 5 users or 5,000? 100 products or 500,000?Why: tenant size distribution decides whether one shared design works or you need a hybrid with dedicated resources for whales.BUSINESS
  3. Do companies need multiple warehouses, branches or legal entities?Why: stock is tracked per location; adding location later touches every inventory query and index.BUSINESS
  4. Is overselling ever acceptable (backorders), or must stock never go negative?Why: this single answer decides whether inventory needs hard reservations and locking or can be eventually consistent.BUSINESS

User questions

  1. Can one person belong to several companies with one login?Why: decides whether identity is global with per-tenant membership, or duplicated per tenant — a very expensive thing to change later.USERS
  2. Are roles fixed (admin/sales/inventory) or fully customizable per company?Why: custom roles mean a permission engine and per-tenant permission data on every request instead of a simple enum.USERS
  3. Do users work offline (warehouse scanners, field sales)?Why: offline means client-side queues, idempotent APIs and conflict rules — architecture, not a mobile detail.USERS

Scale questions

  1. Is the 5,000 RPS mostly reads or writes, and what is the read:write ratio?Why: read-heavy is solved with replicas and cache; write-heavy needs partitioning and a different database conversation entirely.SCALE
  2. Are there predictable peaks — month-end, year-end, seasonal sales?Why: predictable peaks are cheap (pre-scale); random peaks need permanent headroom or autoscaling.SCALE
  3. What is the largest single tenant's share of total traffic today?Why: if one company is 30% of load, the noisy-tenant problem is already real (Part 24), not theoretical.SCALE
  4. How many orders and stock movements per day, and how fast is that growing?Why: drives table growth, partitioning strategy and archiving policy.SCALE

Data questions

  1. How long must transaction history and audit data be kept?Why: seven years of retention changes storage tiering, partitioning and cost dramatically.DATA
  2. Can companies export or delete all their data (offboarding, GDPR)?Why: "delete everything for tenant X" is nearly impossible in shared tables unless designed for from day one.DATA
  3. Does each company need custom fields on products, customers or orders?Why: per-tenant schema flexibility pushes you toward a document column or an EAV design — a big modelling decision.DATA
  4. How accurate must reports be — live to the second, or is a few minutes acceptable?Why: this is the permission slip for a separate read model. Without it, every report hits the transactional database.DATA

Security questions

  1. Are there compliance requirements — PCI, GDPR, SOC 2, local data residency?Why: residency can force regional deployments; PCI forces payment data out of your scope entirely.SECURITY
  2. Do enterprise clients demand their data in a physically separate database?Why: a common sales promise. If yes, the hybrid tenancy model is mandatory, not optional.SECURITY
  3. Who inside our own company may access tenant data, and how is that audited?Why: insider access is the most common real-world breach path; it needs design, not policy alone.SECURITY

Availability questions

  1. What does one hour of downtime cost the business?Why: converts "99.9%" into a budget. If it's trivial, don't buy multi-region; if it's huge, 99.9% is too low.AVAILABILITY
  2. Is a maintenance window allowed, or must upgrades be zero-downtime?Why: zero-downtime across 10,000 tenants forces backward-compatible migrations and rolling deploys.AVAILABILITY
  3. What is the acceptable data loss (RPO) and recovery time (RTO) after a disaster?Why: "zero data loss" costs many times more than "up to five minutes" — the business must choose knowingly.AVAILABILITY

Reporting, integration and deployment questions

  1. Do customers need custom reports or raw data access (API/BI tools)?Why: arbitrary customer queries against your live database is a self-inflicted outage; it forces a governed reporting layer.REPORTING
  2. Which external systems are mandatory at launch — payment, accounting, shipping, e-commerce?Why: each integration is a failure domain with timeouts, retries and duplicate risk that must be isolated (Part 20).INTEGRATION
  3. Are integrations per-tenant configurable (each company its own provider/keys)?Why: per-tenant credentials and endpoints change configuration storage, secret management and blast radius.INTEGRATION
  4. What is the deployment target and how large is the operations team?Why: the honest answer here decides monolith vs microservices more than any technical argument (Part 6).DEPLOYMENT
THE FOUR ANSWERS THAT CHANGE EVERYTHING

If you only get four: (1) read:write ratio, (2) can stock go negative, (3) does any client demand a separate database, (4) how big is the ops team. Those four determine your read model, your locking strategy, your tenancy model and your architecture style — the entire backbone of this design.

Product owns descriptions and prices; Inventory owns quantities. If you get asked one modelling question today, it will be this one.

THE INTERVIEW LINE

"Thirteen business areas, all starting as modules inside one deployable application with enforced boundaries — separate schemas or table groups, no cross-module table access, and communication through interfaces and events. Notification and Reporting leave first, then Inventory or Payment when a measured reason appears."

PART 06 — CHOOSE THE OVERALL ARCHITECTURE

Big System ≠ Microservices

The most expensive mistake in enterprise architecture is choosing microservices because the domain is large. Size justifies good boundaries. Only specific pressures — independent scaling, independent deployment, team autonomy, failure isolation — justify separate deployables.

The three options in plain English

OPTION A — TRADITIONAL MONOLITHOne application, one database, code organized by technical layers (controllers, services, repositories) with no enforced business boundaries. Anything can call anything.
OPTION B — MODULAR MONOLITHOne deployable application, but code is split into strict business modules with their own tables and public interfaces. Modules talk through contracts and events, never through each other's tables.
OPTION C — MICROSERVICESMany small applications, each deployed separately with its own database, communicating over the network via APIs and events.

Honest evaluation against our situation

CRITERION A — MONOLITH B — MODULAR MONOLITH C — MICROSERVICES
Team size Fine for a small team. Ideal for one to three teams sharing a codebase with clear ownership. Needs many autonomous teams; a small team ends up maintaining plumbing instead of features.
Business complexity Complexity turns into spaghetti — order code reads inventory tables directly. Handles high complexity through explicit boundaries. Handles it too, but pays with distributed complexity.
Traffic (5,000 RPS) Handled by scaling copies of the app. Same — a stateless modular monolith scales horizontally very well. Also handled, but 5,000 RPS is not a number that requires it.
Deployment needs One risky release for everything. One release, but modular code plus feature flags reduce risk. Independent releases per service — the real prize.
Scaling All-or-nothing: scale everything to fix reporting load. Scale copies of the whole app; separate the read model to relieve the hot spot. Scale precisely the part that hurts.
Cost Lowest. Low — a few app nodes, one primary database, replicas, cache, broker. Highest: more nodes, more databases, gateways, service mesh, tracing.
DevOps maturity Minimal requirement. Moderate — CI/CD, monitoring, one deployment pipeline. High — service discovery, distributed tracing, per-service pipelines, on-call depth. We have a limited team.
Data consistency Easy: local database transactions. Easy: order + stock reservation commit in one transaction. Hard: cross-service transactions become sagas with compensation — real risk for stock and money.
Failure handling One bad module can take the whole app down. Same process risk, mitigated by bulkheads, timeouts and async work. Failures isolate — if you build the resilience patterns properly.
Future growth Painful to break apart later. Boundaries already exist → extraction is planned, not archaeological. Already there — if you survive building it.
RECOMMENDATION — MODULAR MONOLITH (OPTION B) WITH A PLANNED EXTRACTION PATH

Build one deployable application containing thirteen strictly bounded modules, scaled horizontally behind a load balancer, with a separate read/reporting store, a cache, a message broker and background workers. Extract Notification and Reporting as the first real services, then Inventory or Payment only when a measured trigger appears.

Why this is right now

  • The critical transaction stays simple. Order creation must reserve stock and save the order atomically. In one database that is a single transaction. Across services it is a distributed saga with compensation — enormous complexity for a business that hates overselling.
  • The real bottleneck is reporting, not order volume. 50,000 orders/day is small; heavy dashboards are the pain. That is solved by a read model and cache, not by splitting the write side into services.
  • The DevOps team is small. Microservices convert architecture problems into operations problems. With a limited team, that trade destroys velocity and reliability at once.
  • Boundaries deliver most of the benefit. Clean modules give testability, ownership and replaceability — the genuine wins usually credited to microservices — without network failure between every call.
  • It preserves optionality. A well-modularized monolith can become services later. A premature distributed system rarely becomes simple again.

What would make us change the architecture later

TRIGGER (MEASURED, NOT FELT) THE MOVE IT JUSTIFIES
Reporting load keeps destabilizing transactional performance despite the read model. Extract Reporting into its own service with its own store and scaling.
Notification volume/spikes or vendor issues affect app nodes. Extract Notification (it has no shared transactions — easiest first cut).
Inventory contention becomes the dominant scaling limit, or warehouse/mobile traffic needs separate scaling and SLAs. Extract Inventory — accepting that order/stock coordination becomes a saga.
Payment compliance (PCI scope) or many per-tenant providers demand isolation and independent release cadence. Extract Payment behind an idempotent interface.
Team grows past ~3 squads stepping on each other in one release train. Split along module lines already drawn — organizational trigger, not technical.
A tenant contractually requires isolated infrastructure. Deploy a dedicated stack/cell for that tenant (hybrid tenancy, Part 8) — not a global re-architecture.
HOW TO SAY IT IN THE INTERVIEW

"I'd choose a modular monolith with hard module boundaries, a separate read model and a documented extraction path. Microservices are a solution to independent deployment, independent scaling and team autonomy — I don't have those problems yet, but I do have a small ops team and a stock transaction that must be atomic. I'll extract Notification and Reporting first, and I've written down the triggers that would make me extract more."

PART 08 — MULTI-TENANT DESIGN

The Decision That Defines This Platform

Multi-tenant means many separate customers (tenants) share one running system while believing they each have their own. The apartment building again: shared structure, private locked homes. How you draw the walls decides security, cost and operations for years.

The four options

CRITERION A — SHARED DB, SHARED TABLES (TenantId column) B — SHARED DB, SCHEMA PER COMPANY C — DATABASE PER COMPANY D — HYBRID (grouped + dedicated)
Security / isolation Logical only — one missing filter leaks data. Must be enforced by the framework, never by hand. Better — separate namespaces, per-schema permissions possible. Strongest — physical separation, separate credentials. Strong where it's needed, cheap where it isn't.
Cost Lowest — one database, shared resources. Moderate — many schemas add overhead. Highest — 10,000 databases, pools, licences, backups. Controlled — most tenants cheap, a few premium.
Scale to 10,000 Easy — one schema, huge tables handled by indexing/partitioning. Painful — thousands of schemas strain tooling and metadata. Operationally brutal at this count. Scales by adding tenant groups (cells).
Maintenance / migrations One migration for everyone. Run per schema — thousands of executions per release. Thousands of migrations; version drift is inevitable. Per group — manageable batches.
Backup / restore Restoring one tenant is hard — you restore everything and extract. Per-schema export is possible. Trivial per tenant — the big selling point. Per-cell restore; dedicated tenants restore alone.
Performance / noisy tenant One heavy tenant can hurt all — needs limits and monitoring. Slightly better; still shared resources. Fully isolated performance. Whales moved out of the shared pool.
Operational complexity Simplest to run. Medium. Very high — needs a platform team we don't have. Medium — two paths to support, one automated.
RECOMMENDATION FOR 10,000 COMPANIES

Start with Option A — shared database, shared tables, mandatory TenantId — and design from day one so it can grow into Option D (hybrid). Concretely: every tenant-owned table carries CompanyId as the first column of its primary index; a global query filter applies it automatically; tenants are assigned to a cell (a database group) via a tenant routing registry, so we can later move a large or premium tenant to a dedicated database without changing application code. That routing indirection costs almost nothing today and buys the entire hybrid future.

How tenant identity flows through the system

FIG 2 — TENANT CONTEXT FLOW: DECIDED AT LOGIN, ENFORCED AT EVERY LAYER
  1. LoginThe user authenticates and — if they belong to several companies — selects which company they are working in. The server verifies that membership from the database, never from anything the client sent.
  2. TokenThe issued token contains user id, company id and roles, and is digitally signed so it cannot be edited. A user wanting another company must obtain a new token — switching context is an authenticated action, not a parameter.
  3. API layerEvery request's token is validated and the company id is placed into a per-request tenant context. Any company id appearing in the URL or body is compared with the token and rejected on mismatch.
  4. Business logicAll queries and commands take the tenant from the ambient context. Developers cannot pass a different tenant "by accident" because they never pass it manually.
  5. DatabaseA global filter automatically appends the company condition to every query on tenant-owned tables; CompanyId leads every index and unique key (e.g. order number is unique per company). Writes stamp the tenant automatically.

How we stop Company A reading Company B's data

  • Single source of tenant truth: the signed token — client input is never trusted.
  • Automatic enforcement: a framework-level query filter, so isolation is the default and forgetting is impossible; bypassing it requires an explicit, reviewed, logged escape hatch.
  • Data model support: tenant-first composite indexes and per-tenant unique constraints make cross-tenant reads both wrong and unnatural.
  • Everything else is namespaced: cache keys, file paths, queue messages, search indexes, report models and log entries all carry the tenant.
  • Verification: automated tests that deliberately attempt cross-tenant access on every endpoint, plus audit logs and anomaly alerts on any query that runs without a tenant filter.
THE HONEST RISK

Shared tables mean isolation is logical, not physical. One bad query in a rarely used report can leak data. That is why enforcement must be automatic and centralized rather than remembered by every developer — and why the hybrid escape hatch exists for clients whose contracts require physical separation. Say this openly in an interview; pretending shared tables are risk-free is what loses credibility.

PART 09 — AUTHENTICATION AND AUTHORIZATION

Who Are You, and What May You Do Here?

Authentication answers "who are you?" Authorization answers "what are you allowed to do?" In a multi-tenant system there is a third question hiding between them: "in which company are you acting right now?" Miss that one and everything else collapses.

ELEMENT DESIGN
Login Email/username + password with MFA available; enterprise tenants may use their own single sign-on. If the user belongs to several companies, they choose one — the server verifies the membership.
Token Short-lived signed access token carrying user id, company id, role names and a permission version stamp; longer-lived refresh token to renew. Short life means a revoked user loses access quickly.
User identity Global — one person, one account, even across companies. Avoids duplicate passwords and makes offboarding coherent.
Company identity Membership records link user → company → roles. Switching company issues a new token; nothing about the current company is ever taken from client input.
Roles Named bundles of permissions, defined per company so tenants can customize without affecting others.
Permissions Fine-grained action rights (order.create, stock.adjust, report.view.financial) checked in the business layer, cached per user with invalidation on change.

Example roles

ROLE TYPICAL PERMISSIONS EXPLICITLY DENIED
Company Admin Everything within their own company: users, roles, settings, all documents, financial reports. Anything belonging to another company; platform-level administration.
Sales User Create/update orders, view customers and product prices, view available stock, view own sales reports. Adjusting stock, approving purchases, viewing cost prices or company-wide financials.
Inventory User Stock in/out, adjustments, transfers, receiving goods, inventory reports. Creating sales orders, seeing customer credit data, issuing refunds.
Purchase User Create purchase orders, manage suppliers, record receipts and supplier invoices. Sales orders, customer data, stock adjustments outside receiving.
Report User Read-only access to dashboards and permitted report categories; export. Any write operation anywhere.

Enforcing authorization consistently

  1. One central mechanism, used everywhere. A single permission-check component invoked by every module — not scattered if role == "admin" checks that drift apart over years.
  2. Check at the business layer, not only the UI. Hiding a button is user experience; blocking the action server-side is security. Mobile and API clients bypass your UI entirely.
  3. Deny by default. A new endpoint with no declared permission must refuse access rather than allow it — the cheapest way to prevent accidental exposure.
  4. Tenant check and permission check are separate gates. "May this user do this action?" and "does this record belong to their company?" must both pass, always, independently.
  5. Cache permissions carefully. Cache per user with a version stamp; bump the version when roles change so revocation takes effect in seconds, not at token expiry.
  6. Audit sensitive actions. Price changes, stock adjustments, permission edits and data exports are logged with actor, tenant, time and before/after values.
THE ONE-LINE SUMMARY

"Authentication proves the person, the token pins the company, permissions decide the action — and all three are enforced server-side by shared infrastructure that developers cannot forget to call."

PART 10 — ORDER FLOW

One Order, End to End

The order path is the business. Keep it short, synchronous and exact; push everything else into the background. Label each step as you walk it — that labelling is the architect's signature move.

  1. IMMEDIATECustomer / sales user starts an orderThe UI loads customer, price list and available stock — all cached or replica reads. Nothing is written yet.
  2. MUST BE EXACTCreate order request arrivesThe request carries a client-generated request id so a retry (double-click, mobile network blip) cannot create two orders.
  3. VALIDATEValidationTenant and permission checks; customer exists and is active; credit limit not exceeded; products belong to this company; prices and discounts recalculated server-side; quantities sane.
  4. ATOMICSave order + reserve inventory in one transactionThe order row and its lines are written and stock is reserved (not yet deducted) in the same database transaction. Either both happen or neither does — this single decision is why we chose a modular monolith.
  5. IMMEDIATERespond to the userOrder number returned, confirmation shown. Target: well under a second. Everything after this point can happen later without the user waiting.
  6. EVENTPublish OrderCreatedOne event carrying tenant, order id and summary — the trigger for all downstream work.
  7. DEPENDSPaymentFor card payment at checkout this is synchronous (the user waits for approval) and idempotent. For credit customers ("pay on invoice") it is recorded later and entirely asynchronous.
  8. CONFIRMOrder confirmationOn payment success (or credit approval) the order moves to Confirmed and the reservation converts to a real stock deduction — again atomically, guarded by the order state machine.
  9. BACKGROUNDNotificationsEmail/SMS confirmation to the customer, alerts to staff, low-stock warnings. Slow external providers never touch the order path.
  10. BACKGROUNDReporting updateWorkers consume the events and update dashboards and report models. Seconds of delay are acceptable and expected.
  11. BACKGROUNDIntegrations & documentsPush to accounting, create shipment, generate the invoice PDF, write audit entries — all retried independently.
CATEGORY STEPS WHY
Immediate (user waits) Validation, credit check, stock reservation, order save, order number, card payment result. These decide whether the sale is real. Wrong or missing here means overselling or lost money.
Background (user does not wait) Emails, SMS, invoice PDFs, report models, accounting sync, shipping booking, audit, analytics. Slow, external or optional. Their failure must never fail an order.
Where events help OrderCreated, InventoryReserved, PaymentCompleted, OrderConfirmed, InventoryUpdated. Many consumers react without the order module knowing they exist — new features subscribe instead of editing the write path.
THE CLASSIC TRAP

Sending the confirmation email inside the order transaction. The mail server hangs for 30 seconds, the database transaction stays open holding locks, other orders queue behind it, and one slow vendor becomes a platform outage. Nothing external belongs inside a business transaction.

PART 11 — INVENTORY DESIGN

Two Users, One Last Unit

Inventory is where correctness is tested hardest. Two salespeople open the last item at the same moment; both screens say "1 available". Only one may succeed — and the system must guarantee it without slowing everyone down.

The stock model

CONCEPT MEANING
On hand Physically in the warehouse right now.
Reserved Promised to open orders but not yet shipped.
Available On hand − reserved. This is the number sales may sell against.
Movement ledger Append-only history of every change: purchase receipt, sale, adjustment, transfer — with reason, user, document reference and timestamp.

Balances are the fast current truth; the ledger is the auditable explanation. Every operation writes both in one transaction, so you can always answer "why is stock 7?" — a question auditors and angry warehouse managers ask constantly.

The operations

  • Reservation (order created): available decreases, on hand unchanged. Reservations carry an expiry for draft/unpaid orders so abandoned carts release stock automatically.
  • Release (order cancelled or reservation expired): reservation removed, available restored.
  • Deduction (goods shipped/confirmed): on hand decreases, reservation cleared, ledger entry written.
  • Purchase receipt: on hand increases; partial receipts supported; ledger records supplier and document.
  • Adjustment: correction after a physical count or damage — always requires a reason code and is always audited.
  • Transfer: decrease at source, increase at destination, one transaction, optionally with an in-transit state for long moves.

Preventing two users selling the same unit

  1. Never read-then-write in two steps"Check available = 1, then save order" leaves a gap where both users pass the check. The decrement itself must be the check: a single conditional update that only succeeds while enough stock remains.
  2. Make the update atomic and conditionalAtomic means indivisible — no other transaction sees it half-done. The reservation is one statement that reduces available only if available is still sufficient. The second user's statement affects zero rows and their order is refused with "insufficient stock".
  3. Wrap order + reservation in one transactionBoth commit together or roll back together, so an order can never exist without its stock reservation.
  4. Choose locking deliberatelyPessimistic (lock the stock row for the moment of update) is simple and correct, and fine here because the lock is held for milliseconds on a single row. Optimistic (version number, retry on conflict) avoids locks but needs retry logic. Either works; what fails is no strategy.
  5. Keep transactions tinyDo validation, pricing and credit checks before opening the transaction. Inside it: write order, write reservation, write ledger, commit. Milliseconds, one row per product — contention stays low even at peak.
  6. Expire reservationsA background job releases reservations for abandoned orders so stock does not stay locked by ghosts.
  7. Reconcile and alertA periodic job recomputes balances from the ledger and alerts on any mismatch — the safety net that catches bugs before customers do.
THE ANSWER TO MEMORIZE

"Available stock is changed by a single atomic conditional update inside the same transaction that writes the order, backed by an append-only movement ledger. The database serializes the two users; the loser gets a clean 'insufficient stock' message rather than a silent oversell."

IF THE BUSINESS ALLOWS BACKORDERS

Then stock may go negative and the whole problem softens — you can accept the order and reconcile later. Always ask this question (Part 4) before designing heavy locking: sometimes the business has already solved your hardest technical problem for you.

PART 12 — READ VS WRITE DESIGN

Small Precious Writes, Enormous Forgiving Reads

The two workloads in this platform want opposite things. Writes want correctness, locks and normalized tables. Reads want speed, denormalized shapes and freedom to be slightly stale. Serving both from one place is what makes enterprise systems collapse at month-end.

DIMENSION TRANSACTION WORKLOAD READ-HEAVY WORKLOAD
Examples Create order, reserve stock, receive purchase, record payment. Dashboards, sales/inventory/purchase reports, searches, exports, analytics.
Volume Low — tens of writes per second at peak. Very high — the large majority of 5,000 RPS.
Shape Small, normalized, one record at a time. Wide aggregations over months of history.
Freshness Must be exact, immediately. Seconds or minutes of delay is fine.
Failure impact Lost sale, wrong stock, wrong money. A chart is late.

The staged approach — earn each step

  1. Stage 1 — one database is enoughEarly on, with modest data, a well-indexed transactional database serves both. Do not build a read model before you have a problem; complexity you don't need is complexity you still have to operate.TRIGGER TO MOVE ON: REPORTS START AFFECTING WRITE LATENCY
  2. Stage 2 — add read replicasA replica is a copy of the database kept in sync for reading. Point reports and list screens at replicas; keep writes on the primary. Cheap, invisible to the domain model, and often enough for years.LIMIT: REPLICAS STILL RUN EXPENSIVE QUERIES ON A NORMALIZED SCHEMA
  3. Stage 3 — add cachingCompany settings, product data, permissions and common lookups are read constantly and change rarely — cache them (Part 16) and a large fraction of read traffic never reaches any database.LIMIT: DOESN'T HELP BIG AGGREGATIONS
  4. Stage 4 — build a read modelPre-shaped tables built for the questions people actually ask ("daily sales per product per warehouse"), updated by workers from events. A dashboard becomes one small indexed read instead of scanning millions of order lines.TRIGGER: DASHBOARDS SLOW OR HEAVY ON THE PRIMARY
  5. Stage 5 — separate reporting infrastructureWhen reporting volume, retention or custom-query demands grow, move the read model to its own database/service so it can scale and fail independently of trading.TRIGGER: REPORTING LOAD OR OWNERSHIP DEMANDS INDEPENDENCE
THE PRINCIPLE

Separate the paths by their tolerance for being wrong. The write path must be exact and therefore stays small and protected. The read path may be slightly stale and therefore can be copied, cached, denormalized and scaled freely. Most enterprise performance problems are just this separation, missing.

PART 13 — EVENT-DRIVEN DESIGN

Announce What Happened; Let Others React

An event is a factual announcement that something already happened — past tense, immutable. The publisher does not know or care who listens, which is exactly what keeps the order path short while the business keeps adding requirements.

TWO RULES THAT MAKE EVENTS SAFE

1 · The outbox. Write the event into the same database transaction as the business change, then let a publisher push it to the broker. This removes the classic bug where the order commits but the event is lost (or the event is sent but the order rolls back).

2 · Idempotent consumers. Brokers deliver at least once, so every consumer must recognise an event id it has already processed and skip it. Repeat delivery then becomes harmless instead of a duplicate email or double stock deduction.

PART 14 — MICROSERVICES DECISION

What Leaves the Monolith, and in What Order

Extraction is a tool, not a milestone. Each candidate below states why extract, what problem it solves, what complexity it creates, and what must be true first. If the preconditions are not met, staying a module is the senior decision.

CANDIDATE WHY EXTRACT PROBLEM IT SOLVES COMPLEXITY IT CREATES MUST BE TRUE FIRST
Notification
EXTRACT 1ST		 No shared transactions, purely event-driven, spiky load, external vendors. Vendor slowness and email spikes stop consuming app resources; independent retry/scale. Low — one more deployable, one more dashboard. Delivery is already eventual. Events flowing reliably (outbox + broker) and per-tenant preferences modelled.
Reporting
EXTRACT 2ND		 Completely different workload — heavy reads, big scans, own scaling curve. Month-end reporting can never again slow order entry; scale reads independently. Medium — a second store to operate, data lag to explain, consistency questions from users. Read model already exists and is trusted; business has accepted "reports may lag seconds".
Payment
CONDITIONAL 		Compliance isolation (PCI scope), many per-tenant providers, different release cadence and risk profile. Shrinks the audit surface; payment changes ship without redeploying the platform. Medium-high — money across a network boundary demands idempotency keys, reconciliation and careful state machines. Idempotent payment interface and reconciliation job already working inside the monolith.
Inventory
LAST / RELUCTANT		 Contention is the dominant limit; warehouse/mobile traffic needs its own SLA and scaling. Stock operations scale and fail independently of order entry. Highest — order + reservation stops being one transaction and becomes a saga with compensation. This is a real risk of overselling if done carelessly. Measured contention that replicas/caching cannot fix, plus team capability to run sagas safely.

STAY AS MODULES (FOR A LONG TIME)

Company, User, Customer, Supplier, Product, Order, Purchase, Audit. They share transactions, they are read together constantly, and splitting them would turn simple joins into chatty network calls with no scaling benefit. The order module in particular should stay with anything it must commit atomically with.

THE EXTRACTION RULE

Extract when you can name the metric that hurts (report latency, queue backlog, deploy conflicts, compliance scope) and the boundary is already clean (no shared tables, only events and interfaces). If you cannot name both, you are not extracting a service — you are creating a distributed bug.

PART 15 — DATABASE DESIGN

Relational Core, Purposeful Copies

This domain is deeply relational — orders reference customers, products, warehouses and payments, and it all must balance. That makes SQL the right core, with additional stores added only where the workload genuinely differs.

DATA GROUP CHARACTER DESIGN NOTES
Company Tiny table, read on nearly every request. Cache aggressively; holds settings, limits, feature flags, cell/routing assignment.
User & membership Moderate size, read constantly for permissions. Global user identity + per-company membership and roles; permission sets cached with a version stamp.
Customer / Supplier Master data, moderate churn. Tenant-scoped indexes on name/code for lookup; balances updated transactionally.
Product Read-heavy master data, rarely written. Prime cache target; variants as child rows; never store quantities here.
Order + Order Items High-volume, append-mostly, queried by date/customer/status. Largest transactional tables. Composite indexes lead with CompanyId; consider partitioning by period as history grows; price snapshot stored on the line.
Purchase Similar shape to orders, lower volume. Links supplier, receipt status, and generated stock movements.
Inventory Small balance table + large append-only ledger. Balances: one row per (company, product, warehouse) — hot, tiny, heavily updated. Ledger: never updated, partitioned by period, ideal for archiving.
Payment Financial records, moderate volume, strict correctness. Unique constraint on idempotency key; provider reference stored; never store raw card data.
Audit Very high volume, append-only, read rarely. Separate store or separate tables with its own retention; must not compete with trading tables for space and I/O.

The three data homes

TRANSACTION DATABASE (SQL)Source of truth for everything above. ACID transactions, foreign keys, unique constraints per tenant. Primary handles writes; read replicas serve ordinary reads.
READ / REPORTING STOREDenormalized, pre-aggregated tables built for dashboards and reports, updated from events. Can be a separate database, a set of summary tables, or later a dedicated analytics store.
HISTORICAL / ARCHIVEOld orders, old ledger entries, old audit rows moved to cheaper storage with the same tenant keys, still queryable for compliance but out of the hot path.
OBJECT STORAGEImages, PDFs, imports and exports live as files; the database keeps only metadata and paths (Part 19).

The four operational levers

  • Indexing: every tenant-scoped index starts with CompanyId, then the query's real key (date, status, customer). Unique constraints are per tenant — order number 1001 exists in a thousand companies. Too many indexes slow writes, so index the queries you actually run.
  • Partitioning: splitting a huge table into smaller physical pieces — here by time (month/quarter) for orders, ledger and audit. Old partitions can be archived or dropped cheaply, and queries touching recent data stay fast.
  • Replication: keeping synchronized copies. Replicas serve reads and act as failover targets. Accept that replicas lag slightly — never read stock levels for a reservation decision from a replica.
  • Backup: automated full + incremental backups with point-in-time recovery, restores tested on a schedule, and a documented procedure for restoring a single tenant's data (harder in shared tables — plan the extraction script before you need it at 2 a.m.).
SQL VS NOSQL — SAY IT CLEANLY

"SQL for the core, because this domain is relational and needs transactions and constraints — stock and money must balance. NoSQL where the workload is genuinely different: an document/analytics store for reporting, an index engine for search, object storage for files. Choosing NoSQL for orders would mean rebuilding transactions and joins in application code, which is a downgrade dressed as modernization."

PART 16 — CACHING

Cache the Hot, Stable and Forgiving

A cache is a small fast copy of data you would otherwise fetch from the database (Redis is a common example). It absorbs a huge share of read traffic — but every cached value is a promise that slightly old data is acceptable.

WHAT TO CACHE TYPICAL LIFETIME WHY / HOW IT IS INVALIDATED
Company settings Minutes–hours Read on nearly every request, changed rarely. Invalidate immediately when an admin saves settings.
Product master data & price lists Minutes Huge read volume, low churn. Invalidate on product/price edit; version keys avoid stale price quotes.
User permissions Minutes (with version stamp) Checked on every request. Bump the user's permission version on role change so revocation is near-instant.
Dashboard tiles / report summaries Seconds–minutes Expensive to compute, tolerable when slightly old. Refresh in the background rather than on user request.
Master lookups (categories, units, tax codes, warehouses) Hours Practically static; invalidate on edit.

What must never depend on cache alone

  • Available stock for a reservation decision — always read and update the authoritative row inside the transaction. Cached stock is fine for display, never for committing.
  • Payment status and financial balances — money reads the ledger.
  • Tenant identity and access decisions — cache the permission set, but never let a cache miss or stale entry grant access it shouldn't.
  • Credit limit checks at the moment of approving an order.

The four questions every cache must answer

LIFETIME (TTL)Every key expires. Choose the time from how stale the data may be: seconds for dashboards, hours for tax codes. No expiry means silent wrongness forever.
INVALIDATIONWhen the underlying data changes, remove or replace the key immediately — usually driven by the same event that updated the database. TTL is the safety net, not the strategy.
CACHE FAILUREIf the cache is down, the system must still work by reading the database — with throttling so a cold cache does not stampede the database into a second outage. Slower is acceptable; broken is not.
STALENESS & TENANCYDecide per item what "slightly old" costs. And every key is prefixed with the company id — a shared cache is the easiest place in the whole platform to accidentally leak data between tenants.
THE MULTI-TENANT CACHE RULE

{companyId}:product:123 — never product:123. One unprefixed key in one code path serves Company A's price list to Company B. This is the most common real-world tenant leak, and it never shows up in normal testing because it only appears under concurrency.

PART 17 — REPORTING ARCHITECTURE

Reports Must Never Be Able to Stop Trading

A single "sales by product for the last two years" query can scan millions of rows, hold locks and saturate I/O. If it runs on the same database that reserves stock, one manager curious at month-end can stall order entry for everyone in the platform.

CONCERN HOW THE DESIGN HANDLES IT
Data delay Agree an explicit delay budget with the business per report type and show the "last updated" time on screen. Delay is only a problem when it is unexpected.
Reporting consistency Reports are eventually consistent; financial period reports run against closed periods so numbers never change after sign-off. Reconciliation jobs compare read model totals against source data and alert on drift.

PART 18 — BACKGROUND PROCESSING

Anything the User Should Not Wait For

Background work is how a system stays fast while doing a lot. The rule is simple: if the answer is not needed to complete the user's action, it does not belong in the request.

TASK WHY IT MUST NOT BLOCK THE REQUEST
Notifications (email, SMS, push) External providers are slow and fail. A hung SMTP call would hold the order transaction and its locks.
Report generation & exports A 200,000-row Excel export takes minutes; run it as a job and hand the user a file link when ready.
Read model updates Recomputing dashboard aggregates inside the order save would double or triple order latency for nobody's benefit.
Data synchronization (accounting, shipping, e-commerce) Third-party systems have their own outages and rate limits; syncing must retry patiently on its own schedule.
Bulk import (products, customers, opening stock) Uploading 50,000 rows must be validated and applied in chunks with a progress report — never in one HTTP request that times out halfway.
Scheduled jobs Nightly stock valuation, reservation expiry, low-stock scans, invoice reminders, archiving, reconciliation — time-driven, not user-driven.
Long calculations Period-end costing, ABC analysis, forecasting — minutes of CPU that belong on worker nodes, away from the API.

Retry and failure handling for background work

  1. Retry with backoff and jitter: wait longer between attempts (seconds → minutes) and add randomness so thousands of failed jobs don't retry in the same instant and finish off a recovering dependency.
  2. Bounded attempts, then quarantine: after N failures the message moves to a dead letter queue — a holding area for messages that cannot be processed — with an alert. Nothing is silently dropped, and one poison message cannot loop forever.
  3. Idempotent handlers: a job may run twice (retry, worker crash after completing but before acknowledging). Keying on the event id makes the second run a no-op rather than a duplicate email or double-counted report row.
  4. Visibility: queue depth, oldest-message age, failure rate and per-tenant job counts on a dashboard. A silently growing queue is the most common invisible outage in systems like this.
  5. User feedback: long jobs report status ("export ready", "import completed with 12 errors") so users are not left guessing whether their action worked.
  6. Fairness: separate queues (or per-tenant limits) so one company's 100,000-row import cannot starve everyone else's order confirmations — see Part 24.
PART 19 — FILE MANAGEMENT

Files in Object Storage, Facts in the Database

Product images, customer documents, invoices, import files and generated exports all need a home — and that home is not the transactional database.

CONCERN DESIGN
Where files live Object storage — a service built to hold large files cheaply and serve them directly. Paths are tenant-scoped: /tenant/{companyId}/products/{id}/image.jpg.
What lives in the database Only metadata: file id, tenant, owner record, original name, size, type, checksum, uploader, timestamp, storage path. Small rows, fast queries, small backups.
Why not blobs in the database Large binaries bloat tables and backups, wreck cache efficiency, slow restores, and make every schema migration heavier — for zero benefit, since files are never queried by content.
Secure access No public buckets. The application checks tenant and permission, then issues a short-lived signed link (a URL that expires and cannot be guessed). A leaked link dies in minutes and never grants access to other tenants' folders.
Large files & uploads Upload directly to storage (not through the API servers) with size/type limits, virus scanning on arrival, and resumable uploads for poor connections. Generated images get thumbnails so lists stay light.
Backup & lifecycle Versioning and replication for durability; lifecycle rules move old imports/exports to cheap cold storage or delete them; invoices follow the legal retention period; tenant offboarding deletes an entire prefix cleanly.
CONSISTENCY DETAIL WORTH MENTIONING

File storage and the database can disagree — an upload succeeds but the record fails, or the record is deleted while the file remains. Handle it with a scheduled orphan cleanup job comparing storage against metadata. Interviewers notice when you volunteer this; most candidates never think about it.

PART 20 — EXTERNAL INTEGRATIONS

Their Bad Day Must Not Become Our Outage

Payment, email, SMS, accounting and shipping providers are systems we do not control. Assume each will be slow, fail, duplicate, or return late — and design the boundary so the platform keeps trading regardless.

MECHANISM WHAT IT MEANS HOW WE APPLY IT
API communication Calls over the internet through a dedicated adapter per provider. One integration module per provider so swapping vendors touches one place; per-tenant credentials stored as secrets.
Timeout A maximum wait before giving up. Always set (a few seconds). An unbounded call is how one slow vendor consumes every application thread.
Retry with backoff Try again later, waiting longer each time. Only for transient errors and only for safe or idempotent operations; bounded attempts, then dead letter.
Duplicate protection (idempotency) Doing it twice has the same effect as once. Every payment/shipment request carries a stable key so a retry after a timeout can never charge or ship twice.
Circuit breaker After repeated failures, stop calling for a while and fail fast. Protects our threads and gives the provider room to recover; a half-open probe tests when to resume.
Fallback / degradation A defined behaviour when the provider is unavailable. Queue the work and inform the user ("invoice will be sent shortly"); allow credit orders when card payment is down; never block stock or order writes.
Monitoring Per-provider health visibility. Success rate, latency percentiles, timeout and retry counts, queue backlog, and alerts before customers notice.
THE PROTECTION PRINCIPLE

Every external call is asynchronous unless the user genuinely cannot proceed without the answer. Card authorization at checkout is synchronous (with a timeout and idempotency key). Accounting sync, invoice emails, shipping bookings and SMS are queued. That single rule keeps a provider outage as a delayed side effect instead of a stopped business.

THE TIMEOUT-AMBIGUITY CASE

A payment call times out — did it charge or not? Never assume failure and retry as a new transaction. Query the provider by the idempotency key, or wait for their callback, and reconcile. "Unknown" is a valid state that must exist in your payment model.

PART 21 — SECURITY ARCHITECTURE

Seven Layers Between Company A and Company B

In a multi-tenant platform, one leak is an existential event — contracts, reputation and possibly the company itself. Isolation therefore cannot rest on any single mechanism; it must survive one layer failing.

How we prevent Company A reading Company B's data

  1. Layer 1 — Trusted tenant contextThe company id comes only from the signed token established at login, never from a URL, header or request body. Any mismatch between request content and token is rejected and alerted as a probable attack.
  2. Layer 2 — Automatic query filteringA framework-level filter appends the tenant condition to every query on tenant-owned data. Isolation is the default behaviour; bypassing it requires an explicit, code-reviewed, logged exception used only by platform jobs.
  3. Layer 3 — Data model enforcementCompanyId leads every index and every unique constraint. Cross-tenant reads are not just forbidden, they are unnatural and slow — and a missing tenant predicate becomes visible in query plans.
  4. Layer 4 — Object-level authorizationEven inside the right tenant, every record access checks permission (a sales user cannot open a purchase document). Fetch-then-verify on every single-record endpoint, because ids are guessable.
  5. Layer 5 — Namespaced infrastructureCache keys, file paths, queue messages, search indexes, report models, exports and log entries all carry the tenant. The database is not the only place data can leak.
  6. Layer 6 — Continuous verificationAutomated tests attempt cross-tenant access on every endpoint in CI; static analysis flags raw queries that skip the filter; anomaly alerts fire when a user touches unusual volumes or a query runs without a tenant predicate.
  7. Layer 7 — Audit and blast-radius controlEvery sensitive read/write is audited with actor and tenant. Internal staff access is role-limited, time-boxed and logged. Tenants with contractual isolation requirements move to a dedicated database cell.

The rest of the security surface

AUTHENTICATIONStrong passwords, MFA, optional enterprise SSO, short-lived tokens with refresh, lockout on brute force, instant revocation.
AUTHORIZATIONDeny by default, central permission checks in the business layer, least privilege for both users and service accounts.
ENCRYPTIONTLS everywhere in transit; encryption at rest for database, backups and files; per-region keys where residency applies.
SECRETSProvider keys and connection strings in a managed secret store, rotated, never in source control or config files, scoped per environment and per tenant where relevant.
API PROTECTIONInput validation, output encoding, parameterized queries, file type/size limits, CORS rules, security headers, no internal ids or stack traces in error responses.
RATE LIMITINGPer tenant, per user and per endpoint — protecting both against abuse and against one company accidentally flooding the platform (Part 24).
AUDIT LOGGINGAppend-only records of logins, permission changes, price and stock edits, exports and admin actions — stored separately so an attacker cannot quietly rewrite them.
DATA PROTECTIONMinimal personal data, retention limits, export and delete capability per tenant, masked data in non-production environments, and no production data on developer machines.
SAY THIS ABOUT ISOLATION

"Tenant isolation is enforced in layers — token, automatic query filter, tenant-first indexes, object-level permission, namespaced cache/files/queues, automated cross-tenant tests, and audit. No single mistake in one layer can expose another company's data, and clients who require physical separation get their own database cell."

PART 22 — RELIABILITY AND FAILURE HANDLING

Sixteen Ways This Platform Breaks

Interviewers score seniority on the failure walk. Each scenario gets five columns: what happens, business impact, immediate response, recovery, prevention.

FAILURE WHAT HAPPENS / IMPACT IMMEDIATE RESPONSE RECOVERY PREVENTION
1 · Primary database unavailable Writes fail — no orders, no stock moves. Business stops. Highest severity. Automatic failover to standby; API returns clear "temporarily unavailable" instead of hanging; reads may continue from replicas in a degraded read-only mode. Promote replica, verify integrity, drain queued events, confirm no partial writes. HA cluster with automatic failover, tested regularly; connection pool limits; health checks; documented runbook.
2 · Read replica lag or failure Lists and reports show old data or error; trading unaffected. Route reads to the primary temporarily with throttling, or to another replica. Rebuild/resync the replica; monitor lag back to normal. Multiple replicas, lag alerts, never read stock-for-reservation from a replica.
3 · Cache (Redis) unavailable All reads fall to the database; latency spikes; risk of overload cascade. Fail open to the database with throttling and short-circuit limits; shed non-essential reads (dashboards) first. Restart/failover cache; warm critical keys gradually to avoid a thundering herd. Cache cluster with replication; load test with cold cache; never make cache mandatory for correctness.
4 · Message broker unavailable Events stop flowing: reports stale, emails unsent, integrations paused. Orders still work. Outbox keeps events safely in the database; publishing retries; users see stale report timestamps. Broker failover; publisher drains the outbox in order; consumers catch up idempotently. Clustered broker; outbox pattern so events are never lost; queue-age alerts.
5 · Payment provider unavailable Card payments fail at checkout; credit orders unaffected. Circuit breaker fails fast; offer alternate method or "pay later"; order is saved with payment pending — never lost. Queued payment attempts retry; reconciliation matches provider records. Timeouts, idempotency keys, secondary provider option, status polling + callbacks.
6 · Notification provider unavailable Emails/SMS delayed. Cosmetic — no business data at risk. Queue and retry with backoff; users still see confirmations in the app. Backlog drains when the vendor returns; dead letters reviewed. Never synchronous; multi-provider fallback for critical messages; backlog alerts.
7 · Reporting system unavailable Dashboards and reports down; trading continues normally. Show a clear message with last-known-good timestamps; keep events queued. Restart the read store; replay events; if corrupted, rebuild from the transactional source of truth. Separate infrastructure from trading, reconciliation checks, rebuild tooling that is actually tested.
8 · One tenant floods the system Shared resources saturate; other companies slow down. The multi-tenant nightmare. Per-tenant rate limits throttle the offender; heavy jobs moved to a lower-priority queue; autoscale. Investigate cause (bad integration loop, bulk import); adjust quota or move tenant to a dedicated cell. Per-tenant quotas, fair queuing, per-tenant metrics, contractual limits (Part 24).
9 · Background worker crashes Jobs stop silently — the dangerous kind of failure, because nothing looks broken. Health checks restart workers; unacknowledged messages return to the queue automatically. Workers resume from the queue; idempotent handlers make re-processing safe. Queue-depth and oldest-message alerts, worker heartbeats, multiple worker instances.
10 · Duplicate order request Double-click or mobile retry creates two identical orders and double stock reservation. Server recognises the client request id and returns the original order instead of creating a second. Any duplicate that slips through is cancelled and stock released. Idempotency key on create endpoints, unique constraint, UI submit lock.
11 · Duplicate payment request Customer charged twice — direct financial and trust damage. Idempotency key makes the second call return the first result; no second charge. If a duplicate occurred at the provider, automated refund and alert. Stable keys derived from order + amount, unique constraint, daily reconciliation.
12 · Inventory race condition Two users sell the last unit; stock goes negative; a customer is disappointed. Atomic conditional update means one succeeds, the other gets "insufficient stock". If negative stock appears, reconciliation from the ledger corrects it and alerts. Single-statement conditional decrement in one transaction, ledger, reconciliation job (Part 11).
13 · Network failure between components Timeouts and partial operations; user sees errors. Timeouts everywhere, retries for safe operations, circuit breakers; transactions roll back cleanly. Reconnect and replay from durable state; verify no half-applied work. Keep the critical transaction inside one process/database — the strongest reason for the modular monolith.
14 · API server failure Some requests fail mid-flight; capacity drops. Load balancer removes the unhealthy node; stateless design means users continue on other nodes. Replace the node automatically; in-flight transactions rolled back by the database. N+1 capacity, stateless servers, health checks, rolling deploys.
15 · Region failure Everything down for all tenants in that region. Worst case. Declare disaster, fail over to the secondary region, communicate honestly to tenants. Promote replicated database, redirect traffic, verify data to the last replicated point (RPO), then reconcile. Cross-region replication, tested failover drills, documented RPO/RTO (Part 25).
16 · Bad event / poison message A malformed event crashes a consumer repeatedly, blocking the queue behind it. After N attempts move it to the dead letter queue and continue processing others. Inspect, fix the handler or data, replay from the dead letter queue. Schema validation on publish, event versioning, DLQ monitoring and review routine.
THE PATTERN BEHIND ALL SIXTEEN

Detect (health checks, queue age, lag metrics) → contain (timeouts, circuit breakers, per-tenant limits) → degrade honestly (stale data labelled, "payment pending" rather than silent failure) → recover (failover, replay, reconcile) → prevent (redundancy, idempotency, drills). Notice how many rows end with "orders still work" — that is the architecture doing its job.

PART 23 — SCALING

Grow by Adding, Never by Rewriting

Horizontal scaling means adding more machines rather than buying a bigger one. Each layer below scales differently, and knowing which lever fits which pain is the whole skill.

LAYER HOW IT SCALES TRIGGER
API layer Add stateless nodes behind the load balancer; autoscale on CPU and latency. The easy one. p95 latency or CPU threshold.
Business modules Scale with the application today; extract and scale independently once a module's load curve clearly diverges (Part 14). One module dominating resources.
Database (reads) Read replicas plus caching absorb the majority of traffic. Read latency, primary CPU.
Database (writes) Vertical scaling first (simplest), then archiving/partitioning to keep hot tables small, then tenant sharding — splitting tenants across database cells. Write latency, lock waits, table size.
Reporting Scale the read store separately; pre-aggregate more; move heavy exports to off-peak batch jobs. Report latency, reporting CPU.
Cache Cluster and shard by key; add memory; tune what deserves caching. Hit ratio falling, evictions rising, memory pressure.
Message processing Add worker instances per queue; partition queues by workload and tenant priority. Queue depth and oldest-message age.
File storage Object storage scales itself; add CDN for frequently downloaded images. Bandwidth cost, download latency.

Tenant-based scaling: the multi-tenant superpower

Because every row carries a company id, tenants can be distributed across cells — independent groups of infrastructure each serving a subset of companies. Cell 1 serves companies 1–3,000, cell 2 serves 3,001–6,000, and a very large client gets a cell of their own. A tenant routing registry maps company → cell, so application code never changes. This gives near-linear growth, smaller blast radius per incident, and a clean answer to "one enormous tenant".

WHEN ONE VERY LARGE COMPANY BECOMES EVERYONE'S PROBLEM

A single tenant with 40% of the orders, a 500,000-row catalogue and hourly full exports will: dominate database I/O and cache memory, make shared tables skewed (their rows crowd out others in every index), fill shared queues with their jobs, and turn their month-end into a platform-wide slowdown. The answer is isolation, not more hardware — per-tenant quotas first, then their own database cell, ideally priced into their contract as a premium tier. Detecting this early requires per-tenant metrics, which is exactly why Part 26 insists on them.

PART 24 — THE NOISY TENANT PROBLEM

"One Company Just Sent 10× Their Normal Traffic"

In a shared platform, one tenant's bad Tuesday is everyone's bad Tuesday — unless the architecture actively prevents it. This is the defining operational risk of multi-tenancy, and interviewers love it because it has no purely technical answer.

Why does it happen? A broken integration loop retrying forever, a bulk import of 200,000 products, a customer wiring your API into their own system, a seasonal sale, or simply a company that grew ten times bigger than when they signed up. None of these are attacks — which is why blocking is the wrong instinct and fair sharing is the right one.

PROTECTION HOW IT WORKS
Rate limiting Per-tenant (and per-user) request limits at the API layer, with generous normal limits and clear error responses. Stops runaway loops in seconds rather than hours.
Resource isolation Separate connection-pool budgets, worker capacity and memory quotas so a single tenant cannot consume everything. Bulkheads: one flooded compartment does not sink the ship.
Separate / prioritized queues Bulk imports and exports go to a low-priority queue; order confirmations and payment events to a high-priority one. Round-robin across tenants so no company's backlog blocks another's.
Tenant limits & plans Documented quotas per subscription tier (API calls/hour, export size, import rows, concurrent jobs). Limits are a product feature, not a hidden punishment.
Scaling & shedding Autoscale for genuine growth; shed or defer non-essential work (report refreshes, analytics) before touching order processing.
Monitoring per tenant Requests, queue jobs, database time and storage attributed per company, with alerts on sudden deviation from a tenant's own baseline.
Escalation path Throttle → contact the customer → raise their plan → move them to a dedicated cell. A business conversation, backed by data, not a silent block.
WHY THIS MATTERS SO MUCH

Every tenant signed a contract expecting the platform to behave as if it were theirs. Without fairness mechanisms, your best-growing customer becomes the reason your other 9,999 customers complain — and the failure is invisible until it is severe. Fair resource usage is not an optimization; in multi-tenant systems it is the architecture.

PART 25 — DISASTER RECOVERY

Planning for the Day Something Big Is Lost

Backups are not a strategy; tested restores are. Two numbers drive everything, and the business — not the architect — must choose them.

RPO — RECOVERY POINT OBJECTIVEHow much data may we lose, measured in time. "RPO = 5 minutes" means after a disaster we may lose up to the last five minutes of work. Smaller RPO = more frequent/continuous replication = more cost.
RTO — RECOVERY TIME OBJECTIVEHow long we may be down before service returns. "RTO = 1 hour" means everything must be running again within an hour. Smaller RTO = warmer standby infrastructure = more cost.
BACKUPDaily full plus frequent incremental backups with point-in-time recovery, stored in a different location from the primary system, encrypted, with defined retention.
RESTOREThe part everyone skips: scheduled restore rehearsals into an isolated environment, timed and documented, plus a tested procedure for restoring a single tenant.

A realistic recovery strategy

SCENARIO TARGET APPROACH
Single server/node lost RPO 0 · RTO seconds Redundant stateless nodes; load balancer removes the failed one automatically. No human involved.
Database primary lost RPO ~0 · RTO minutes Synchronous standby in another availability zone; automatic promotion; application reconnects.
Data corruption / bad deploy RPO minutes · RTO 1–2 hours Point-in-time restore to just before the incident; replay events where possible; this is why backups matter even with replication (replicas faithfully copy corruption).
Single tenant data loss RPO minutes · RTO hours Restore a copy to a staging environment, extract that company's rows by CompanyId, re-import. Rehearse this — shared tables make it non-trivial.
Whole region lost RPO ~5–15 min · RTO 2–4 hours Asynchronous cross-region replication with infrastructure defined as code; declare disaster, promote the secondary region, redirect traffic, reconcile the gap, and communicate to tenants.
THE COST / SPEED / COMPLEXITY TRIANGLE

Zero data loss and instant recovery means a fully active second region — often double the infrastructure bill plus permanent operational complexity (conflict handling, dual deployments, drift). An hour of downtime and five minutes of data loss might cost this business far less than that. Ask what an hour of downtime costs, then buy exactly the recovery the business is willing to pay for — and write it down so nobody is surprised on the worst day.

PART 26 — OBSERVABILITY

You Cannot Operate What You Cannot See

Three kinds of signal answer three different questions — and in a multi-tenant platform every one of them must be sliceable by company.

LOGS — WHAT HAPPENED?Structured records of events and errors, each tagged with tenant, user, correlation id and module. Never log sensitive data. Used for investigating a specific incident.
METRICS — HOW MUCH / HOW OFTEN?Numbers over time: request rates, latency percentiles, error rates, queue depth, cache hit ratio. Used for alerting and trend analysis.
TRACES — WHERE DID TIME GO?The path of one request through the system with timing per step. Answers "why was this order save slow?" — usually the fastest route to a real fix.
PER-TENANT DIMENSIONUnique to multi-tenancy: every signal carries the company id, so you can answer "is this slow for everyone or just this company?" — the first question in almost every support call.
WHAT TO MONITOR WHY IT MATTERS / WHEN TO ALERT
API response time (p95/p99) The user's felt experience. Alert when order-create p95 exceeds its target — the single most business-relevant latency in the platform.
Error rate By endpoint and by tenant. A spike in one tenant points to their integration; a spike everywhere points to us.
Database load CPU, connections, slow queries, lock waits, replica lag. Lock waits rising is the early warning for inventory contention.
Cache health Hit ratio, evictions, connection errors. A falling hit ratio predicts a database overload before it happens.
Queue size & oldest message age Age matters more than depth. A quietly ageing queue means users are not getting emails and reports are stale while nothing looks "down".
Worker failures & dead letters Any dead letter arrival should be visible; repeated failures on the same handler signal a code or data problem.
Per-tenant traffic & resource usage Detects the noisy tenant early and supports fair-usage conversations with evidence.
Payment failures Direct revenue signal. Alert on failure-rate deviation, not just absolute numbers.
Inventory anomalies Negative stock, reservation-vs-balance mismatches, reconciliation differences — correctness alarms, not performance ones.
Business KPIs Orders per minute is the best outage detector you will ever have: if it drops to zero and no technical alarm fired, your monitoring has a gap.
ALERT DISCIPLINE

Page a human only for customer-visible or money-losing conditions: order failures, database down, payment failure spike, queue age beyond threshold, negative stock. Everything else is a dashboard or a ticket. Alert fatigue is a reliability risk — a team that ignores pages will ignore the real one.

PART 27 — ARCHITECTURE TRADE-OFFS

Fifteen Decisions, Fully Defended

Each block states the problem, both options, the decision, the reasoning, the risk we accept, and when we would revisit. That last column is what separates an architect from someone with opinions.

D1 Monolith vs Modular Monolith vs Microservices

OPTION A — MICROSERVICES NOWIndependent deploy/scale per area; heavy operations, distributed transactions for order+stock.
OPTION B — MODULAR MONOLITHOne deployable, hard module boundaries; simple ops, atomic transactions, coarse scaling.
ADVANTAGES (B)Atomic order+stock, small ops burden, fast delivery, boundaries preserved for later.
DISADVANTAGES (B)Whole app scales together; one release train; a bad module can affect the process.
DECISION & WHYModular monolith. Our pain is reporting load and a small ops team, not independent deployment. Microservices would trade a solved consistency problem for an unsolved distributed one. RISKBoundaries erode under deadline pressure, leaving a traditional monolith. Mitigate with enforced module rules in CI and architecture tests. REVISITWhen deploy conflicts, divergent scaling or team count make independence cheaper than coupling.

D2 Shared database vs separate database per tenant

OPTION A — SHAREDOne database for all tenants; cheapest, one migration, logical isolation only.
OPTION B — PER TENANT10,000 databases; physical isolation, easy per-tenant restore, brutal operations.
ADVANTAGES (A)Low cost, simple releases, efficient resource use, easy onboarding of new tenants.
DISADVANTAGES (A)A code bug can leak data; single-tenant restore is awkward; noisy tenants share resources.
DECISION & WHYShared database with mandatory TenantId, plus a tenant→cell routing registry so any tenant can be moved to dedicated infrastructure without code changes. 10,000 databases is not operable by our team. RISKLogical isolation depends on automatic enforcement; a bypass leaks data. Mitigate with framework filters, tenant-first indexes, cross-tenant tests and audits. REVISITWhen a client contractually requires physical separation, or a tenant outgrows the shared pool.

D3 Shared tables vs schema per company

OPTION A — SHARED TABLESTenantId column; one set of tables; simplest migrations and pooling.
OPTION B — SCHEMA PER COMPANYNamespace per tenant; better isolation and per-tenant export; thousands of migrations per release.
ADVANTAGES (A)One migration, efficient connections and caches, easy cross-tenant platform analytics.
DISADVANTAGES (A)Very large tables; per-tenant operations harder; isolation purely logical.
DECISION & WHYShared tables. At 10,000 tenants, schema-per-tenant makes every release a multi-thousand-step operation with guaranteed version drift — a reliability risk larger than the isolation benefit it buys. RISKIndex skew from very large tenants; noisy neighbours in the same tables. REVISITIf tenant count stays low (hundreds) and per-tenant customization or export becomes a core product requirement.

D4 SQL vs NoSQL for the core

OPTION A — RELATIONALACID transactions, joins, constraints; scaling writes needs effort.
OPTION B — NOSQLEasy horizontal writes; no cross-document transactions or joins.
ADVANTAGES (A)Order+stock+payment consistency in one transaction; referential integrity; mature reporting tooling.
DISADVANTAGES (A)Write scaling eventually needs partitioning or cells; schema changes need care at scale.
DECISION & WHYSQL for the core; NoSQL/object/index stores only for genuinely different workloads (reporting, files, search). Our write volume is modest and our correctness needs are absolute — exactly SQL's strength. RISKSingle primary is a write bottleneck at extreme growth. REVISITIf write throughput or table sizes exceed what partitioning and cells can handle.

D5 Cache vs direct database access

OPTION A — ALWAYS DATABASEAlways fresh; database pays for every repeated read.
OPTION B — CACHE HOT DATASub-millisecond reads, big load reduction; staleness and invalidation to manage.
ADVANTAGES (B)Removes a large share of the 5,000 RPS; protects the database during spikes; cheaper than scaling the database.
DISADVANTAGES (B)Stale data risk, invalidation bugs, another component to run, tenant-key discipline required.
DECISION & WHYCache hot, stable, forgiving data (settings, products, permissions, dashboard tiles) with TTL + event invalidation. Never cache stock for reservation decisions or financial balances. RISKCache outage causes a database load spike; an unprefixed key leaks across tenants. REVISITContinuously — review what is cached whenever hit ratio drops or stale-data complaints appear.

D6 Synchronous vs asynchronous processing

OPTION A — DO EVERYTHING IN THE REQUESTSimple to follow; every dependency's slowness becomes the user's slowness.
OPTION B — ASYNC FOR NON-ESSENTIALSFast responses and resilience; eventual side effects and more moving parts.
ADVANTAGES (B)Order save stays fast; vendor outages don't fail orders; spikes are absorbed by queues.
DISADVANTAGES (B)Users must understand "email is coming"; debugging spans systems; needs idempotency.
DECISION & WHYSynchronous only for what the user needs to complete the action (validation, stock reservation, order save, card authorization). Everything else is queued. RISKSilent backlog if queues are not monitored. REVISITPer feature — if users demand instant reporting, revisit that specific path, not the principle.

D7 Direct in-process calls vs events between modules

OPTION A — DIRECT CALLSImmediate, easy to trace, transactional; creates coupling.
OPTION B — EVENTSDecoupled, extensible, retriable; eventual and harder to follow end-to-end.
ADVANTAGES (MIX)Transactional correctness where required; freedom to add consumers without touching the order path.
DISADVANTAGES (MIX)Two mental models in one codebase; discipline needed about which to use.
DECISION & WHYDirect calls inside a transaction (order → inventory reservation); events for everything after commit, published via the outbox so they can never diverge from the data. RISKTeams over-use events and lose traceability; mitigate with correlation ids and tracing. REVISITWhen a module is extracted — its direct calls must become contracts or events.

D8 One database for reports vs a separate reporting model

OPTION A — REPORT FROM THE TRANSACTION DBAlways current, no extra infrastructure; heavy queries hurt trading.
OPTION B — SEPARATE READ MODELFast, isolated, scalable; data is slightly delayed and must be maintained.
ADVANTAGES (B)Month-end cannot stall order entry; dashboards become trivial reads; reporting scales alone.
DISADVANTAGES (B)Eventual consistency to explain; pipeline to operate; rebuild tooling needed.
DECISION & WHYSeparate read model, built up in stages (replicas → cache → shaped tables). This business explicitly has heavy dashboards and reporting; it is the highest-value structural decision after tenancy. RISKUsers mistrust delayed numbers; drift between model and source. REVISITShow "updated at" timestamps and run reconciliation; if delay becomes unacceptable for a report, serve that one from the primary.

D9 Shared infrastructure vs isolated tenant resources

OPTION A — FULLY SHAREDBest efficiency and cost; noisy tenants and shared blast radius.
OPTION B — ISOLATED PER TENANTPredictable performance and strong isolation; multiplies cost and operations.
ADVANTAGES (HYBRID)Cheap for the 9,900 normal tenants, isolated for the few who need or pay for it.
DISADVANTAGES (HYBRID)Two deployment paths to support and automate.
DECISION & WHYShared by default with quotas and fair queuing; dedicated cells for whales and contractually isolated clients — priced as a premium tier so the cost lands where the demand comes from. RISKCell sprawl and configuration drift. REVISITWhen more than a handful of tenants need cells — then invest in automation before adding more.

D10 Centralized vs distributed (multi-region) deployment

OPTION A — ONE REGIONSimple, cheap, strongly consistent; regional outage is total, distant users see latency.
OPTION B — MULTI-REGIONResilience, locality, residency compliance; replication complexity and double cost.
ADVANTAGES (A)Matches a small ops team; no data-sync complexity; lowest cost.
DISADVANTAGES (A)Regional failure means hours of downtime; no residency story.
DECISION & WHYSingle region with multi-zone redundancy plus cross-region backups/replication for disaster recovery. Buy full multi-region only when downtime cost or residency law justifies it. RISKRTO of hours in a regional disaster — accepted explicitly by the business. REVISITOn entering a regulated market or signing clients with stricter availability terms.

D11 Build vs use external services

OPTION A — BUILDFull control, no per-transaction fees; you own compliance, reliability and maintenance forever.
OPTION B — BUYFast, compliant, maintained; fees and vendor dependency.
ADVANTAGES (B)Payment providers handle card compliance; email/SMS vendors handle deliverability — neither is our business.
DISADVANTAGES (B)Recurring cost, outages outside our control, lock-in risk.
DECISION & WHYBuy payment, email, SMS and object storage; build inventory, ordering and reporting logic — that domain is the product. Wrap every vendor in our own adapter so switching costs stay low. RISKVendor outage or price rise. REVISITWhen volume makes fees exceed the cost of building, or a vendor's reliability breaches our SLA.

D12 Immediate vs delayed report data

OPTION A — LIVE NUMBERSAlways current; expensive queries on the transactional database.
OPTION B — DELAYED (SECONDS/MINUTES)Cheap and fast; users must accept a lag.
ADVANTAGES (B)Dashboards cost almost nothing to serve and cannot harm trading.
DISADVANTAGES (B)"My order isn't in the report yet" support calls.
DECISION & WHYDelayed by default with visible "updated at" labels; a small number of operational screens (today's open orders, current stock) read live data because the business genuinely needs them exact. RISKUsers compare a live screen with a delayed report and lose trust. REVISITPer report, when a business case proves the delay causes real harm.

D13 Single region vs multiple regions for tenants

OPTION A — ALL TENANTS IN ONE REGIONSimple operations; latency for far-away tenants; no residency support.
OPTION B — TENANTS PLACED BY REGIONLocal performance and compliance; multiple stacks and a routing layer.
ADVANTAGES (B)Meets data residency law; better latency; failure contained to one region's tenants.
DISADVANTAGES (B)Region-by-region releases, more monitoring surface, higher cost.
DECISION & WHYStart single-region, but make region an attribute of the tenant routing registry from day one so placing a tenant elsewhere later is configuration rather than re-architecture. RISKCross-region tenants (a company operating in two markets) need a defined home region. REVISITFirst residency requirement or first large distant-market client.

D14 Shared queue vs tenant-specific queues

OPTION A — ONE SHARED QUEUESimple and efficient; one tenant's bulk job delays everyone.
OPTION B — QUEUE PER TENANTPerfect fairness; 10,000 queues is unmanageable.
ADVANTAGES (MIDDLE)Queues split by workload class plus per-tenant concurrency limits give fairness without queue sprawl.
DISADVANTAGES (MIDDLE)Scheduling logic to build and tune; more dashboards.
DECISION & WHYPriority queues by work type (critical events vs bulk imports/exports) with round-robin and per-tenant concurrency caps. Dedicated queues only for cell-isolated tenants. RISKMis-tuned limits starve legitimate large jobs. REVISITWhen per-tenant queue-wait metrics show unfairness.

D15 One large application vs independently scalable areas

OPTION A — SCALE THE WHOLE APPSimple: add identical nodes; you scale everything to fix one hot spot.
OPTION B — SCALE AREAS SEPARATELYEfficient targeting; requires separate deployables.
ADVANTAGES (PRAGMATIC MIX)Same codebase deployed in different roles — API nodes, worker nodes, reporting nodes — scaled independently without splitting the code.
DISADVANTAGES (MIX)Some wasted memory per node; role configuration to manage.
DECISION & WHYDeploy one artifact in three roles and scale each role on its own metric. This captures most of the independent-scaling benefit at almost none of the microservice cost — the best value decision in the whole design. RISKA memory leak or bad release affects all roles. REVISITWhen one role's resource profile diverges so much that a true service extraction pays for itself.
PART 28 — COST THINKING

The Best Architecture You Cannot Afford Is a Bad Architecture

Architects spend company money. In a multi-tenant platform the number that matters is cost per tenant per month — because it decides whether the product's pricing works at all.

COST AREA WHAT DRIVES IT HOW TO CONTROL IT
Compute API nodes, workers, reporting nodes, autoscaling headroom, non-production environments. Right-size instances, autoscale down at night, shut off idle test environments, reserved capacity for the stable baseline.
Database Usually the largest line: primary, standby, replicas, IOPS, storage growth. Archive old data, index only what is queried, use the cache to avoid a bigger primary, add replicas only when measured.
Cache Memory size and cluster nodes. Cache what is genuinely hot; short TTLs on large objects; monitor hit ratio to justify every gigabyte.
Message broker Throughput, retention, cluster size. Modest retention for processed events, right-sized cluster, avoid publishing events nobody consumes.
Storage Files, backups, archives, audit history growing forever. Lifecycle rules to cold tiers, compress exports, enforce retention, delete offboarded tenants' data.
Network Data transfer, cross-zone/region traffic, file downloads. CDN for images, keep chatty components in one zone, compress payloads, avoid unnecessary cross-region replication.
Monitoring Log volume and metric cardinality — quietly one of the biggest surprise bills. Sample verbose logs, shorten retention for debug data, avoid high-cardinality labels, keep full fidelity only on money paths.
Backup Frequency, retention, cross-region copies. Match retention to legal need, tier older backups, verify restores rather than keeping more copies "just in case".
External providers Per-transaction payment fees, SMS (expensive), email volume. Prefer email/push over SMS, batch digests instead of per-event messages, negotiate volume rates, let tenants use their own providers.

When the technically better architecture is financially worse

  • Microservices for 5,000 RPS. Technically elegant isolation; financially, you multiply infrastructure, tracing, pipelines and on-call time to solve problems you do not have. For this business it is a large permanent bill for a hypothetical benefit.
  • A database per tenant. Beautiful isolation; 10,000 databases means idle capacity in thousands of them plus enormous operational labour — likely more than the revenue of the smaller tenants.
  • Full active-active multi-region. Excellent availability; roughly double infrastructure forever plus conflict-resolution complexity — to protect against an event that may cost less than the annual premium.
  • Event sourcing everywhere. Perfect auditability; far more storage, more complex queries and a steeper learning curve, when an audit table and a movement ledger already answer every real business question here.
  • Real-time streaming analytics. Impressive dashboards; a streaming platform to run and tune, when managers happily accept numbers that are one minute old.
  • Maximum observability. Full-fidelity logs and traces for everything can rival compute costs; sampling keeps 95% of the insight for a fraction of the price.
THE COST SENTENCE FOR INTERVIEWS

"I track cost per tenant per month and I protect four things from every cost cut: data durability, tenant isolation, transactional correctness, and the monitoring that tells me when those three are failing. Everything else — report freshness, log retention, extra replicas, spare capacity — is negotiable."

No read-then-write gap exists. The ledger records the winner, and reconciliation would catch any drift.
  9. "Numbers must be live": some genuinely must — current stock and today's open orders read live data. Historical dashboards and analytics do not, and users usually accept seconds of delay once we show an "updated at" timestamp. The alternative is honest: live aggregation over years of history means month-end reports can lock the tables that take orders. We separate by report, not by ideology.
  10. Why not microservices: they solve independent deployment, independent scaling and team autonomy. We have one team, 5,000 RPS and an order+stock transaction that must be atomic. Splitting Inventory from Order converts a database transaction into a saga with compensation — real oversell risk. We keep the boundaries (the valuable part) without the distribution (the expensive part), and we have written triggers for when that changes.
  11. First extraction and proof: Notification — no shared transactions, spiky load, harmless failure — so it validates our event and deployment machinery at low risk. Proof it was right: notification spikes no longer affect API latency, its deploys are independent, and no order has ever failed because of it. Reporting follows once the read model is trusted.
  12. Payment succeeds, order fails: the money fact is recorded the moment the provider confirms, with its reference and idempotency key, independently of order completion. Order completion is then driven by the PaymentCompleted event with retries. If it truly cannot complete — stock genuinely gone — we issue a compensating refund and notify the customer. Daily reconciliation against provider settlements catches anything stuck as "unknown".
  13. Database unavailable: users see a clear "temporarily unavailable, your work is not lost" message rather than a hang; reads may continue in a degraded read-only mode from replicas. First ten minutes: confirm the failover fired (automatic promotion of the standby), verify integrity and replication position, check for partial writes, communicate to tenants, then drain queued events. The runbook exists and has been rehearsed.
  14. Regional outage: declare disaster, promote the cross-region replica, redirect traffic, reconcile the replication gap. We lose up to our stated RPO — roughly 5–15 minutes of data — and return within 2–4 hours. That target was chosen by the business against the cost of an always-warm second region; it is written down, and we drill it so the number is real rather than aspirational.
  15. 30% budget cut: archive history to cold storage, remove unused indexes and one replica, sample logs and shorten debug retention, reserved instances for baseline, aggressive off-peak autoscaling, non-production environments off at night, CDN for images, email instead of SMS. Trade-offs stated openly: slower historical reports, shorter log history. Untouchable: backups, isolation controls, transactional correctness, and the monitoring that proves them.
  16. 5× growth and first breaking point: the primary database write path and the largest tenant's skew break first. Sequence: index and query hygiene, cache, replicas, archive and partition, then tenant cells via the routing registry, then extract Reporting. 5× more tenants is easy; 5× bigger tenants is the hard case, and cells are the answer to it.
  17. What would you remove: if forced, the separate read model at the very beginning — start with replicas plus caching and add the read model when reporting genuinely hurts. Also start with a single queue and add priority queues when unfairness is measured. The things I would never remove are tenant enforcement, the outbox, and monitoring.
  18. Preventing a ball of mud: automated architecture tests in CI that fail the build on cross-module table access or forbidden references; module ownership with named maintainers; public interfaces and events as the only allowed inter-module contract; regular boundary reviews; and a standing rule that "temporary" cross-module shortcuts need an architecture decision record. Boundaries survive only if a machine enforces them.
  19. 30-day tenant deletion: yes, but it must be designed — a maintained inventory of every tenant-owned table, cache namespace, file prefix, search index and read-model table; a verified export; suspend-then-hard-delete with a grace period; legal retention honoured for financial records; backups aged out on schedule; the whole operation audited. This is the one area where shared tables cost more than database-per-tenant, and we accept it.
  20. Zero-downtime schema change: expand-migrate-contract — add nullable structures without locking, deploy code that writes both and reads old, backfill in batches off-peak, switch reads, remove the old shape in a later release. Feature-flag the rollout, test timing against production-sized data, and never run a long-locking operation on a huge shared table in business hours. Shared tables mean this is one migration, not ten thousand.
  21. Live e-commerce inventory sync: do not let them poll the transactional database. Publish InventoryUpdated events and push deltas to their system, or expose a rate-limited read API served from the read model with an "as of" timestamp. Contractual quota, their own queue, and a circuit breaker so their outage cannot back-pressure us. If they need true real-time, that is a premium integration with a dedicated cell.
  22. Investigating wrong stock: the movement ledger makes this answerable — replay every movement for that product and warehouse, compare the computed balance with the stored balance, and check for stuck reservations, failed events in the dead letter queue, or a manual adjustment in the audit log. Reconciliation should already have alerted us. Without an append-only ledger, this conversation has no factual basis at all.
  23. Trusting 99.9%: it comes from redundancy at every layer with no single point of failure in the request path, measured historical availability, and the failure scenarios we have actually rehearsed. It excludes what we have chosen not to buy — regional disaster recovery takes hours, which is why a regional outage would breach the number. I would rather state that honestly than promise four nines we have not funded.
  24. One engineer on call: reduce moving parts and improve signal — fewer queues, fewer optional components, automated failover everywhere, aggressive runbooks, alerting only on customer-visible conditions, and self-healing defaults (retries, DLQ, reservation expiry, reconciliation). This is also the strongest argument for the modular monolith: one deployable is one thing to reason about at 3 a.m.
  25. Biggest risk and first action: logical tenant isolation — one leak is existential. First action is not more code but proof: automated cross-tenant tests across every endpoint in CI, runtime detection of unfiltered queries, and a scheduled third-party review. Second risk is boundary erosion, addressed by architecture tests; third is silent background failure, addressed by queue-age alerts.

ONE POWERFUL ARCHITECT STATEMENT

In a shared enterprise platform, the architecture earns its keep by holding four walls at once — every tenant sealed, every transaction exact, every load absorbed, every failure survivable — and by being simple enough that the team can still operate it at three in the morning.

Isolation, correctness, scalability and reliability are the promises. Unnecessary operational complexity is the tax that quietly breaks all four. The architect's job is buying the promises without paying the tax.
