Section 2 — Interviewer Instructions

Read before the interview starts.Time: pre-read, 5 min

How to conduct this interview

  1. Ask the primary question exactly as written. Do not paraphrase into an easier version.
  2. Allow silence. Give 10–20 seconds of thinking time before helping. Senior candidates structure before speaking.
  3. Ask the follow-ups. The primary question separates nobody; the follow-ups separate everybody.
  4. Challenge assumptions. Even when the answer is correct, push: "What if that assumption is wrong?"
  5. Ask "Why?" at least twice per topic. Ask "Why not the alternative?" once.
  6. Demand real examples. "Give me a specific system you did this on, and what the numbers were."
  7. Force trade-offs. An architect never answers with one option; they answer with a chosen option plus the cost of choosing it.
  8. Ask what could fail. Production thinking is the strongest architect signal.
  9. Ask how success would be measured. Metrics, SLOs, business KPIs, cost.
  10. Score only after the discussion. Never score during the answer; you will anchor on delivery, not substance.

Do NOT reward

  • Buzzwords ("we used CQRS, event-driven, cloud-native")
  • Technology name-dropping without decision reasoning
  • Memorised textbook definitions
  • Long answers with no conclusion
  • "We" answers that never become "I"
  • Fake or unverifiable experience
  • Blaming previous teams / management for everything
  • Confident wrongness that never self-corrects

DO reward

  • Clear structured reasoning under ambiguity
  • Correct fundamentals (they never go out of date)
  • Concrete production experience with numbers
  • Explicit trade-off statements and reversibility thinking
  • Ownership of failure and of the fix
  • Business/cost awareness
  • Operability: logs, metrics, traces, alerts, rollbacks
  • Communication: can explain to a junior AND to a CFO

The five levels of demonstration (used in every rubric)

Level Meaning Typical signal Maps to
1. Knowing Can explain the concept "Redis is an in-memory key-value store" Mid-level
2. Applying Has used it in a real situation "We cached the pricing lookup with a 5-minute TTL" Senior
3. Designing Can build a solution around it "Cache-aside + stampede protection + invalidation on outbox event" Senior+/Lead
4. Defending Can justify vs. alternatives, under attack "We rejected write-through because…, and here is the cost model" Architect
5. Leading Can align others, manage trade-offs, own the outcome "I ran the ADR review, got infra buy-in, we measured X after 60 days" Architect / Solution Architect

Rule: A Technical Architect must demonstrate all five. Knowing many technologies is not architecture. If the candidate never gets past level 3 across the whole interview, they are a strong Senior/Lead — not an Architect.

Scoring scale (use consistently everywhere)

Score Band Definition
1–3 Weak Incorrect, absent, or fundamentally confused. Cannot be coached quickly.
4–5 Below level Partially correct, textbook only, no real application, needs prompting throughout.
6 Acceptable Correct with prompting. Meets senior engineer bar, not lead/architect bar.
7 Good Correct, applied, one real example, some trade-off awareness.
8 Strong Correct + designed + measured. Handles follow-ups without help.
9 Very strong Defends against challenge with evidence; discusses cost and failure modes unprompted.
10 Architect-level Taught the interviewer something; org-level thinking; owns outcomes and people.
Calibration anchor: a strong senior engineer with 12 years should average 7. An architect-ready candidate averages 8+ with System Design and Architecture ≥ 8. Do not award 9–10 for fluency alone.

Panel etiquette

  • Only one interviewer drives at a time; the second takes notes in the note fields.
  • Never reveal an <details> panel while the candidate can see the screen. Share only the question area if screen-sharing.
  • Give the candidate a whiteboard/diagram tool for §14 and §25.
  • Announce timings: "We have 25 minutes for this design; I'll interrupt to keep us moving."
  • Reserve the last 5 minutes for the candidate's questions — their questions are themselves a signal (score in §24).
  • Debrief and finalise scoring within 30 minutes of the interview ending, while evidence is fresh.
Interviewer Only — Red flags that should end the Architect consideration (not necessarily the interview)
  • Cannot explain a decision they personally claim to have made.
  • Every architecture answer is "microservices + Kubernetes + Kafka" regardless of the problem.
  • No awareness of cost, licensing, headcount, or operational burden.
  • Cannot name a single thing that went wrong in 12 years, or blames only others.
  • Cannot read/critique code — "I don't code anymore" at 12 YOE is a lead/architect disqualifier in most product orgs.
  • Treats non-functional requirements (latency, RTO/RPO, compliance) as someone else's job.
  • Hostile or dismissive when challenged in §26.

Section 3 — Introduction & Career Discussion

Target: 10 minutesScoring category: Behavioral / CommunicationGoal: establish the truth of the CV and set the depth of the rest of the interview

Open with: "This is a long interview — roughly 3.5 hours with one break. I will interrupt you sometimes to keep us on time; that is not a negative signal. I will also disagree with you deliberately at points — I want to see how you reason, not whether you agree with me."

3.1Tell me about yourself — 3 minutes, technical focus, not a CV recital.
Follow-ups: (a) Which of those years were the most technically formative and why? (b) What is the single system you would put on your tombstone? (c) If I called your last tech lead, what would they say you are worst at?
Interviewer Evaluation Guide

Testing: communication, self-awareness, ability to compress 12 years into a narrative with a thesis.

Strong indicators: chronological arc with a reason for each move; mentions scale/impact numbers; ends by connecting to the target role; answers the "worst at" question honestly and specifically.

Warning signs: reads the CV linearly for 6+ minutes; cannot stop talking (a real problem for an architect who must brief executives); "worst at" answer is a humblebrag ("I care too much").

3.2Walk me through your career progression. What changed in how you work between year 4, year 8 and year 12?
Follow-ups: (a) When did you stop being measured on code and start being measured on outcomes? (b) What did you have to unlearn? (c) What is still true today that you believed in year 4?
Interviewer Evaluation Guide

Testing: genuine growth vs. 12 × 1 year of experience.

Strong: describes a shift from "how do I build it" → "should we build it, and who else is affected"; can name specific unlearned beliefs (e.g. "I used to think abstraction was always good", "I used to over-engineer for scale we never hit").

Warning: the last 8 years sound identical; describes seniority purely as bigger tickets; no mention of people, stakeholders, or money.

3.3What are you personally responsible for today? Describe yesterday, hour by hour.
Follow-ups: (a) What breaks if you are on holiday for 3 weeks? (b) Who reviews your work? (c) What do you sign off on that nobody else can?
Interviewer Evaluation Guide

Testing: real scope vs. inflated title. "Hour by hour yesterday" is very hard to fake.

Strong: concrete mix — design review, unblocking two devs, a production issue, a stakeholder call, some code; can name what only they can approve (schema changes, API contracts, release go/no-go).

Warning: pure ticket execution; cannot describe any decision authority; claims architect responsibilities but the day is 100% coding assigned work.

3.4What percentage of your work is architecture? Define what you mean by "architecture" first.
Follow-ups: (a) Show me an artefact you produced — ADR, diagram, NFR doc, cost model. (b) Who consumed it? (c) What did it change?
Interviewer Evaluation Guide

Testing: whether "architecture" means "I drew a box diagram" or "I own cross-cutting decisions that are expensive to reverse".

Strong: defines architecture as the set of decisions that are hard/expensive to change; 20–50% of time; names artefacts (ADR, C4 diagrams, NFR catalogue, threat model, cost estimate) and their audience.

Warning: claims 80% architecture but cannot produce an artefact or name a consumer; confuses architecture with tech selection only.

3.5Name three technical decisions you personally made in the last 18 months, and the one you got wrong.
Follow-ups: (a) What were the alternatives and why did you reject them? (b) Who disagreed? (c) How did you find out it was wrong? (d) What did it cost to correct?
Interviewer Evaluation Guide

Strong: decisions have context, constraints, alternatives, and a measurable outcome; the wrong one is described without defensiveness, with a detection mechanism ("our p99 alert caught it in week 2").

Warning: all three decisions are tool choices; no alternatives considered; "nothing went wrong"; the mistake is trivially small (a naming convention).

3.6What is the most complex system you have worked on? Draw it in 90 seconds (whiteboard).
Follow-ups: (a) Where was the complexity — essential or accidental? (b) What was the peak throughput / data volume / user count? (c) What part did YOU design? (d) What would you redesign now?
Interviewer Evaluation Guide

Testing: depth of exposure and the ability to abstract on demand. Keep this diagram — reuse it in §14 and §26 ("you said your system did X; how would that survive here?").

Strong: distinguishes essential domain complexity from accidental complexity; knows real numbers (TPS, GB, rows, users, latency); clearly separates their contribution from the team's.

Warning: "complex" = many technologies; no numbers; cannot draw the data flow; ownership is vague.

3.7What was your single biggest technical contribution, measured in business terms?
Follow-ups: (a) What was the metric before and after? (b) Who else could have done it? (c) How was it maintained after you left it?
Interviewer Evaluation Guide

Strong: converts technical work into money/time/risk ("cut infra spend 38% = £190k/yr", "reduced order failures from 2.1% to 0.05%"); mentions handover and durability of the change.

Warning: can only express value technically ("we migrated to .NET 8"); no before/after metric.

3.8What is the biggest mistake you have made in production? Full story: cause, blast radius, detection, recovery, prevention.
Follow-ups: (a) How long until you knew? (b) Who told you — a monitor or a customer? (c) What did the post-mortem change permanently? (d) Did it ever happen again?
Interviewer Evaluation Guide

Testing: ownership, blameless post-mortem culture, systemic vs. personal fixes.

Strong: owns the decision, not just the keystroke; detection story is honest; the fix is systemic (guardrail, test, alert, process) rather than "I was more careful afterwards".

Warning: the mistake is someone else's; no post-mortem; "we added a manual checklist" as the only remedy.

3.9Why are you targeting Technical Architect / Solution Architect now? What do you think the job actually is?
Follow-ups: (a) What part of your current work would you have to give up? (b) What is the hardest part of the architect job that engineers underestimate? (c) How would you know, 6 months in, that you were failing?
Interviewer Evaluation Guide

Strong: understands that the job is influence without authority, writing, saying no, and being accountable for decisions whose consequences appear 18 months later; accepts loss of hands-on time but insists on staying technically credible.

Warning: wants the title/salary; thinks architects don't code and don't get paged; describes it as "designing systems and handing them over".

Killer follow-up: "Describe a time you got a team to adopt a decision you had no authority to enforce."

Section 4 — Computer Science Fundamentals

Target: 20 minutesCategory: Computer Science (5%)Rule: no definitions — every question is applied

Pick 8–12 questions total. Prioritise 4.1–4.6, 4.11, 4.16–4.20. Fundamentals at 12 YOE should be automatic; hesitation here predicts weak performance in §9 and §14.

4A · Data Structures (applied)

4.1You must check whether a user ID already exists among 10 million records, thousands of times per second. What data structure do you choose and why?
Follow-ups: (a) Lookup complexity, average and worst case? (b) Memory implications for 10M entries? (c) What if the set changes constantly? (d) What if it must be shared across 6 API instances? (e) What if 100 MB of RAM is the hard limit — what do you sacrifice?
Interviewer Evaluation Guide

Expected: HashSet<T> / Dictionary — O(1) average, O(n) worst case with pathological collisions (in .NET, randomised string hashing mitigates this). Memory: ~10M × (hash + key + bucket overhead) ≈ hundreds of MB for strings, far less for long. Mutating data → invalidation strategy needed.

Level 3+ signals: moves the set out of process (Redis SET/SISMEMBER) once multiple instances exist; mentions Bloom filter or cuckoo filter for the memory-constrained variant, and correctly states it gives false positives but never false negatives → use it as a pre-filter before the database.

Level 4+: asks first "is this a correctness check or an optimisation?"; discusses the race between check and insert (must be enforced by a unique constraint in the DB regardless of the cache).

Warning: answers "a List and Contains"; unaware of hash collisions; never mentions the database's unique index as the source of truth.

4.2Array vs List<T> vs LinkedList<T> in .NET: when has the choice actually mattered in your code?
Follow-ups: (a) What happens internally when a List<T> grows past capacity? (b) Why is LinkedList<T> almost always slower in practice despite O(1) insertion? (c) When would you pre-size a list? (d) What is Span<T> for?
Interviewer Evaluation Guide

Expected: List = dynamic array; doubling reallocation + copy (amortised O(1) add); LinkedList loses on cache locality and per-node allocation — the O(1) is theoretical, memory access dominates. Pre-size when count is known to avoid repeated copies and LOH pressure. Span<T>/Memory<T> for allocation-free slicing over contiguous memory.

Strong: mentions Large Object Heap (>85 KB arrays), ArrayPool<T>, and that "measure first" beats theory.

4.3Explain how a Dictionary works internally. What happens if I use a mutable object as a key and then mutate it?
Follow-ups: (a) Contract between GetHashCode and Equals? (b) What makes a good hash function? (c) What is the effect of a hash function that returns a constant? (d) Why are record types convenient here?
Interviewer Evaluation Guide

Expected: buckets + chaining; hash → bucket index → equality comparison within bucket. Mutating a key changes its hash → the entry becomes unreachable (a real, silent bug class). Equal objects must have equal hash codes; the reverse is not required. Constant hash → all entries in one bucket → O(n) lookups (also a hash-DoS vector). Records give structural equality and hash for free.

4.4Stack vs Queue — give me a production problem you solved with each. Then: how would you implement an "undo" feature with a 50-step limit?
Follow-ups: (a) Why is a plain Stack<T> wrong for the 50-step limit? (b) What structure gives O(1) push/pop at both ends? (c) How would you make undo work across a distributed session?
Interviewer Evaluation Guide

Expected: stack = LIFO (undo, DFS, expression parsing, call stack); queue = FIFO (work distribution, message consumers, BFS). 50-step limit needs a bounded deque / circular buffer — a stack cannot evict from the bottom. Distributed undo → Redis list with LPUSH/LTRIM, or an event log.

4.5You need the top 20 highest-value orders from a stream of 50 million orders that does not fit in memory. Design it.
Follow-ups: (a) Why not sort everything? (b) Which heap — min or max — and why? (c) Complexity and memory? (d) How does this change if the stream is partitioned across 10 nodes? (e) What if you also need the top 20 per customer?
Interviewer Evaluation Guide

Expected: bounded min-heap of size 20 — compare each incoming item to the root, replace if larger. O(n log k) time, O(k) memory, single pass. Sorting is O(n log n) and needs all data resident. .NET: PriorityQueue<TElement,TPriority>.

Level 4 signal: distributed = each node computes local top-20 → merge 200 candidates centrally (this is correct for top-k; note it is NOT correct for medians/percentiles, which need sketches like t-digest/HLL). Top-20-per-customer → SQL window function ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY CustomerId ORDER BY Value DESC) is likely better than app code.

Warning: "load into a list and OrderBy().Take(20)" without noticing the memory constraint.

4.6Where do trees and graphs show up in the systems you build? (Not in interviews — in your actual work.)
Follow-ups: (a) How does a B+ tree relate to a SQL index? (b) Model an org chart / category hierarchy / permission inheritance in SQL Server — which approaches exist? (c) How do you detect a cycle in a dependency graph? (d) What is topological sort used for in your world?
Interviewer Evaluation Guide

Expected: B+ tree = index pages, high fanout, few disk reads, sorted leaves supporting range scans — that's why an index helps BETWEEN and ORDER BY. Hierarchies: adjacency list + recursive CTE, nested sets, materialized path, or hierarchyid; each with read/write trade-offs. Cycle detection: DFS with a visiting/visited colouring, or Kahn's algorithm. Topological sort: build/deploy ordering, workflow steps, migration dependencies, DI graph resolution.

Warning: can recite tree traversals but has never connected them to indexes, deployment ordering, or hierarchy modelling.

4.7Rapid structure-selection drill — one sentence each, 60 seconds total.
Requirement Candidate's choice ✓
Deduplicate 5M email addresses
Rate-limit: last 100 request timestamps per user
LRU cache with 10k entries
Autocomplete over 2M product names
Scheduled jobs, always execute the earliest due
Fast range query "orders between two dates"
Interviewer Only — Acceptable answers

HashSet · circular buffer/deque (or Redis sorted set with score = timestamp) · Dictionary + doubly linked list (or MemoryCache/Redis with LRU eviction policy) · trie / prefix index / search engine (Elastic, SQL full-text) · min-heap / priority queue (or Quartz/Hangfire backed by a DB index) · B-tree index / sorted structure.

Score the reasoning speed: a senior should answer 5 of 6 instantly.

4B · Algorithms (conceptual + practical)

4.8Ten conceptual questions — ask quickly, ~45 seconds each.
  1. When is binary search valid, and what is the classic bug in its implementation?
  2. Why is quicksort usually faster than mergesort despite the same average complexity — and when is it a bad choice?
  3. What does "stable sort" mean and when do you need it?
  4. How does .NET's List.Sort() differ from OrderBy() in LINQ regarding stability and allocation?
  5. Explain recursion vs iteration — what limits recursion in .NET, and what is tail-call elimination?
  6. BFS vs DFS: which one for "shortest number of hops", which one for "does a path exist with low memory"?
  7. What is memoization and where have you used it?
  8. What is the difference between a greedy algorithm and dynamic programming? Give one real example of each.
  9. What is a hash collision and how do hash tables resolve it?
  10. What is the two-pointer / sliding-window technique good for?
Interviewer Evaluation Guide

Key expected points: binary search needs a sorted, randomly-accessible collection — classic bug is (low+high)/2 overflow and the off-by-one in the loop bound. Quicksort: in-place, cache-friendly, but O(n²) with bad pivots / adversarial input; mergesort is stable and predictable, better for linked structures and external sorting. List.Sort() = unstable introsort, in-place; OrderBy() = stable, allocates. Recursion limited by the ~1 MB default thread stack; the .NET JIT does not guarantee tail-call elimination in C#. BFS = shortest hops + more memory; DFS = lower memory, may find non-optimal path. Greedy: coin change with canonical coins, scheduling by earliest deadline; DP: knapsack, edit distance, retry-cost optimisation.

Score holistically: 8+ if they answer 8 of 10 crisply with an example; 5 if textbook-only; ≤4 if several are wrong.

4.9Five practical algorithm scenarios — ask 2 or 3 depending on time.
# Scenario What good looks like (interviewer only — do not read aloud)
S1 Reconcile 4 million bank transactions against 4 million ledger entries nightly. Design the matching. Hash join on a composite key in memory or in SQL; O(n+m) not O(n·m); handle duplicates, tolerance windows, and unmatched buckets; idempotent re-runs.
S2 Detect duplicate customer records across 2M rows where names/addresses differ slightly. Blocking/bucketing first (postcode, soundex, first 3 chars) to avoid O(n²); then fuzzy distance (Levenshtein/Jaro) within blocks; threshold + human review queue.
S3 Merge 30 sorted daily export files (each 2 GB) into one sorted file. k-way merge with a min-heap, streaming, O(N log k), constant memory — classic external sort.
S4 Given a warehouse layout graph, find the cheapest picking route between two zones. Dijkstra with a priority queue; A* if a heuristic exists; discuss negative weights (Bellman-Ford) and pre-computation caching.
S5 An API must return the 95th percentile latency over a rolling 5-minute window, continuously. Cannot store everything — use histogram buckets / t-digest / HDR histogram; discuss approximation vs exactness and why averages hide problems.

4C · Complexity Analysis

4.10Complexity ladder — show each snippet in order, ask for time AND space complexity.

Snippet 1

for (int i = 0; i < n; i++)
    for (int j = 0; j < n; j++)
        Console.WriteLine(i + j);

Snippet 2

for (int i = 0; i < n; i++)
    for (int j = i; j < n; j++)
        Sum(i, j);

Snippet 3

while (n > 1) { n = n / 2; Process(n); }

Snippet 4

for (int i = 0; i < n; i++)
    for (int j = 1; j < n; j *= 2)
        Work(i, j);

Snippet 5 — the one that matters

// orders: 100,000 items   customers: 50,000 items
var result = orders
    .Where(o => customers.Any(c => c.Id == o.CustomerId))
    .ToList();

Snippet 6

public int Fib(int n) => n <= 1 ? n : Fib(n - 1) + Fib(n - 2);

Snippet 7 — the production trap

foreach (var order in await _db.Orders.ToListAsync())
{
    var customer = await _db.Customers.FindAsync(order.CustomerId);
    order.CustomerName = customer.Name;
}
For snippet 5 and 7 ask: "What would you change, and what is the new complexity / query count?"
Interviewer Only — Answers
# Time Space Notes
1 O(n²) O(1) Baseline sanity check. Anyone failing this at 12 YOE is a serious concern.
2 O(n²) O(1) n(n+1)/2 iterations — still quadratic. Watch for "O(n²/2)" — constants dropped.
3 O(log n) O(1) Halving loop.
4 O(n log n) O(1) Outer linear × inner doubling.
5 O(n·m) = 5×10⁹ comparisons O(1) extra Fix: build a HashSet of customer IDs → O(n+m). If these are IQueryable, it becomes a SQL join instead — ask which.
6 O(2ⁿ) O(n) stack Memoize → O(n)/O(n); iterative → O(n)/O(1).
7 1 + N queries (N+1 problem) — Fix: single projection/join Include or Select; also note it loads the whole Orders table into memory, and mutating tracked entities here may cause unintended writes on SaveChanges.

Also probe: average vs worst case (hash lookup, quicksort), amortised cost (List growth), and "when does O(n²) not matter?" — correct answer: when n is bounded and small, and clarity wins. An architect knows Big-O is a screening tool, not the decision.

4.11Five complexity discussion questions
  1. What is the difference between average case and worst case, and give a case where the worst case actually happened to you in production.
  2. What is amortised complexity? Where does it appear in .NET collections?
  3. Why can an O(n log n) algorithm beat an O(n) algorithm on real hardware?
  4. How does space complexity influence a decision when RAM is cheap but GC pauses are not?
  5. Your batch job is O(n) but takes 6 hours. Complexity is fine — what do you investigate?
Interviewer Evaluation Guide

Q3 expected: constant factors, cache locality, branch prediction, allocation and GC, I/O dominance. Q4: allocations drive Gen0/Gen2 collections and LOH fragmentation → latency spikes; a "slower" algorithm with fewer allocations often wins on p99. Q5 expected checks: is it I/O bound (network/disk/DB round trips), per-item overhead, serialization, chattiness, single-threaded when it could be partitioned, DB locking, logging cost. The signal: they stop treating Big-O as the answer and start measuring.
