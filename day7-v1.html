SECTION 01 — THE REVIEW

Week 1, In One Pass

Six days, distilled to what matters. Not a re-teach — a map of what you should reach for during the mock.

DAY WHAT I LEARNED WHY IT MATTERS WHAT AN ARCHITECT THINKS ABOUT
1 · Think Architecture = the expensive-to-change decisions. The five pillars: reliability, performance, scalability, security, maintainability. Every later decision sits on these five. They are the lenses, not a checklist. Which pillar am I deliberately trading away today — and can I say so out loud?
2 · Scale Registered ≠ concurrent. The funnel to peak RPS. Bottleneck hunting. Scale out vs up. Statelessness. The V1→V6 path. Size for the peak, not the marketing number. The weakest part wins. What's the real number — and what breaks first at 10×?
3 · Data Data shape before technology. SQL vs NoSQL. ACID. Consistency. Indexes, replicas, sharding. Multi-tenant. The database is the truth. Get it wrong and every layer inherits the mistake. What does this data need — not what is popular?
4 · Flows Partial success. Sagas. Compensation. Sync vs async. Idempotency. Retry + timeout. One action, many services — failure is normal, not exceptional. What does the customer see when step 3 fails?
5 · Cache When/what/how to cache. TTL. Invalidation. Stampede. Redis failure. Key design. The fastest query is the one the DB never sees — but it is a trade, not a gift. How wrong may this data be, and what happens when the cache dies?
6 · Message Queue vs direct call. RabbitMQ concepts. Retries, DLQ. Idempotency. Ordering. Scaling consumers. Decouple, survive spikes, lose nothing — only delay. Does the user need the answer now, or later?
THE WEEK IN ONE SENTENCE

An architect reads the numbers, finds the weakest part, chooses the cheapest fix that survives failure, and can explain the trade-off — while never letting the customer feel the machinery. Everything in Days 1–6 serves that sentence.

SECTION 02 — THE FRAMEWORK

The Architect Thinking Framework

Fourteen steps, in order. Use this in any system design interview. It keeps you from jumping to technology, keeps you from skipping failure, and makes sure you say the trade-offs before the interviewer asks.

  1. Understand the business problemWhat does the business earn, save, or learn from this system? Restate it in one sentence. If you cannot, you do not know what you are building.SAY: "so the core value here is…"
  2. Ask important questionsWho uses it? What is the peak? What is sensitive? What may fail? How much may it cost? The first five minutes are questions, not boxes.SAY: "before I draw, three questions…"
  3. Identify users and scaleRegistered → daily active → concurrent → peak RPS. Four multiplications that turn marketing into engineering.SAY: "1M registered means about 2,000 peak RPS, so…"
  4. Identify important requirementsSeparate must-have from nice-to-have. Name the functional and the non-functional (latency, availability, consistency).SAY: "the non-negotiable here is…"
  5. Identify risks and failuresWhat breaks first? The DB. The payment gateway. The cache. Walk the failure path before you walk the happy path.SAY: "the first thing to die will be…"
  6. Create a simple architectureStart with the fewest boxes that could work. A monolith, one DB, one cache. Complexity is earned, never assumed.SAY: "I'd start embarrassingly simple…"
  7. Decide the database approachData shape → relationships → consistency → read/write ratio → scaling path. SQL for the core, document for flexible, cache for sessions.SAY: "this data is connected and transactional, so…"
  8. Decide the caching approachWhat is hot, cold-changing, and forgivable? TTL, invalidation, and the cache-down plan — all three, or you have not decided.SAY: "cache X for 60s, and if Redis dies…"
  9. Decide the messaging approachWhich steps can wait? Queue them. Which must answer now? Call them. Which are both? Split them.SAY: "payment is sync, notification is queued…"
  10. Think about securityTrust boundaries, auth vs authorization, what you never store, tenant isolation, least privilege.SAY: "the boundary is here, and we never store…"
  11. Think about scalingWhat scales out (stateless), what scales up (the writer), what you add when the number grows. Name the triggers.SAY: "at 10× I add…"
  12. Think about monitoring and recoveryWhat do you measure, what do you alert on, and what is the runbook when it breaks? A design with no metrics is a guess.SAY: "I'd alarm on hit ratio and queue depth…"
  13. Explain trade-offsFor every choice: what did I pick, what did I reject, and why. This is where seniority shows — not in the boxes, in the reasons.SAY: "I chose X over Y because…"
  14. Summarize the final designClose with a 30-second recap: the shape, the two or three real decisions, and what you would change at 10×.SAY: "so in one sentence, this is…"
THE POINT

Notice what is not in the list: "pick the technology." Technology is the answer to steps 7–9, and only after steps 1–6 have framed the problem. If you find yourself naming frameworks in minute one, you skipped the framework.

Set a timer. Treat this as a real interview — you talk, the page does not. Design the Online Ordering Platform.

"Design a system for an online ordering platform. A customer can browse products, search, add items to a cart, place an order, pay, and receive notifications. Multiple companies use the same platform. Orders must not be lost, payments must not be duplicated, and the system should stay available when individual components fail."

SECTION 04 — THE QUESTIONS

15 Questions the Interviewer Will Ask

Progressive difficulty — warm-up, core, deep, then curveballs. Answer each out loud as you design. There are no answers here; the questions are the interview.

  1. Who are the users of this platform, and what does each group actually do?WARM-UP
  2. What is the expected traffic — and how do you translate "1 million users" into something you can design against?WARM-UP
  3. Which operations are most important to get right — and which can be slightly wrong?WARM-UP
  4. Walk me through your high-level architecture. What are the main components?CORE
  5. How will the database scale as orders and reporting grow?CORE
  6. What should be cached — and what must never go in the cache?CORE
  7. Where should messaging be used in the order flow, and where should you keep it synchronous?CORE
  8. How is payment failure handled when the inventory step fails after the charge?CORE
  9. What happens if Redis goes down at peak? Walk me through the minute-by-minute.DEEP
  10. What happens if the database becomes slow — how do you know, and what do you fix first?DEEP
  11. How do you prevent a customer being charged twice for one order?DEEP
  12. How do you handle one company generating unusually high traffic on a shared platform?DEEP
  13. What happens if an entire Azure region fails? What is your recovery story?DEEP
  14. Tell me three decisions you made — and for each, what you rejected and why.CURVEBALL
  15. If the budget were cut by a third tomorrow, what would you remove from your design first?CURVEBALL
WHAT THE INTERVIEWER IS REALLY SCORING

Not whether you know the terms. Whether you ask before answering, attach numbers to words, find the weakest part, and name the trade-off. A wrong answer defended with clear reasoning beats a right answer delivered as a fact.

HOW TO SCORE THIS ROUND

Each answer should name: the component that breaks · the metric that tells you · the change you make · the new trade-off you accept. If your answer is "add more servers" more than twice, revisit Day 2. If it is "the cache will handle it" more than twice, revisit Day 5.

SECTION 08 — THE HONEST LOOK

Where Did I Think Like a Developer Instead of an Architect?

Tick everything that happened during your mock. No judgement — just the list that tells you what Week 2 must fix.

  • Jumping to technology too early — I named RabbitMQ or Redis before I knew the numbers.
  • Not asking enough questions — I designed before I understood who and what.
  • Ignoring business impact — I optimised for the call, not for the customer or the money.
  • Ignoring cost — I never mentioned what the design would cost to run.
  • Overengineering — I added microservices, sharding, or event sourcing nobody asked for.
  • Not explaining alternatives — I gave one answer and never said what I rejected.
  • Designing only the happy path — I walked the success and skipped the failure.
  • Forgetting failure scenarios — no answer for the cache dying or the region failing.
  • Focusing on implementation details — I described code, schemas, or config instead of the architecture.
  • No numbers — I said "fast" and "scalable" without attaching a figure.
THE RULE

Every tick is not a failure — it is a map. Week 2 targets whatever you ticked most. If you ticked more than four, retake the mock before moving on; the gaps are habits, and habits need repetition, not new content.

WEEK 1 — CLOSE

The Architect Mindset, One Week In

Five rules you should now be able to say without looking. These are the whole of Week 1.

01

Ask before you draw.

Who, how many, how much, what fails, what may it cost. The first five minutes of any design are questions — the boxes come after.

02

Numbers, or it did not happen.

"Fast" and "scalable" are feelings. p95 under 300ms, 99.9% availability, 10× load for 15 minutes — those are requirements.

03

The weakest part wins.

Find the bottleneck, protect the database, plan the failure. Strengthening anything else changes nothing.

04

Every choice is a trade.

Choose, reject, and say why — for both. If you cannot name what you gave up, you did not make a decision.

05

Simple until proven otherwise.

The best design is the smallest one that survives the real constraints, with a cheap path to grow. Complexity is earned, one measured pain at a time.

Six days of learning, one day of proof. If the mock humbled you — good. That is the point. Week 2 starts tomorrow, and it assumes you carry these five rules with you.
