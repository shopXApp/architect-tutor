PART 1 — FIRST PRINCIPLES

What Is an Architecture Trade-off?

A trade-off is simple: to get more of one thing, you accept less of another. Every architecture decision is a trade-off, whether you notice it or not.

Everyday examples first

Buying a carA sports car is fast but expensive and uncomfortable for a family. A minivan is roomy but slow. There is no car that is fastest, cheapest, roomiest and safest at once. You choose based on your life, not on "the best car."
Choosing a restaurantFast food is quick and cheap but not healthy. A fine restaurant is delicious but slow and costly. On a lunch break you pick speed. On an anniversary you pick quality. Same person, different decision — because the requirement changed.
Renting vs buying a homeBuying gives control and long-term value but needs a large upfront cost and locks you in. Renting is flexible and cheap to start but you own nothing. Neither is "right" — it depends on how long you will stay.
Packing for a tripPack everything and you carry a heavy bag. Pack light and you may miss something. You decide based on where you are going and for how long — not on a rule.

Now connect it to software

  1. What is a trade-off? Choosing one benefit while accepting a cost somewhere else. Faster reads → more memory for cache. Higher availability → more servers and more cost.
  2. Why can we rarely have everything? Because the good qualities pull in different directions. Strong consistency needs coordination, which slows things down. Maximum flexibility needs more options, which adds complexity.
  3. Why does improving one area hurt another? Resources are shared. Adding a cache improves speed but now you must keep it in sync. Splitting into services improves team independence but adds network failures.
  4. Why is there no "perfect architecture"? Because "perfect" depends on the business. A design that is perfect for a bank (consistency first) is wrong for a social feed (availability first).
  5. Why compare multiple options? Because if you only consider one, you cannot know its cost. You only see the price of something when you put it next to the alternative.
  6. Why is "best technology" the wrong question? Technologies do not exist in a vacuum. The right question is: "Which option gives this business the most of what it needs, at a cost it can afford, with the risks it can accept?"
THE ONE SENTENCE

An architect does not pick the best tool. An architect picks the right compromise — and can explain what was given up.

PART 2 — THE REPEATABLE METHOD

The Architect's Decision Process

Nine steps. Use them in every interview and every real project. Each step is illustrated with a running example: "Reports are slowing down our order system."

  1. Understand the business problemNot "we need a data warehouse" — but "month-end reports make checkout time out, and we lose orders." Restate it in money or customer pain.EXAMPLE: LOST ORDERS DURING REPORT RUNS = LOST REVENUE
  2. Identify functional requirementsWhat must the system do? Create orders, show dashboards, produce monthly reports, keep audit history.EXAMPLE: REPORTS MUST STILL EXIST — WE CANNOT REMOVE THEM
  3. Identify non-functional requirementsPerformance = how fast. Scalability = how well it grows. Availability = how often it is up. Security = how protected the data is. Reliability = does it do the right thing every time. Maintainability = how easy to change. Cost = what it takes to build and run.EXAMPLE: CHECKOUT < 500 MS · REPORTS MAY BE 1 HOUR OLD · 99.9% UPTIME
  4. Identify constraintsBudget, team skills, existing technology, deadline, compliance, infrastructure. Constraints cut options before you even compare them.EXAMPLE: 1 DEVOPS ENGINEER · SQL SERVER ALREADY LICENSED · 6-WEEK DEADLINE
  5. Create possible solutionsAt least three. If you only have one, you have not thought enough. Include a "do the minimum" option.EXAMPLE: A) ADD INDEXES · B) READ REPLICA FOR REPORTS · C) SEPARATE REPORTING SERVICE + WAREHOUSE
  6. Compare the solutionsUse the 10-factor framework (Part 3). Score honestly. Write down what each option costs, not just what it gives.EXAMPLE: A IS CHEAP BUT MAY NOT BE ENOUGH · B IS 2 DAYS · C IS 3 MONTHS
  7. Choose onePick the option that best fits the requirements and the constraints. Not the most impressive one.EXAMPLE: B — READ REPLICA. SOLVES THE PAIN IN DAYS WITHIN THE TEAM'S SKILLS
  8. Document whyWrite an Architecture Decision Record (Part 7). Future engineers must know why, not just what.EXAMPLE: "WE CHOSE B BECAUSE… WE REJECTED C BECAUSE…"
  9. Define risks and future impactWhat can go wrong? Replication lag. When do we revisit? If report volume grows 10× or the business needs real-time analytics.EXAMPLE: REVISIT IF LAG > 5 MIN OR IF REAL-TIME DASHBOARDS ARE REQUIRED
INTERVIEW TIP

You do not have to say "step one, step two". Just walk through it naturally: "First I'd confirm the real problem… then the must-haves… then constraints… then I'd compare three options…" Interviewers hear the structure and score it.

PART 3 — THE TOOL

The 10-Factor Trade-off Framework

Ten questions. Ask every one of them about every option. This is the whole framework — simple enough to remember under interview pressure.

FACTOR THE QUESTION WHAT YOU ARE REALLY CHECKING
Business Does it solve the actual business problem? Is this fixing the real pain, or a pain we imagine? Does it move revenue, cost, or customer experience?
Cost What will it cost to build and run? Developer time now, cloud bill every month, licences, and the hidden cost of extra tools.
Performance Will it be fast enough? Not "fastest" — fast enough for the stated target (e.g. p95 under 500 ms).
Scale Can it handle future growth? What happens at 3×, 10×? Where is the first bottleneck? Is there a path forward without a rewrite?
Availability What happens when something fails? Single points of failure. Failover. Does one broken piece take down everything?
Security Is the data protected? Attack surface, data isolation, compliance, secrets, who can reach what.
Complexity How difficult is it to operate? Moving parts, monitoring, on-call burden, how many things can break at 3 a.m.
Team Can the current team support it? Do they know it? Can they debug it? Can we hire for it? A great design nobody can run is a bad design.
Maintenance How easy is it to change later? Are we locked in? How hard is a schema change, a version upgrade, a vendor swap?
Risk What could go wrong? Technical risk, delivery risk, vendor risk, knowledge risk. And what is the plan if it does?

How to use it

  • Step 1: List your options in columns (A, B, C).
  • Step 2: For each factor, write one honest line per option. Not scores yet — words.
  • Step 3: Mark which 2–3 factors matter most for this business. This is the key step — weight the factors, do not treat them equally.
  • Step 4: The option that wins the important factors and does not fail badly on the rest is your choice.
  • Step 5: Say the losing options out loud: "B was rejected because it failed on Team and Complexity, which matter most here."
THE MISTAKE TO AVOID

Do not add up scores mechanically. A design that scores 8/10 on nine factors but fails Security for a bank is not an 8 — it is disqualified. Some factors are gates, not weights. Identify the gates first.

PART 4 — THE CLASSICS

The 10 Most Important Architecture Trade-offs

These ten appear in almost every system and almost every interview. Learn the shape of each one.

1. Simplicity vs Scalability

The tension: Simple systems are easy to build, understand, debug and operate. Highly scalable systems have more moving parts — load balancers, queues, shards, caches — each of which can break.
When scalability matters: When you have real evidence — measured traffic, a real growth curve, a real bottleneck. Not a slide from a competitor.
When designing for huge scale early is wasteful: A 5,000-user product built for 50 million users spends its first two years paying for complexity it never uses, while the competitor ships features.
Example: A startup builds a sharded, multi-region, event-sourced platform for a product with 300 users. The team spends 60% of its time on infrastructure. A single well-indexed database would have served them for three years.

2. Performance vs Cost

The tension: Faster usually costs more. Bigger servers, more replicas, premium storage, caches, CDNs — each adds a line to the bill.
Caching: Improves speed dramatically but adds a new component to run, a new failure mode, and the classic invalidation problem.
Extra servers: Improve throughput linearly — but so does the cost. And they do nothing for a slow query.
Example: An API responds in 800 ms. Option A: double the server size for $400/month more → 600 ms. Option B: add one missing index for $0 → 120 ms. The architect measures first, then spends.

3. Consistency vs Availability

The tension: Consistency means every reader sees the latest write immediately. Availability means the system always answers. When parts of a system cannot talk to each other, you must choose: refuse to answer (stay consistent) or answer with possibly old data (stay available).
Must be immediately correct: Payment — you cannot charge twice. Inventory at the moment of purchase — you cannot sell the last item twice.
Temporary difference acceptable: Reporting — a dashboard five minutes old is fine. Order history — appearing three seconds after checkout is fine.
Why business matters: The same "order" data needs strong consistency at checkout and eventual consistency in the sales report. The architecture must treat them differently — not pick one rule for everything.

4. Flexibility vs Simplicity

The tension: Every option you add — configurable rules, pluggable providers, generic engines — is more flexibility for tomorrow and more complexity for today.
Too many options: Systems with 200 configuration flags where nobody knows which combinations are tested. "Flexible" becomes "fragile".
Example: A team builds a generic "workflow engine" so any future process can be configured. Two years later, only one workflow exists, and every change requires understanding the engine. A plain 200-line service would have been clearer.

5. Microservices vs Simplicity

From Day 18–19: Microservices are useful when many teams need independent releases, when one part needs radically different scale, or when failure isolation is worth the network cost.
Unnecessary complexity when: The team is small, the domain is still moving, traffic is moderate, or transactions must span the areas you would split.
Modular monolith is better when: You want clean boundaries and team ownership without paying the distributed-systems tax. It also keeps the door open to extract a service later.

6. SQL vs NoSQL

Data relationships: Orders link to customers, products, payments. SQL handles this natively with joins. NoSQL makes you duplicate or join in code.
Transactions: SQL gives multi-row, multi-table atomic updates. Most NoSQL stores give atomicity per document.
Scale: NoSQL scales writes horizontally more easily. SQL scales reads easily (replicas) and writes with more effort.
Query needs: Ad-hoc reporting and unknown future queries favour SQL. Known, fixed access patterns favour NoSQL.
Flexibility: NoSQL tolerates changing shapes. SQL needs migrations — which is also a safety net.
Team skills & cost: Most .NET teams know SQL deeply. Licensing (SQL Server) vs open-source (PostgreSQL) vs managed NoSQL pricing all matter.
Scenario: Core orders and payments → SQL. Product catalog with varying attributes per category → document store is reasonable. Session and cart → key-value store.

7. Synchronous vs Asynchronous

Order → Payment → Notification.
Synchronous (wait for each step): User gets an immediate, definite answer. Simple to reason about. But if Notification is slow, checkout is slow. If Payment is down, checkout fails.
Asynchronous (queue the later steps): Checkout answers fast; email and stock updates happen in the background. Failures are retried. But now you need a queue, retries, idempotency, and the user may not see instant confirmation.
Architect's split: Payment stays synchronous — the user must know if they were charged. Notification becomes asynchronous — nobody needs the email in the same 300 ms.

8. Cache vs Database

Why cache helps: Memory is thousands of times faster than disk, and it removes repeated load from the database.
Why cache is not the source of truth: It is volatile, it can be evicted, it can be stale, and it usually has no durability guarantees. The database is where truth lives; the cache is a fast copy.
Cache failure: If Redis goes down, every request falls back to the database at once — the "thundering herd". You must plan for it (Day 5 and Day 12).
Invalidation: When data changes, the cached copy is wrong until refreshed. TTLs, explicit invalidation, or accepting staleness — you choose, per data type.
Cost: Memory is more expensive per GB than disk. Cache the hot 5%, not everything.

9. Build vs Buy

Build internally: Full control, exact fit, no vendor dependency. But slowest, most expensive, and you own every bug forever.
Use an existing product / library: Fast start, proven, community support. But you adapt to its way of thinking and its upgrade schedule.
Use a cloud service: Zero operations, scales on demand, pay for use. But lock-in, per-request cost at scale, and less control.
External SaaS: Fastest of all, no code. But your data lives elsewhere, pricing can change, and customisation is limited.
The rule: Build what differentiates your business. Buy everything else. Nobody wins customers by writing a better email-sending library.

10. Cloud vs On-Premises

Cost: Cloud is cheap to start and expensive at steady high load. On-prem is expensive to start and cheap at steady load. Spiky traffic favours cloud; flat, predictable, heavy traffic can favour on-prem.
Control & security: On-prem gives full control — and full responsibility. Cloud gives shared responsibility and strong defaults, but some regulations demand data stays in a specific building.
Scalability & operations: Cloud scales in minutes with no hardware purchase. On-prem needs a small operations team and lead time for capacity.
Migration effort: Moving a 15-year-old system to the cloud is a project in itself, with its own risk.
Do not assume cloud is always better: A factory with an unreliable internet link, a bank with data residency laws, or a company with a paid-off data centre and steady load can all rationally choose on-prem or hybrid.

PART 6 — THE CORE INTERVIEW SKILL

"Why X?" and "Why Not Y?"

Interviewers ask "why did you choose X?" to hear your reasoning. Then they ask "why not Y?" to see whether you actually considered alternatives. Most candidates can answer the first. Architects answer both.

The structure of a strong answer

  1. Name the requirement that drove it. "The key constraint was…"
  2. Name the alternative you considered. "I looked at Y as well."
  3. Say what Y does better — honestly. "Y would have given us…"
  4. Say why that did not win here. "…but for this system, that mattered less than…"
  5. Name when Y would win. "If we had 50 teams / 10× traffic / strict residency, I would revisit."

Five examples — weak vs strong

Example 1 — "Why Redis?" / "Why not just the database?"

WEAK ANSWER"Redis is faster than the database."
STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER"Product reads were 4,000 per second against data that changes a few times an hour. I considered indexes plus a read replica — that would have worked and kept things simpler. But it still leaves 4,000 queries per second on the database tier, and it does not remove the load. A cache with a 60-second TTL and invalidation on update removed 90% of that load for a fraction of the cost. I accepted the risk of a briefly stale price and a cache-down fallback plan. If the data changed every second, the cache would stop helping and I would go with the replica instead."

Example 2 — "Why a modular monolith?" / "Why not microservices?"

WEAK ANSWER"Microservices are too complex."
STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER"Microservices would give us independent releases and isolation, and I did consider them seriously. But we have 20 developers, one DevOps engineer, and 150 requests per second. The real pain was reporting slowing checkout and slow regression testing — neither of which requires distribution to fix. A modular monolith with a read replica solves both within weeks and inside the team's skills. I kept the module boundaries strict so Notifications can be extracted as the first service if release independence becomes a measured problem. At 50+ developers or a component needing 10× scale, I would revisit."

Example 3 — "Why SQL?" / "Why not NoSQL?"

WEAK ANSWER"SQL is what the team knows."
STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER"Team familiarity is a real factor, but not the main one. Orders, payments and inventory are highly related and must update atomically — that is what relational transactions are built for. NoSQL would scale writes more easily, and I would use a document store for the product catalog where attributes vary by category. But for the transactional core, giving up multi-row transactions and ad-hoc reporting to gain write scale we do not need yet would be the wrong trade. If order writes exceeded what a well-tuned SQL primary handles, I would partition first and consider sharding second."

Example 4 — "Why a queue?" / "Why not direct API calls?"

WEAK ANSWER"Queues are more scalable."
STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER"Direct calls are simpler and I kept them for payment, because the user must know immediately whether they were charged. For email and warehouse updates, a direct call means checkout waits on the slowest, least reliable dependency, and a provider outage fails the sale. A queue lets checkout answer in 200 ms, retries failures, and absorbs Black Friday spikes. The cost is a broker to operate and idempotent consumers. If our volume were 50 orders a day, the broker would not be worth it and I would use a simple background job table instead."

Example 5 — "Why App Service?" / "Why not Kubernetes?"

WEAK ANSWER"Kubernetes is overkill."
STRONG ARCHITECT ANSWER"Kubernetes gives full control and portability, and for 30 services with a platform team it is the right call. We have five deployables, steady traffic, and one DevOps engineer. Kubernetes would mean that engineer spends most of their time on cluster upgrades, networking and security instead of on our delivery pipeline. App Service gives us deployment slots, auto-scale and managed patching with almost no operational cost. The trade-off is less control and some platform limits. I would revisit if we passed 15–20 services or needed workloads the platform cannot run."
THE TELL

A strong answer always says something good about the rejected option. If you cannot name what Y does better, the interviewer assumes you never really considered it.

PART 7 — WRITING IT DOWN

The Architecture Decision Record (ADR)

An ADR is a one-page document that captures what you decided, why, what you rejected, and when to look again. Six months later, nobody remembers the conversation — the ADR does.

ADR-007 — APPLICATION ARCHITECTURE: MODULAR MONOLITH VS MICROSERVICES

Decision
We will structure the Inventory & Order platform as a Modular Monolith with strict module boundaries and per-module database schemas, extracting the Notification module as the only independent service.

Context
The current .NET application has 7 areas in one tangled codebase. 20 developers, 1 DevOps engineer, 500,000 users, 50,000 orders/day (~150 RPS peak). Month-end reporting slows checkout. Teams want faster releases. Management has requested "microservices". Business expects 5× growth in 18 months.

Options considered
A) Keep the current monolith and tune it.
B) Refactor into a Modular Monolith + read replica + extract Notifications.
C) Split into 7 microservices with a database each.

Decision factors (most important first)
1. Team capacity to operate the result (1 DevOps engineer).
2. Time to relieve the checkout/reporting pain.
3. Transactional integrity of Order + Inventory + Payment.
4. Path to scale 5× without rewrite.
5. Release velocity.

Why B
Solves the reporting pain in days (replica). Gives module ownership and faster, safer releases through boundaries and architecture tests. Keeps ACID transactions where the business needs them. 5× growth (~750 RPS) is well within a horizontally scaled monolith. Extracting Notifications gives the one team that needs independent releases exactly that, and validates our container pipeline at low risk.

Rejected options
A — does not address coupling or release speed; pain returns as the team grows.
C — requires operational maturity we do not have; introduces Sagas for order/payment/inventory; triples infrastructure cost; 9–12 months during which feature delivery slows. Solves an organisational problem we do not yet have (we are 20 developers, not 200).

Risks
Boundaries erode without enforcement → mitigated by NetArchTest in CI.
Replica lag confuses users → mitigated by routing only reports/dashboards to the replica.
Notification extraction introduces first network boundary → mitigated by async queue, retries, monitoring.

Future review
Revisit when any of: developers exceed 50; one module needs >10× the scale of others; a module needs a different runtime; deployment lead time exceeds one week despite modularisation. Review date: 12 months.

WHY THIS MATTERS IN INTERVIEWS

When you say "I'd write an ADR with the options, the factors, and the review trigger", you are telling the interviewer you have done this for real. Few candidates mention it. It is a strong signal.

PART 8 — BEYOND TECHNICAL QUALITY

Business vs Technical Trade-off

An architect who only optimises for technical elegance is a very expensive senior developer. The job is to make the business succeed.

Option A — Technically excellent, expensiveMulti-region, event-sourced, fully isolated, 99.99% availability, 9 months, $2M, needs 3 new hires.
Option B — Good enough, cheaper, fasterSingle region with backups, modular monolith, 99.9% availability, 10 weeks, $200K, current team.

The correct answer depends on…

FACTOR WHEN A WINS WHEN B WINS
Revenue The system processes $50M/day; one hour of downtime costs more than the extra build. The system supports a $2M/year product line; A would never pay back.
Deadline No hard date; quality matters more than timing. A regulatory or market deadline in 3 months. Late = worthless.
Risk Failure is catastrophic (safety, money, law). Failure is recoverable and rare; users tolerate brief outages.
Users Millions of users across continents; latency and residency matter. Thousands of users in one country.
Business importance This is the core product the company lives on. This is an internal tool or an experiment.
THE PRINCIPLE

"Technically better" is not the same as "business better." A design that ships in 10 weeks and earns revenue can fund the better version later. A design that is perfect but arrives after the market moved has zero value. The architect's job is to know which situation they are in.

PART 9 — THE FULL PRICE

How an Architect Thinks About Cost

Most people see one number: the cloud bill. An architect sees five.

1. Build costDeveloper effort and calendar time. The most visible cost — and the one most often the smallest over the system's life.
2. Run costServers, databases, storage, network, monitoring, licences. Every month, forever. Grows with traffic and with the number of components.
3. Maintenance costEvery future change, upgrade, security patch and dependency bump. More components = more of these.
4. Failure costWhat one hour of downtime costs in revenue, reputation, refunds and staff time. Justifies (or does not justify) high-availability spend.
5. Complexity costThe hidden one. Extra tools, extra skills, extra people, longer onboarding, slower debugging, more on-call pain. It shows up as slower delivery, not as a line item.

Example: simple vs highly distributed (same business, 3 years)

COST TYPE SIMPLE (MODULAR MONOLITH + REPLICA) HIGHLY DISTRIBUTED (12 SERVICES, K8S, 12 DBs)
Build 3 months, existing team 10 months, existing team + 2 hires
Run (monthly) ~$1,500 — 3 app instances, 1 DB + replica, cache ~$9,000 — cluster, 12 DBs, broker, gateway, tracing, logs
Maintenance 1 pipeline, 1 runtime to upgrade, 1 schema migration path 12 pipelines, 12 base images, contract versioning, 12 migration paths
Failure Fewer moving parts; one clear log stream; fast triage Partial failures; distributed tracing needed; slower triage
Complexity New hire productive in 1 week New hire productive in 1–2 months; DevOps becomes a bottleneck
3-year total (rough) Build ~$250K + run ~$55K + ops modest Build ~$900K + run ~$325K + 2 extra salaries ~$600K
WHEN THE CHEAP OPTION BECOMES EXPENSIVE

The reverse is also true. A "cheap" single server with no replica, no backups and no monitoring looks like $300/month — until the disk dies, a week of orders is lost, and the recovery costs more than five years of a proper setup. Cheap to build is not cheap to own. Always price the failure.

PART 10 — WHEN REQUIREMENTS FIGHT

Trade-offs Under Conflicting Constraints

Real projects never give you all the money, all the time and all the quality. The architect's first job is to find out which requirement wins when they collide — and to make the stakeholders say it out loud.

Situation 1 — "Cheap, highly available, fast, and delivered next month"

Conflict: Very low cost vs very high availability vs very high performance vs fast delivery. You can have two, maybe three.
Questions to ask: What does one hour of downtime actually cost? Is next month a real deadline or a wish? What is "fast" in numbers? What budget is the ceiling?
Recommended priority: Delivery date and availability first (they are usually tied to revenue), performance "good enough", cost as the variable.
Example decision: Managed PaaS with two instances and automated failover, one region, a cache for the hot path. Ship in 4 weeks. Multi-region and tuning come after launch when revenue justifies them.

Situation 2 — "Strong consistency everywhere and global low latency"

Conflict: Immediate consistency across continents means every write waits for cross-ocean coordination — which destroys latency.
Questions to ask: Which data truly needs immediate global consistency? Which can be regional? Who reads what, from where?
Recommended priority: Strong consistency only for money and stock at the point of sale; eventual consistency for catalog, profile and reporting.
Example decision: Single-region primary for transactions; regional read replicas and CDN for everything else. Users see fast catalog everywhere and correct payment always.

Situation 3 — "Maximum security and frictionless user experience"

Conflict: Every extra check (MFA, re-authentication, device verification) adds friction. Every removed check adds risk.
Questions to ask: What is being protected? What is the regulatory floor? Where does fraud actually happen in the flow?
Recommended priority: Security as a gate for high-risk actions (payment, address change, export); low friction for browsing and low-value actions.
Example decision: Step-up authentication only at checkout and account changes; risk-based checks in the background; browsing stays frictionless.

Situation 4 — "Latest technology and a team that has never used it"

Conflict: Adopting a new platform (say, Kubernetes or an event-streaming stack) vs a team with zero experience and a live product to keep running.
Questions to ask: What problem does the new technology solve that we actually have? Who supports it at 3 a.m.? Can we hire or train in time?
Recommended priority: Team capability over technology novelty. Reliability of the current product over the new stack's benefits.
Example decision: Stay on the known platform for the core. Pilot the new technology on one low-risk, non-critical workload with training budget. Adopt wider only after the pilot proves the team can run it.

Situation 5 — "Budget cut 30%, same features, same deadline"

Conflict: Scope, time and money — one of them has to move, and the business has fixed two.
Questions to ask: Which features drive revenue in the first 90 days? Which non-functional targets can be relaxed temporarily (99.99% → 99.9%)? What can be bought instead of built?
Recommended priority: Protect the revenue path and data safety. Cut redundancy that protects against rare events, defer nice-to-haves, buy commodity pieces.
Example decision: Single region instead of two (keep backups and tested restore), SaaS for notifications and search instead of custom, defer advanced reporting to phase 2. Document what was deferred and the trigger to restore it.
THE HABIT

When requirements conflict, do not silently pick one. Surface the conflict, propose a priority, and get the business to agree. Then the trade-off is a shared decision — not your private gamble.

PART 11 — DO NOT SAY THIS

Interview Traps: One-Line Answers That Sink Candidates

Each of these sentences sounds confident and is incomplete. Interviewers use them to find out whether you think in requirements or in slogans.

THE TRAP WHY IT IS INCOMPLETE HOW AN ARCHITECT REPHRASES IT
"Kafka is faster." Faster than what, for what? Kafka is a durable, high-throughput log. If you need 200 messages/day with simple routing, RabbitMQ or even a database table is simpler and "fast enough". "If we need very high throughput, replay of events, and multiple independent consumers, Kafka fits. For modest volume and task-style messaging, a simpler broker costs less to run. What is our volume and do we need replay?"
"Microservices are more scalable." A well-designed monolith scales horizontally too. Microservices scale parts independently — which only helps if parts have very different load. They also add network and operational cost. "Microservices let us scale one component independently. That matters if, say, search needs 50× the capacity of checkout. If load is uniform, a horizontally scaled monolith gives the same scale with less complexity."
"Cloud is better." Better for spiky load, fast start and no hardware. Worse for steady heavy load cost, some compliance rules, and sites with poor connectivity. "Cloud fits when we need elasticity and want to avoid capital spend and hardware ops. If load is flat and heavy, or data must stay on site, on-prem or hybrid can be cheaper and compliant. What does our traffic and regulation look like?"
"NoSQL scales better." It scales writes horizontally more easily — at the cost of joins, multi-row transactions and ad-hoc queries. Most systems never reach the write volume where this matters. "NoSQL wins for horizontal write scale and flexible shapes with known access patterns. For related transactional data with reporting needs, SQL is the better trade until write volume proves otherwise. What are our write rates and query patterns?"
"Redis is faster." Faster reads, yes — but it is a copy, not the truth. It adds a component, an invalidation problem and a failure mode. "A cache makes repeated reads fast and offloads the database. It pays off when the read/write ratio is high and staleness is acceptable. For data that changes constantly, it does not help. What is our read ratio and change rate?"
"Kubernetes is more reliable." Kubernetes provides self-healing and orchestration — if you operate it well. Run badly, it is a new source of outages. A managed PaaS can be more reliable for a small team. "Kubernetes gives self-healing and portability, and it needs a team that can run it. With a small ops team and a handful of services, a managed platform gives similar uptime with far less operational risk. How many services and who operates them?"
"GraphQL is better than REST." Better for clients that need flexible shapes in one round-trip. Harder to cache, easy to over-fetch on the server (N+1), extra learning curve. "GraphQL helps when many client shapes cause over- or under-fetching. For stable internal or partner APIs, REST is simpler and caches well. Do we have a measured fetching problem on the client?"
THE CONVERSION RULE

Any statement of the form "X is better" becomes an architect statement when you add: "…when [condition], because [requirement]; otherwise [alternative]." Practise this until it is automatic.

Suggested Architect Decisions

1. Application structure → Modular Monolith
Requirement: high availability, limited budget, small DevOps, 5× growth.
Options: Monolith / Modular Monolith / Microservices.
Comparison: Microservices best for independence and isolation but needs operational maturity we lack and triples cost. Plain monolith cheapest but couples code and slows teams. Modular monolith gives boundaries, single deployment, in-process speed.
Decision & reason: Modular monolith, horizontally scaled, with strict module contracts and schema ownership. 5× growth is a few more instances, not a rewrite.
Rejected: Microservices — solves an organisational problem we do not have and the DevOps team cannot carry it.
Risk: boundary erosion → architecture tests in CI.
Revisit: team > 50, or the AI module needs a different runtime (likely first extraction candidate).

2. Data store → SQL (relational) as the core, with room for a specialised store later
Requirement: related transactional data (orders, stock, payments), reporting, audit.
Comparison: SQL gives transactions, joins, ad-hoc reports, mature security and audit. NoSQL gives write scale and flexible shapes we do not need for the core.
Decision & reason: Managed SQL (PostgreSQL or SQL Server per licensing) with per-module schemas and a read replica.
Rejected: NoSQL as the primary — loses atomic multi-table updates and reporting.
Risk: single write primary ceiling → partition by tenant/date before considering sharding.
Revisit: when write volume approaches the primary's measured limit, or when AI features need a vector/feature store (add alongside, do not replace).

3. Cache vs direct DB → Cache the hot read paths
Requirement: high dashboard traffic, product/catalog reads, limited budget.
Comparison: Direct access is simplest but puts every dashboard hit on the database. Cache removes most repeated reads at low cost, adds one component and an invalidation duty.
Decision & reason: Redis for catalog, tenant configuration and dashboard aggregates with short TTLs; never for stock-at-checkout or payment state.
Rejected: no cache — replica alone still carries the full read volume and costs more at 5×.
Risk: stale dashboard numbers; cache outage storm → fallback throttling and clear "as of" timestamps in the UI.
Revisit: if hit rate falls below ~80% or if data change frequency makes caching pointless.

4. Sync vs async → Split by user need
Requirement: frequent orders, high availability, external providers.
Comparison: All-sync is simple but couples checkout to the slowest dependency. All-async hides payment results from the user.
Decision & reason: Order save + payment synchronous; notifications, warehouse sync, audit projection and report refresh asynchronous via a managed queue with retries and idempotent consumers.
Rejected: all synchronous — fails availability under provider outages; all asynchronous — unacceptable UX for payment.
Risk: duplicate messages → idempotency keys; queue backlog → depth alerts and consumer auto-scale.
Revisit: if a downstream system becomes a hard real-time requirement (e.g. warehouse demands instant stock).

5. Shared vs separate data stores → Shared database with strict tenant + schema ownership; separate store only for reporting
Requirement: strong security, 10,000 tenants, heavy reporting, limited budget.
Comparison: Separate stores per module/tenant give isolation but explode operational cost and break transactions. Shared with `TenantId` and schema ownership keeps cost low and transactions intact; reporting still competes with checkout unless separated.
Decision & reason: One transactional database with row-level tenant filtering enforced centrally, per-module schemas; a separate read replica / reporting store for dashboards and reports; dedicated database only for a small number of premium or regulated tenants.
Rejected: database per tenant for all — 10,000 databases is unmanageable for a small team.
Risk: a missed tenant filter leaks data → enforced at the data-access layer plus automated tests; noisy tenant → per-tenant quotas and monitoring.
Revisit: if compliance requires physical isolation broadly, or if reporting needs a true warehouse.

15 CHALLENGING QUESTIONS

  1. Why this technology stack and not something more modern?
  2. Why not microservices from day one, given 5× growth is expected?
  3. What happens if traffic becomes 10×, not 5×?
  4. What happens when the database primary fails?
  5. What happens when Redis fails during peak?
  6. What happens when the queue broker is down?
  7. How much does this cost per month, and at 5×?
  8. What if the team does not know PostgreSQL / the queue technology?
  9. What if management cuts the budget by 30% next quarter?
  10. What if a large customer demands a fully isolated environment?
  11. What if the business requirement changes to real-time stock across warehouses?
  12. How do you prevent one tenant from slowing down the others?
  13. How will you add AI features into this architecture later?
  14. How do you know your module boundaries are right?
  15. How will you measure whether this architecture was the correct decision in 12 months?

Model Answers

1. Why this stack? Because it matches the constraints: a small DevOps team, limited budget, transactional data, and a team that already knows .NET and SQL. "Modern" is not a requirement; reliability, cost and delivery speed are. Every piece is managed or well-understood, which is the cheapest reliability we can buy.

2. Why not microservices for 5× growth? 5× of ~150 RPS is ~750 RPS — comfortably within a horizontally scaled monolith and a tuned database. Microservices solve team-scale and divergent-load problems, not a 5× uniform increase. We keep strict module boundaries so extraction is cheap if that changes.

3. 10× traffic? App tier: add instances behind the load balancer. Reads: more replicas and higher cache hit rate. Writes: the first real ceiling. Plan: partition orders by tenant/date, move reporting fully off the primary, and if the write primary still saturates, shard by tenant group. All of these are staged, none is a rewrite.

4. Primary database fails? Managed failover promotes the replica within the platform's RTO (minutes). Application reconnects via retry policies. We test the failover quarterly and monitor replication lag so we know our RPO. The queue buffers post-checkout work during the switch.

5. Redis fails at peak? Requests fall through to the database with a request-throttle to prevent a thundering herd; dashboards show slightly slower and "as of" times. Nothing that affects money or stock lives only in the cache. We alert on cache availability and rebuild/warm it on recovery.

6. Queue broker down? Checkout still succeeds because payment is synchronous and the order is durably saved. Outgoing messages are written to an outbox table in the same transaction and published when the broker returns — nothing is lost, only delayed. Notifications and stock sync catch up automatically.

7. Cost? Roughly: app tier and workers ~$600, managed SQL + replica ~$700, cache ~$150, queue ~$100, monitoring ~$150 — about $1,700/month today. At 5×, mostly linear on app and replica tiers, ~$4–5K/month. Compared with a 12-service Kubernetes estate at ~$9K plus two extra salaries, this is the low-cost path.

8. Team doesn't know the technology? Then that is a first-class factor. If the team knows SQL Server, we use SQL Server and accept the licence cost. If they have never run a broker, we start with a managed queue service and a database-backed outbox, and train before adding complexity. A design the team cannot operate is not a good design.

9. Budget cut 30%? Protect the revenue path and data safety first. Options in order: drop the second replica and keep one; reduce cache size; defer the reporting store and route reports to the single replica off-peak; keep backups and tested restore untouched. Document what was removed and the trigger to restore it.

10. Customer demands full isolation? The hybrid tenant model already allows a dedicated database (and if needed a dedicated deployment) for specific tenants. It costs more, so it is priced into that customer's contract. We do not move all 10,000 tenants because of one.

11. Real-time stock across warehouses? That changes Inventory from "eventual is fine" to "strongly consistent, cross-site". I would keep stock reservation synchronous within the transactional database, introduce a reservation/hold model, and look at whether Inventory becomes the second extracted service with its own scaling. It is a requirement change, so it triggers an ADR review.

12. Noisy tenant? Per-tenant rate limits at the API layer, per-tenant query monitoring, and the ability to move a tenant to a dedicated pool. The observability from Day 12 — tenant as a dimension on every metric — is what makes this possible.

13. Adding AI later? The modular boundaries and event stream make this straightforward: AI features consume domain events (orders placed, stock moved) into their own store — likely a separate service in Python with a vector or feature store — without touching the transactional core. That is the most likely first extraction and the design leaves room for it.

14. Right boundaries? Each module owns its data and can be tested alone; changes rarely cross modules; the vocabulary differs (a "product" in catalog vs in warehouse); and the architecture tests fail the build on violations. If we see constant cross-module changes, the boundary is wrong and we merge or redraw it.

15. Measuring success in 12 months? Against the ADR: checkout p95 under target during month-end; zero report-caused incidents; deployment lead time down; cost per order flat or lower at higher volume; no cross-tenant data incidents. If these hold, the decision was right. If deployment lead time is still slow despite modularisation, that is the trigger to extract the next service.

THE PROBLEM — FIELD SERVICE PLATFORM

A company that maintains industrial equipment wants a platform for its 3,000 field technicians and 400 back-office staff across 12 countries.

Business requirements: Technicians receive jobs, log work and parts used, and capture photos and signatures on site. Back-office schedules jobs, approves invoices and runs monthly SLA reports for customers. Invoices must be correct to the cent and legally auditable for 7 years.

Conflicting requirements: Sales wants "real-time everything" on the dashboards; finance insists invoice data is never eventually consistent; operations wants launch in 5 months; security requires customer data to stay inside the EU for EU customers.

Growth: 3× technicians in 3 years; two acquisitions possible. Availability: 99.9% during working hours in each region; overnight maintenance windows acceptable. Security: customer-level data isolation, full audit trail, EU residency.

Your deliverables:

  1. The important requirements (ranked)
  2. The conflicting requirements and how you resolve each
  3. Three architecture options
  4. Comparison against the 10 factors
  5. Your selection
  6. Why
  7. Why you rejected the alternatives
  8. Risks
  9. Cost impact
  10. When you would revisit

Model Architect Answer

1. Ranked requirements. (1) Invoice correctness and 7-year auditability — legal gate. (2) EU data residency — legal gate. (3) Offline-capable mobile with reliable sync — the core job. (4) 5-month launch. (5) 99.9% working-hours availability. (6) 30-second scheduling freshness. (7) $8K/month ceiling. (8) 3× growth path. "Real-time dashboards" is a preference, not a requirement.

2. Conflicts resolved. Real-time vs cost/complexity: dashboards refresh every 30–60 s from a read replica; sales agrees "near real-time" once shown the cost of true streaming. Finance consistency vs mobile offline: the mobile app records work events offline; invoices are generated server-side, transactionally, only after sync and approval — so invoice data is never eventually consistent, even though field data is. 5 months vs residency: deploy in one EU region first (covers residency), add a second region for non-EU later. 3. Options. A — Lift-and-shift: move the on-prem SQL and a web API to cloud VMs, add a mobile app talking directly to the API. B — Modular monolith on managed PaaS: .NET modular monolith (Jobs, Scheduling, Invoicing, Media, Reporting), managed SQL Server with replica, blob storage + CDN for photos, managed queue for sync/outbox, mobile app with local store and sync protocol. C — Microservices: 6–8 services on Kubernetes, database per service, event bus, separate mobile BFF.

4. Comparison. Business: A fails offline sync robustness; B and C both meet it. Cost: A ~$3K but hidden ops; B ~$5–6K; C ~$12K+ and over ceiling. Performance: all adequate; photos must go to blob/CDN in any option. Scale: A weakest; B handles 3× by adding instances; C best but unneeded. Risk: A = availability and scale risk; C = delivery and operations risk; B = moderate, mitigable.

The invoicing module keeps strict transactions in SQL; the media module offloads 120 GB/day of photos to blob storage and CDN, keeping the database small and fast; the queue and outbox give reliable sync and let scheduling update within seconds; the replica serves reports and dashboards without touching invoicing.

7. Why not the others. A was rejected because it simply relocates the existing weaknesses: no failover story, no clean sync design, photos in the database, and it does not prepare for 3× or acquisitions. It is cheaper only until the first outage. C was rejected because it exceeds the run budget, cannot be operated by one DevOps engineer with no Kubernetes experience, would put the 5-month date at serious risk, and forces Sagas across Jobs/Invoicing where finance demands atomicity. It solves a scale-of-organisation problem this company does not have.

8. Risks. Sync conflicts when two people edit one job offline → last-writer-wins with conflict flags and a review queue. Migration of 6 years of SQL data → dry runs, checksums, parallel run for one billing cycle. Single EU region for launch → tested backup/restore and documented RTO; second region on the roadmap. Photo storage cost growth → lifecycle policy to cool storage after 90 days. Acquisitions bringing different data models → the module boundaries and tenant model give a place to plug them in.

9. Cost impact. Run cost ~$5–6K/month at launch (PaaS instances, managed SQL + replica, blob + CDN, queue, monitoring), rising to ~$7–8K at 3× — at the ceiling but not over it, and roughly half of Option C before salaries. Build cost is dominated by the sync protocol and migration, not by infrastructure. Failure cost is bounded by managed failover and tested restores.

10. Revisit when. A second acquisition doubles the team; a specific module (likely Media or Scheduling) needs scale or a runtime the monolith cannot provide; residency rules require active-active regions; or monthly run cost trends past $8K despite tuning. Scheduled review at 12 months against the metrics in the ADR: sync success rate, invoice defects (target zero), deployment lead time, p95 API latency, cost per active technician.

DAY 20 — CLOSE

10 Rules of Architecture Trade-offs

Short enough to remember in an interview. True enough to use for a career.

01

Every decision is a trade-off.

If you cannot name what you gave up, you have not made a decision — you have made a wish.

02

Start with the business problem, not the technology.

Restate the pain in money or customer impact before you draw a single box.

03

Always compare at least three options.

Including "do the minimum". You only see the price of a choice next to its alternatives.

04

Weight the factors before you score the options.

Decide what matters most for this business first — otherwise the shiny option wins by default.

05

Some factors are gates, not weights.

Security, compliance and team capability can disqualify an option no matter how well it scores elsewhere.

06

"Fast enough" beats "fastest".

Performance beyond the requirement is cost without value.

07

Price the whole life, including failure.

Build, run, maintain, fail, and the hidden cost of complexity. Cheap to build is not cheap to own.

08

Say something good about the option you rejected.

If you cannot, you never really considered it — and the interviewer knows.

09

Write it down with a review date.

An ADR with options, reasons, risks and a revisit trigger turns a decision into an asset.

10

Design for the business you have, with a path to the one you expect.

Three to five times current scale, and a clear next step — not a rewrite.

ONE-MINUTE INTERVIEW ANSWER — "HOW DO YOU MAKE AN ARCHITECTURE DECISION WHEN THERE ARE MULTIPLE POSSIBLE SOLUTIONS?"

"I start by making sure I understand the actual business problem and what success looks like in numbers — latency, availability, cost, deadline. Then I list the constraints that cut options before I compare anything: budget, team skills, existing systems, compliance. I put at least three options side by side, including the simplest one, and I evaluate each against the same factors — business fit, cost, performance, scale, availability, security, complexity, team, maintenance and risk — but I weight those factors for this specific business first, because for a bank security is a gate and for a startup delivery speed might be. I pick the option that wins the factors that matter most and doesn't fail badly on the rest. Then I write it up as a decision record: what we chose, what we rejected and what the rejected options did better, the risks and how we mitigate them, and the trigger that would make us revisit. That last part matters — the decision is right for today's constraints, and I want the team to know when today has changed."

THE ARCHITECT'S POWER STATEMENT

"There is no perfect architecture — only the set of trade-offs a business can afford, a team can run, and an architect can explain. My job is not to find the best technology. It is to choose the right compromises on purpose, write down what we gave up, and know the moment to choose again."

Twenty days in, you can now do the thing that separates architects from senior developers: hold two good options in your head, choose one for reasons you can defend, and respect the one you did not pick.
